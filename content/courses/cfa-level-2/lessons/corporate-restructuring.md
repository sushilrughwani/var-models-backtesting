title: "Corporate Restructuring"
---

## Learning goals
- Classify restructurings as investment actions, divestment actions or internal restructurings, and explain the motives behind each.
- Distinguish divestiture structures: sales, spin-offs, split-offs and equity carve-outs.
- Value a restructuring using comparable companies, comparable transactions, pre-transaction DCF and sum-of-the-parts methods.
- Calculate takeover premiums, the split of merger gains between acquirer and target, and EPS accretion or dilution.

## Explanation

### Why companies restructure
Restructuring changes a company's scope, structure or capital base. Motives include achieving **synergies** (cost savings or revenue gains), gaining scale, entering new markets or capabilities, sharpening focus, unlocking hidden value, responding to activist investors and fixing financial distress. Less defensible motives, such as managerial empire-building or diversification that shareholders could achieve themselves, often destroy value.

### Types of restructuring
- **Investment actions**: minority equity investments, joint ventures and acquisitions. These expand the company's scope.
- **Divestment actions** shrink scope:
  - **Sale** of a business unit for cash or securities.
  - **Spin-off**: existing shareholders receive shares of the new company pro rata; no cash is raised.
  - **Split-off**: shareholders may exchange parent shares for subsidiary shares; parent share count falls.
  - **Equity carve-out**: the parent sells a minority stake in the subsidiary to the public through an IPO, raising cash and keeping control.
- **Restructuring actions** keep scope but change operations or financing: cost cutting, leveraged recapitalisations, large dividends or buybacks, sale-leasebacks and reorganisation under bankruptcy protection.

### Evaluating a transaction
An analyst asks: is the deal material, what does it do to the combined firm, and does the price create or destroy value? Valuation tools include:

- **Comparable company analysis**: apply trading multiples of peers (EV/EBITDA, P/E) to the target, then add a takeover premium.
- **Comparable transaction analysis**: apply multiples paid in recent acquisitions of similar companies; the premium is already embedded.
- **Pre-transaction DCF**: value the target's standalone cash flows, then add the value of synergies separately.
- **Sum-of-the-parts**: value each segment separately and compare with the market value. A **conglomerate discount** suggests a spin-off could unlock value.

The takeover premium is measured against the **unaffected** price, before rumours lift the stock:

$$
\text{Premium} = \frac{P_{\text{offer}} - P_{\text{unaffected}}}{P_{\text{unaffected}}}
$$

### Who captures the gains?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Gain to target} &= \text{Premium paid} = P_{\text{deal}} - V_T \\
\text{Gain to acquirer} &= S - (P_{\text{deal}} - V_T)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $V_T$ is the target's standalone market value, $P_{\text{deal}}$ the total price paid and $S$ the present value of synergies. If the premium exceeds synergies, the acquirer's shareholders lose. In a stock deal, target shareholders share in the risk that synergies disappoint; in a cash deal, the acquirer bears it all.

### EPS accretion and dilution
In an all-stock deal, combined EPS rises (before synergies) when the acquirer issues shares at a higher P/E than the P/E it pays for the target's earnings. Accretion is not proof of value creation; a low-P/E target may be low-growth or high-risk.

## Worked example
Arden Corp (net income USD 200 million, 100 million shares, price USD 40, P/E 20) offers USD 18 per share in Arden stock for Birch Ltd (net income USD 30 million, 20 million shares, unaffected price USD 15). Synergies have a present value of USD 80 million.

Step 1: Premium = (18 − 15) / 15 = **20%**. Total premium = 3 × 20 million = USD 60 million.

Step 2: Shares issued = (18 × 20 million) / 40 = 9 million.

Step 3: Combined EPS before synergies = (200 + 30) / (100 + 9) = 230 / 109 = **USD 2.11**, versus Arden's standalone 2.00. The deal is accretive because Arden pays 18 / 1.50 = 12 times Birch's earnings while its own shares trade at 20 times.

Step 4: Gain to Birch shareholders = USD 60 million (the premium).

Step 5: Gain to Arden shareholders = 80 − 60 = **USD 20 million**. If synergies turn out to be only 50 million, Arden loses 10 million of value even though EPS is still accretive.

## Common exam traps
- Measuring the premium against a price already inflated by deal rumours.
- Confusing a spin-off (pro rata, no cash) with a carve-out (IPO, cash raised) or a split-off (exchange offer).
- Treating EPS accretion as evidence of value creation.
- Forgetting that comparable transaction multiples already include a control premium, so adding another double counts.
- Giving the acquirer the synergies without subtracting the premium paid.

## Key takeaways
- Restructurings are investment, divestment or internal restructuring actions, each with distinct motives.
- Spin-offs, split-offs and carve-outs differ in who receives shares and whether cash is raised.
- Use comparable companies, comparable transactions, DCF and sum-of-the-parts together to triangulate value.
- Targets capture the premium; acquirers gain only synergies in excess of the premium.
- Stock deals share synergy risk with target shareholders; cash deals leave it with the acquirer.
