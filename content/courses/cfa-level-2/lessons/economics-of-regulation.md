## Learning goals
- Explain the economic rationale for regulation, including informational frictions and externalities.
- Classify regulators and regulatory tools, and describe the role of self-regulatory organisations.
- Evaluate a proposed regulation using cost-benefit analysis, including indirect and unintended costs.
- Describe regulatory capture, regulatory competition and regulatory arbitrage.
- Assess how regulation affects an industry's and a company's prospects and valuation.

## Explanation

### Why regulate at all?
In an ideal market, voluntary exchange allocates resources efficiently and no regulator is needed. Regulation is justified when markets **fail**. Two failures dominate:

- **Informational frictions**: one side knows more than the other (adverse selection and moral hazard). Investors cannot easily judge a fund manager's skill or a bank's asset quality, so disclosure rules, licensing and capital requirements aim to protect them and keep markets trusted.
- **Externalities**: costs or benefits fall on people outside the transaction. Pollution is the classic negative externality; a bank failure that spreads panic through the financial system is another. Without intervention, activities with negative externalities are overproduced.

Regulators also pursue goals such as **financial stability**, **market integrity**, **consumer protection** and **competition** (preventing monopoly abuse and anticompetitive mergers).

### Who regulates
Regulation flows from three sources: **legislative bodies** pass statutes; **administrative or regulatory agencies** write and enforce detailed rules; and **courts** interpret and enforce through judicial decisions. Regulators may be government agencies or independent bodies with delegated authority.

**Self-regulatory organisations (SROs)**, such as exchanges or industry associations, write and enforce rules for their members. They benefit from industry expertise and lower cost to taxpayers, but face a conflict of interest because they regulate the firms that fund them. Outside bodies, such as accounting standard setters, have no enforcement power of their own but become influential when regulators adopt their standards.

### Regulatory tools
- **Price mechanisms**: taxes on harmful activities (a carbon tax) and subsidies for beneficial ones.
- **Restricting or requiring activities**: bans, licensing, capital and liquidity requirements, mandatory disclosure.
- **Government provision**: providing a service directly, such as deposit insurance.
- **Enforcement**: fines, sanctions and, ultimately, criminal prosecution. Rules without credible enforcement change little.

Tools can be combined. A cap-and-trade system sets a quantity limit but lets a market price emerge for the right to emit.

### Cost-benefit analysis
A good regulator compares the present value of benefits with the present value of costs:

$$
NPV_{reg} = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{B_t - K_t}{(1+r)^t}
$$

where $C_0$ is the upfront implementation cost, $B_t$ the annual benefits, $K_t$ annual compliance costs and $r$ the discount rate. Costs are easier to measure than benefits, so analysis tends to be biased toward overstating costs or ignoring diffuse benefits. Analysts should also count **indirect costs**: reduced innovation, reduced competition and **unintended consequences**, such as activity migrating to unregulated "shadow" sectors. Sunset clauses force a later re-evaluation.

### Capture, competition and arbitrage
- **Regulatory capture**: the regulator ends up serving the industry it oversees, often through revolving doors or information dependence.
- **Regulatory competition**: jurisdictions compete to attract business with lighter rules, which can trigger a "race to the bottom."
- **Regulatory arbitrage**: firms exploit differences between jurisdictions or between the substance and the letter of a rule, for example by booking activity in the least regulated entity.

### Regulation and valuation
For analysts, regulation is a source of both risk and opportunity. Rules can raise barriers to entry (protecting incumbents' margins), cap prices (utilities), raise compliance costs, or change capital requirements (banks' return on equity). When forecasting, consider the likely direction of future regulation and its effect on revenue, costs, required capital and the discount rate.

## Worked example
A securities regulator proposes a disclosure rule. Implementation costs firms USD 40 million upfront. Annual compliance costs are USD 5 million, and estimated annual benefits from better-informed pricing and fewer investor losses are USD 12 million, both for 10 years. The discount rate is 6%.

Step 1: Annual net benefit = 12 − 5 = USD 7 million.

Step 2: Annuity factor at 6% for 10 years = (1 − 1.06⁻¹⁰) / 0.06 = 7.3601.

Step 3: PV of net benefits = 7 × 7.3601 = USD 51.52 million.

Step 4: NPV = −40 + 51.52 = **USD 11.52 million**. The rule passes the test on direct costs.

Step 5: Qualitative overlay. If the rule would push smaller issuers to list abroad or stay private (an unintended consequence), the indirect cost could erase the 11.52 million surplus. The analyst should flag this sensitivity rather than treat the NPV as final.

## Common exam traps
- Treating SROs as conflict-free: they regulate their own funders.
- Forgetting that standard setters lack enforcement power unless a regulator adopts their standards.
- Counting gross benefits without subtracting ongoing compliance costs.
- Ignoring indirect and unintended costs when judging a regulation.
- Confusing regulatory arbitrage (firms exploiting gaps) with regulatory competition (jurisdictions competing).

## Key takeaways
- Regulation is justified by market failures: informational frictions and externalities.
- Legislatures, agencies and courts all create regulation; SROs add expertise but carry conflicts.
- Tools include price mechanisms, restrictions and requirements, government provision and enforcement.
- Cost-benefit analysis should include indirect costs and unintended consequences.
- Regulation shapes margins, capital needs and risk, so it belongs in every valuation.
