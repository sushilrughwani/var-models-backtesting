## Learning goals
- Calculate the contributions of capital, labour and total factor productivity using growth accounting.
- Distinguish capital deepening from technological progress and explain why only the latter sustains per-capita growth in the neoclassical model.
- Compare neoclassical and endogenous growth theories and their predictions for steady-state growth.
- Evaluate the absolute, conditional and club convergence hypotheses and the role of trade and capital flows.
- Explain why long-run GDP growth anchors long-run equity market returns.

## Explanation

### Why growth matters to investors
Over long horizons, corporate earnings cannot grow faster than the economy indefinitely; otherwise profits would eventually exceed GDP. So the **potential GDP growth rate** is the anchor for long-run earnings growth and therefore for equity returns. Potential growth also shapes interest rates and credit quality: faster trend growth supports debt service. An analyst who can forecast potential growth has a disciplined input for terminal growth rates in valuation models.

### Preconditions for growth
Capital and labour alone are not enough. Economies that grow persistently tend to have adequate saving and investment, sound financial markets that allocate capital, political stability and the rule of law, protected property rights, education and health investment, low tax and regulatory burdens, openness to trade and stable institutions. When these are weak, more capital simply leaks away.

### The production function and growth accounting
A common model uses a Cobb-Douglas production function $Y = A K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}$. Taking growth rates gives the growth accounting equation:

$$
\frac{\Delta Y}{Y} = \frac{\Delta A}{A} + \alpha \frac{\Delta K}{K} + (1-\alpha)\frac{\Delta L}{L}
$$

where $Y$ is real output, $A$ is total factor productivity (TFP), $K$ is the capital stock, $L$ is labour input, and $\alpha$ is capital's share of income (roughly 0.3 to 0.4 in most economies). TFP is measured as the **Solow residual**: whatever output growth is left after subtracting the weighted contributions of capital and labour.

Dividing through by labour gives labour productivity growth:

$$
\frac{\Delta (Y/L)}{Y/L} = \frac{\Delta A}{A} + \alpha \left(\frac{\Delta K}{K} - \frac{\Delta L}{L}\right)
$$

The second term is **capital deepening**: more capital per worker. Potential GDP growth is then labour force growth plus labour productivity growth.

### Diminishing returns and the neoclassical model
Because $\alpha < 1$, each extra unit of capital per worker adds less output than the last. Capital deepening therefore cannot raise per-capita growth forever. In the neoclassical (Solow) model, the economy settles into a **steady state** where capital per worker grows only because of technology. Steady-state growth rates are:

$$
g^{*}_{Y/L} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha}, \qquad g^{*}_{Y} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + n
$$

where $\theta$ is the growth rate of TFP and $n$ is labour force growth. A higher saving rate raises the **level** of output per worker but not its long-run **growth rate**.

### Endogenous growth
Endogenous growth models argue that investment in knowledge, R&D and human capital creates spillovers. A firm's research helps other firms, so returns to capital at the level of the whole economy need not diminish. In these models a higher saving rate can permanently raise the growth rate, and policies that encourage innovation and education matter a great deal. Because private firms ignore spillovers, the market may underinvest in R&D, justifying subsidies.

### Convergence
- **Absolute convergence**: all countries converge to the same per-capita income level. Weak empirical support.
- **Conditional convergence**: countries with the same saving rates, population growth and technology converge to the same steady state, so poorer ones grow faster only after controlling for these characteristics.
- **Club convergence**: countries that meet certain institutional thresholds join a "club" of converging economies; those that do not can fall further behind.

Open economies converge faster: foreign investment adds capital, and trade transfers technology and gives access to larger markets.

## Worked example
An economy has capital share α = 0.35. Over a year the capital stock grows 4.0%, labour input grows 1.0%, and TFP grows 1.2%.

Step 1: Capital contribution = 0.35 × 4.0% = 1.40%.

Step 2: Labour contribution = 0.65 × 1.0% = 0.65%.

Step 3: Output growth = 1.20% + 1.40% + 0.65% = **3.25%**.

Step 4: Labour productivity growth = 1.20% + 0.35 × (4.0% − 1.0%) = 1.20% + 1.05% = **2.25%**. Check: 2.25% + 1.0% labour growth = 3.25% output growth.

Step 5: In the neoclassical steady state with θ = 1.2%, labour productivity grows at 1.2% / 0.65 = **1.85%** and output at 1.85% + 1.0% = **2.85%**. Today's 3.25% therefore includes transitional capital deepening that will fade.

## Common exam traps
- Swapping the weights: capital gets α and labour gets 1 − α.
- Forgetting to subtract labour growth in the capital deepening term when computing productivity growth.
- Claiming a higher saving rate raises long-run growth in the neoclassical model; it raises only the level of income.
- Confusing conditional convergence (same characteristics) with absolute convergence (all countries).
- Forgetting to divide θ by (1 − α) when computing steady-state growth.

## Key takeaways
- Potential GDP growth anchors long-run earnings and equity returns.
- Growth accounting splits output growth into TFP, capital and labour contributions; TFP is the residual.
- Capital deepening faces diminishing returns; sustained per-capita growth needs technological progress.
- Endogenous growth allows permanently higher growth through knowledge spillovers.
- Convergence is conditional on institutions and policies; openness speeds it up.
