title: "Investments in Private Capital: Equity and Debt"
---

## Learning goals
- Describe the main private equity strategies: leveraged buyouts, venture capital and growth equity.
- Explain the stages of venture capital financing and compute pre- and post-money valuations.
- Compare the exit routes available to private equity funds.
- Describe the main forms of private debt and where they sit in the capital structure.
- Calculate the equity multiple and IRR of a simple leveraged buyout and attribute the value created.

## Explanation

### Private capital: the big picture
Private capital is financing provided to companies outside public markets. It comes in two flavours: **private equity**, which buys ownership stakes, and **private debt**, which lends. Both are usually accessed through the limited partnership funds from earlier in this module, with long lives (often ten years or more) and capital called over time.

### Private equity strategies
**Leveraged buyouts (LBOs)** acquire mature, cash-generative companies using a large amount of borrowed money. The debt is repaid from the target's own cash flows, so the equity owners capture the value created. When the existing management team leads the purchase it is a **management buyout (MBO)**; when an outside team is brought in it is a **management buy-in (MBI)**. Buyout firms add value by improving operations, cutting costs, reshaping strategy and optimising the capital structure.

**Venture capital (VC)** funds young companies with high growth potential but little or no revenue. Most investments fail; a few large winners drive the fund's return. Financing happens in stages:
- **Formative stage**: angel investing (idea stage), seed (product development) and early stage (initial commercial production).
- **Later stage**: expansion capital for a company that already has sales.
- **Mezzanine (pre-IPO) stage**: financing to prepare for a public listing.

**Growth equity** takes minority stakes in established, growing companies that need capital to expand but do not want to give up control. It uses little or no leverage.

### Venture valuation
A VC investor who pays an amount $I$ for a fraction $f$ of the company implies:

$$
\text{Post-money} = \frac{I}{f}, \qquad \text{Pre-money} = \text{Post-money} - I
$$

The pre-money value is what the company was worth before the new cash arrived.

### Exit routes
- **Trade sale**: sale to a strategic buyer, often at a premium for synergies; quick and confidential, but management may resist.
- **Initial public offering (IPO)**: can deliver the highest price, but is costly, slow and depends on market conditions.
- **Secondary sale**: sale to another private equity firm.
- **Recapitalisation**: the company borrows to pay the owners a dividend; not a full exit, but returns cash early.
- **Write-off or liquidation**: the investment failed.

### Private debt
- **Direct lending**: loans made by funds straight to (often mid-sized) companies, usually senior and secured, frequently floating rate.
- **Mezzanine debt**: subordinated debt, often with warrants or conversion features, ranking below senior loans and above equity; higher yield for higher risk.
- **Venture debt**: loans to start-ups that lack the cash flow for conventional borrowing, often with equity kickers.
- **Distressed debt**: buying the debt of struggling companies at a deep discount, sometimes to gain control in a restructuring.
- **Unitranche**: a single loan blending senior and subordinated debt at one blended rate.

Private debt investors earn a premium over public bonds for illiquidity and complexity, and they bear default risk and the difficulty of exiting before maturity.

### Measuring an LBO
Equity value equals enterprise value minus net debt. The return comes from three sources: EBITDA growth, a change in the valuation multiple, and debt paydown.

$$
\text{MOIC} = \frac{E_T}{E_0}, \qquad \text{IRR} = \text{MOIC}^{1/T} - 1
$$

where $E_0$ and $E_T$ are equity values at entry and exit and $T$ is the holding period in years (the IRR shortcut assumes no interim cash flows).

## Worked example
A buyout fund acquires a company with EBITDA of USD 50 million at an EV/EBITDA multiple of 10. It finances the deal with USD 300 million of debt. Five years later EBITDA is 70 million, the exit multiple is still 10, and debt has been paid down to 180 million.

1. Entry enterprise value: $50 \times 10 = 500$ million. Entry equity: $500 - 300 = 200$ million.
2. Exit enterprise value: $70 \times 10 = 700$ million. Exit equity: $700 - 180 = 520$ million.
3. MOIC: $520 / 200 = 2.6\times$.
4. IRR: $2.6^{1/5} - 1 =$ **21.06%** per year.
5. Value attribution (total equity gain of 320 million):
   - EBITDA growth: $(70 - 50) \times 10 = 200$ million.
   - Multiple expansion: $(10 - 10) \times 70 = 0$.
   - Debt paydown: $300 - 180 = 120$ million.

The 200 + 0 + 120 = 320 million matches the equity gain, showing that operating improvement and deleveraging both mattered.

**Venture check**: a VC fund pays USD 5 million for 20% of a start-up. Post-money value $= 5 / 0.20 = 25$ million; pre-money value $= 25 - 5 = 20$ million.

## Common exam traps
- Confusing the mezzanine *stage* of VC (pre-IPO financing) with mezzanine *debt* (subordinated debt with equity features).
- Calculating ownership from the pre-money value instead of the post-money value.
- Forgetting to subtract debt when moving from enterprise value to equity value.
- Treating a recapitalisation as a full exit; the sponsor still owns the company.
- Assuming growth equity uses heavy leverage like an LBO; it typically uses little or none.

## Key takeaways
- Private equity includes LBOs (mature firms, heavy debt), VC (young firms, staged financing) and growth equity (minority stakes, low leverage).
- Post-money value equals investment divided by ownership share; pre-money equals post-money minus the investment.
- Exits include trade sales, IPOs, secondary sales, recapitalisations and write-offs.
- Private debt ranges from senior direct loans to mezzanine, venture, distressed and unitranche debt.
- LBO equity returns come from EBITDA growth, multiple expansion and debt paydown.
