## Learning goals
- Describe the qualitative and quantitative factors used to assess sovereign creditworthiness.
- Distinguish general obligation bonds from revenue bonds issued by non-sovereign governments.
- Apply the four Cs of credit analysis (capacity, capital, collateral and covenants), plus character, to a corporate issuer.
- Calculate and interpret leverage and coverage ratios, including Debt/EBITDA, EBITDA/interest, FFO/debt and debt/capital.
- Explain how a debt-financed transaction changes an issuer's credit profile.

## Explanation

### From "what is credit risk" to "how risky is this borrower"
The previous lesson defined default probability, loss severity and ratings. This lesson is about where those judgements come from: the analysis an agency or credit analyst performs on a government or a company.

### Sovereign issuers: ability and willingness
A national government can tax its citizens and, if it borrows in its own currency, print money. Default on local-currency debt is therefore rare, although inflation can impose real losses. Foreign-currency debt is different, because the sovereign must earn or borrow the hard currency it needs. For this reason, agencies often rate a sovereign's **local-currency debt above its foreign-currency debt**.

Analysts examine five broad areas:
- **Institutional:** political stability, rule of law, corruption and a record of honouring debts. This factor measures willingness to pay.
- **Economic:** GDP per capita, real growth and diversification. A narrow, commodity-dependent economy is more fragile.
- **External:** reserve-currency status, current account balance, foreign exchange reserves and external debt relative to exports.
- **Fiscal:** government debt-to-GDP, interest expense relative to revenue and the deficit trend.
- **Monetary:** central bank independence, inflation credibility and exchange-rate flexibility.

### Non-sovereign governments
Regional and local governments cannot print money, so they behave more like companies.
- **General obligation (GO) bonds** are backed by the issuer's taxing power. Analysts look at tax-base strength, economic diversity, budget balance, pension liabilities and debt per capita.
- **Revenue bonds** are repaid only from a specific project, such as a toll road or water utility. They are analysed like a business, using the **debt service coverage ratio** (net project revenue ÷ debt service). Revenue bonds usually carry more risk than GO bonds from the same issuer.

### Corporate issuers: the four Cs (and a fifth)
A common framework groups corporate analysis into four Cs, and many analysts add a fifth:
- **Capacity:** can cash flow cover debt service? Analysts study industry structure, competitive position, cyclicality and the firm's operating track record.
- **Capital:** what other resources support the firm? Retained earnings, equity cushion and access to funding all count.
- **Collateral:** the value of assets pledged, or available to creditors, if things go wrong. Hard assets usually support recovery better than intangibles.
- **Covenants:** the legal protections in the bond agreement, such as limits on extra debt, restrictions on payouts and change-of-control clauses.
- **Character:** management quality, governance, accounting conservatism and how the firm has treated creditors in the past. This is the fifth C, and it is the hardest to measure.

### The ratios that matter
Credit analysts focus on cash flow and debt rather than earnings per share.

$$
\frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{EBITDA}}, \qquad \frac{\text{FFO}}{\text{Debt}}, \qquad \frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Debt} + \text{Equity}}, \qquad \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Interest}}, \qquad \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest}}
$$

where EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, and FFO (funds from operations) is net income plus depreciation, amortisation, deferred taxes and other non-cash items. Higher Debt/EBITDA and debt/capital mean **more** leverage, which is worse for credit. Higher FFO/debt and coverage ratios mean **stronger** credit. Analysts also adjust reported debt for items such as leases and underfunded pensions, and they compare ratios with industry peers, because a utility can safely carry leverage that would sink a technology firm.

## Worked example
Corvane Industries reports: EBITDA 400, depreciation and amortisation 100 (so EBIT 300), interest expense 60, FFO 260, total debt 1,200, cash 200 and shareholders' equity 1,800 (in millions).

**Step 1 - leverage.**
- Debt/EBITDA = 1,200 / 400 = 3.00×.
- Net debt/EBITDA = (1,200 − 200) / 400 = 2.50×.
- FFO/debt = 260 / 1,200 = 21.7%.
- Debt/capital = 1,200 / (1,200 + 1,800) = 40.0%.

**Step 2 - coverage.**
- EBITDA/interest = 400 / 60 = 6.67×.
- EBIT/interest = 300 / 60 = 5.00×.

**Step 3 - a debt-financed buyback.** Corvane borrows 300 at 5% to repurchase shares. Debt rises to 1,500, interest rises by 15 to 75, and equity falls to 1,500.
- Debt/EBITDA = 1,500 / 400 = 3.75×.
- EBITDA/interest = 400 / 75 = 5.33×.
- Debt/capital = 1,500 / 3,000 = 50.0%.

**Conclusion:** every metric weakens. **Leverage rises from 3.00× to 3.75× and coverage falls from 6.67× to 5.33×**, which could push a borderline investment-grade issuer toward a downgrade, even though shareholders may welcome the buyback.

## Common exam traps
- Reading the direction wrong: a higher Debt/EBITDA is worse, while a higher FFO/debt is better.
- Assuming a sovereign's foreign-currency rating is the same as, or higher than, its local-currency rating. It is usually equal or lower.
- Treating revenue bonds as backed by general taxing power. They rely only on project revenues.
- Ignoring off-balance-sheet obligations. Leases and pension deficits should be added to debt.
- Comparing ratios across industries without context. Peer comparison is essential.

## Key takeaways
- Sovereign analysis weighs willingness to pay (institutions) and ability to pay (economic, external, fiscal and monetary factors).
- Local-currency sovereign debt is generally safer than foreign-currency debt.
- GO bonds rely on taxing power; revenue bonds rely on a single project and are analysed with debt service coverage.
- Corporate credit analysis covers capacity, capital, collateral, covenants and character.
- Key ratios are Debt/EBITDA, FFO/debt, debt/capital and EBITDA or EBIT interest coverage.
- Debt-financed shareholder payouts usually weaken credit quality.
