## Learning goals
- Identify the main fiscal tools on the spending and revenue sides and the attributes of a good tax policy.
- Calculate the fiscal multiplier with proportional taxes and apply it to spending changes, tax changes and balanced-budget changes.
- Distinguish automatic stabilisers from discretionary policy and structural from cyclical deficits.
- Explain the limits of fiscal policy: lags, crowding out, Ricardian equivalence and debt concerns.

## Explanation

### What fiscal policy tries to do
Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and taxation to influence the economy. It has three broad aims: stabilise aggregate demand over the business cycle (the subject of the previous lesson), redistribute income and wealth, and allocate resources to public goods such as defence, roads and education.

### The tools
**Spending tools**
- *Transfer payments* such as pensions and unemployment benefits, which redistribute income without buying goods.
- *Current spending* on goods and services consumed now, such as public-sector wages.
- *Capital spending* on infrastructure, which can raise the economy's productive capacity.

**Revenue tools**
- *Direct taxes* on income, wealth and corporate profits.
- *Indirect taxes* on spending, such as sales tax, VAT and excise duties. These can be changed quickly and raise revenue efficiently.

A good tax system aims for simplicity, efficiency (minimal distortion to behaviour), fairness and enough revenue.

### The fiscal multiplier
When the government spends an extra unit of currency, that spending becomes someone's income. They pay part in tax and spend part of what remains, which becomes someone else's income, and so on. With a marginal propensity to consume $c$ and a proportional tax rate $t$:

$$
\text{Multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - c(1 - t)}
$$

where $c$ is the share of each extra unit of disposable income that is spent and $t$ is the share of each extra unit of income taken in tax. Higher $c$ makes the multiplier larger; higher $t$ makes it smaller because more leaks out at every round.

A tax cut is weaker than an equal spending increase. A cut of $\Delta T$ raises disposable income by $\Delta T$, but only $c \times \Delta T$ is spent in the first round, so the GDP effect is $c \times \Delta T \times$ multiplier. This is also why a **balanced-budget** increase in spending (spending and lump-sum taxes rise by the same amount) still has a positive effect on output.

### Automatic stabilisers versus discretionary policy
**Automatic stabilisers** act without new legislation. In a recession, income tax receipts fall and benefit payments rise, cushioning disposable income. In a boom the reverse happens. **Discretionary policy** is a deliberate decision to change spending or tax rates.

Because the budget balance moves with the cycle, analysts separate the **cyclical** deficit (caused by the economy being below potential) from the **structural** deficit (the deficit that would remain at full employment). The change in the structural balance is the better measure of whether policy is being made looser or tighter.

### Limits of fiscal policy
- **Lags.** The *recognition lag* is the time to realise there is a problem; the *action lag* is the time to agree and legislate a response; the *impact lag* is the time for the measures to affect the economy. By the time a stimulus works, the economy may already have recovered.
- **Crowding out.** Government borrowing can push up interest rates and displace private investment.
- **Ricardian equivalence.** If households expect a debt-financed tax cut to be repaid with higher future taxes, they save the windfall and spending does not change.
- **Supply constraints.** If the economy is near full employment, extra demand mainly raises prices.
- **Debt concerns.** High debt can raise borrowing costs and future tax burdens. Counterarguments: debt owed to domestic citizens is partly a transfer within the economy, and borrowing to fund productive capital spending can raise future output.

## Worked example
A government raises spending by 10 billion. The marginal propensity to consume is 0.80 and the tax rate is 25%.

**Step 1 - Multiplier.**

$$
\frac{1}{1 - 0.80(1 - 0.25)} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.60} = \frac{1}{0.40} = 2.5
$$

**Step 2 - Effect of the spending increase.** $\Delta Y = 2.5 \times 10 = 25$ billion.

**Step 3 - Budget impact.** Higher GDP brings in extra tax of $0.25 \times 25 = 6.25$ billion, so the deficit rises by only $10 - 6.25 = 3.75$ billion, not the full 10 billion.

**Step 4 - Compare a lump-sum tax cut of 10 billion.** First-round spending is $0.80 \times 10 = 8$ billion, so $\Delta Y = 8 \times 2.5 = 20$ billion.

**Step 5 - Balanced-budget change.** Spending rises by 10 billion and lump-sum taxes rise by 10 billion: $25 - 20 = 5$ billion.

The spending increase lifts GDP by **25 billion**, the tax cut by 20 billion, and the balanced-budget package by 5 billion.

## Common exam traps
- Using $1/(1-c)$ when a tax rate is given. With proportional taxes, the leakage per round is $1 - c(1-t)$.
- Applying the full multiplier to a tax cut. Only the spent portion, $c \times \Delta T$, starts the chain.
- Reading a larger deficit in a recession as a deliberate stimulus. Much of it may be cyclical; look at the structural balance.
- Confusing the action lag (politics and legislation) with the impact lag (time for the economy to respond).
- Assuming a balanced budget change has zero effect on GDP. It is positive.

## Key takeaways
- Fiscal tools are spending (transfers, current, capital) and taxes (direct, indirect).
- Multiplier = 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]; it grows with the propensity to consume and shrinks with the tax rate.
- A tax cut has a smaller effect than an equal spending increase; a balanced-budget expansion is still expansionary.
- Automatic stabilisers work without legislation; the structural balance shows the true fiscal stance.
- Lags, crowding out, Ricardian equivalence and supply constraints limit what fiscal policy can achieve.
