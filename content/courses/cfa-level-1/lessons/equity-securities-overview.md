title: "Overview of Equity Securities"
---

## Learning goals
- Describe the characteristics of common and preference shares, including voting rights and the features of different preference share types.
- Distinguish statutory and cumulative voting and calculate the votes available to a shareholder.
- Compare public and private equity and describe ways of investing in foreign equities.
- Distinguish book value from market value and calculate return on equity using average equity.
- Estimate an implied cost of equity and explain why it is the investor's required return.

## Explanation

### Common shares
Common shareholders own the residual claim: they are paid after creditors and preference shareholders, but they capture all upside. They usually vote on board elections and major corporate actions. Two voting systems matter:

- **Statutory voting:** one vote per share for each board seat, seat by seat. A majority holder wins every seat.
- **Cumulative voting:** total votes = shares × seats, and the holder can concentrate them on one candidate. This gives minority shareholders a better chance of electing at least one director.

Some firms issue **multiple share classes** with different voting power, letting founders keep control with a minority economic stake. Common shares may also be **callable** (the issuer can buy them back at a set price) or **putable** (the holder can sell them back).

### Preference shares
Preference (preferred) shares rank above common shares for dividends and in liquidation, usually pay a fixed dividend and typically carry no vote. Key variants:
- **Cumulative:** missed dividends accrue and must be paid before any common dividend. **Non-cumulative** shares lose missed dividends.
- **Participating:** receive the fixed dividend plus a share of extra profits above a threshold.
- **Convertible:** can be exchanged for common shares, giving upside with a fixed-income floor.

Preference shares behave like a hybrid of debt and equity. Cumulative preference shares are less risky to the holder than non-cumulative ones.

### Public versus private equity
Public shares trade on exchanges with price transparency, liquidity and heavy disclosure. Private equity (venture capital, buyouts, private investment in public equity) is illiquid, has limited disclosure and long horizons, but allows owners to take a long-term view without quarterly market pressure. Investors reach foreign equities by buying shares directly, through **depository receipts** (certificates traded locally that represent foreign shares, such as global or American depository receipts) or through funds.

### Risk and return of equity
Equity returns come from price change, dividends and, for foreign holdings, currency movements. Equity is riskier than debt because its claim is residual and its cash flows are uncertain.

### Book value, market value and ROE
**Book value of equity** is assets minus liabilities on the balance sheet: the accounting record of what shareholders have contributed plus retained profits. **Market value** is share price times shares outstanding and reflects investors' expectations about future cash flows. The ratio of the two (price-to-book) tells you how much value the market sees beyond the accounts.

$$
\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income}_t}{\text{Average book equity}} = \frac{NI_t}{(BV_{t-1} + BV_t)/2}
$$

ROE measures how efficiently management uses shareholders' accounting capital. It can rise for poor reasons too, such as more debt or a shrinking equity base after losses or buybacks.

### Cost of equity
The **cost of equity** is the minimum return investors require to hold the shares; it is a cost to the company and a required return to the investor. It cannot be observed directly. One simple estimate inverts a constant-growth dividend model:

$$
r_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g
$$

where $D_1$ is next year's dividend, $P_0$ the current price and $g$ the long-run dividend growth rate. A firm creates value for shareholders only if it earns a return on equity above this cost.

## Worked example
A company reports net income of USD 48 million. Book equity was USD 380 million at the start of the year and USD 420 million at the end. It has 20 million shares trading at USD 60. It just paid a dividend of USD 1.20 per share and dividends are expected to grow 5% a year.

1. Average equity $= (380 + 420)/2 = 400$. ROE $= 48/400 = 12.00\%$. (Using beginning equity alone would give 12.63%.)
2. Book value per share $= 420/20 = $ USD 21.00.
3. Market capitalisation $= 60 \times 20 = $ USD 1,200 million. Price-to-book $= 60/21 = 2.86$.
4. Next dividend $D_1 = 1.20 \times 1.05 = 1.26$.
5. Implied cost of equity $= 1.26/60 + 0.05 = 0.021 + 0.05 =$ **7.10%**.

Because ROE (12.00%) exceeds the cost of equity (7.10%), the firm earns more than investors require on book capital, which is consistent with the market valuing it at almost three times book value.

**Voting check:** a holder of 1,000 shares in an election for five seats has 1,000 votes per seat under statutory voting, but 5,000 votes to place on one candidate under cumulative voting.

## Common exam traps
- Using $D_0$ instead of $D_1$ in the implied cost of equity; the numerator is next year's dividend.
- Thinking cumulative voting gives more total votes. Totals are the same; the difference is being able to concentrate them.
- Confusing cumulative preference shares (missed dividends accrue) with cumulative voting.
- Assuming a high ROE always signals strong performance; leverage and buybacks can inflate it.
- Saying book value equals market value. Book value is historical accounting; market value reflects expected future cash flows.

## Key takeaways
- Common shares are residual claims with voting rights; preference shares rank higher, pay fixed dividends and usually do not vote.
- Cumulative voting helps minority shareholders by letting them concentrate shares × seats votes.
- Private equity trades liquidity and transparency for control and a long horizon; depository receipts give access to foreign shares.
- ROE = net income / average book equity; book and market value differ because markets price future prospects.
- The cost of equity is the investor's required return; $r_e = D_1/P_0 + g$ gives a quick implied estimate.
