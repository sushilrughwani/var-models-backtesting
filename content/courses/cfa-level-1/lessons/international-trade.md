## Learning goals
- Distinguish absolute advantage from comparative advantage and identify which good each country should export.
- Explain the Ricardian and Heckscher-Ohlin explanations of trade patterns.
- Calculate the welfare effects of a tariff for a small importing country, including the deadweight loss.
- Compare tariffs, quotas, export subsidies and voluntary export restraints.
- Rank trading blocs by degree of integration and explain trade creation and trade diversion.

## Explanation

### Why countries trade
Trade lets each country specialise in what it does relatively well and buy the rest. The gains include lower prices and more variety for consumers, economies of scale for exporters, more competition and the spread of technology. The costs fall on specific groups: workers and firms in industries that lose out to imports may face job losses that take years to absorb.

### Absolute versus comparative advantage
A country has an **absolute advantage** in a good if it can produce it with fewer resources than another country. It has a **comparative advantage** if its **opportunity cost** of producing that good, measured in units of the other good given up, is lower.

$$
\text{Opportunity cost of good X} = \frac{\text{output of Y per unit of input}}{\text{output of X per unit of input}}
$$

The key insight: comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, determines who should export what. Even a country that is better at everything gains by specialising in the goods where its edge is largest. Both countries gain if the **terms of trade** (the rate at which goods exchange) lie between their two opportunity costs.

### Two explanations of trade patterns
- **Ricardian model**: differences in labour productivity, driven by technology, create comparative advantage.
- **Heckscher-Ohlin model**: differences in factor endowments drive trade. A capital-rich country exports capital-intensive goods; a labour-rich country exports labour-intensive goods. Trade tends to raise the income of each country's abundant factor and lower that of its scarce factor, which helps explain political resistance to trade.

### Trade restrictions
- **Tariff**: a tax on imports. It raises the domestic price, increases domestic production, reduces consumption and imports, and generates government revenue.
- **Import quota**: a limit on the quantity imported. The domestic price rises as with a tariff, but the gap between domestic and world price goes to whoever holds the import licences (quota rent), not necessarily the government.
- **Export subsidy**: a payment to exporters. It raises the domestic price of the exported good and costs taxpayers.
- **Voluntary export restraint (VER)**: the exporting country agrees to limit shipments. The quota rent goes to foreign exporters, making it the costliest option for the importing country.

For a small country that cannot affect world prices, any of these produces a **deadweight loss**: consumers lose more than producers and the government gain.

### Trading blocs
Blocs are ranked by how deeply members integrate:

1. **Free trade area**: no barriers among members; each keeps its own external tariffs.
2. **Customs union**: adds a common external tariff.
3. **Common market**: adds free movement of labour and capital.
4. **Economic union**: adds common economic institutions and coordinated policy.
5. **Monetary union**: adds a single currency.

A bloc causes **trade creation** when cheaper imports from a member replace higher-cost domestic production, which raises welfare. It causes **trade diversion** when imports switch from a low-cost outsider to a higher-cost member just because the member faces no tariff, which can reduce welfare.

## Worked example
**Part A - Comparative advantage.** In one hour of labour, Country A produces 4 units of cloth or 2 units of wine. Country B produces 1 unit of cloth or 1 unit of wine.

- A has an absolute advantage in both goods.
- A's opportunity cost of 1 wine = 4/2 = 2 cloth; B's = 1/1 = 1 cloth. **B has the comparative advantage in wine.**
- A's opportunity cost of 1 cloth = 2/4 = 0.5 wine; B's = 1 wine. **A has the comparative advantage in cloth.**
- Both gain if 1 wine trades for between 1 and 2 cloth.

**Part B - Tariff welfare.** A small country has domestic demand $Q_d = 100 - 4P$ and supply $Q_s = -20 + 4P$. The world price is 10 and the government imposes a tariff of 2, raising the domestic price to 12.

| | Price 10 | Price 12 |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | 60 | 52 |
| Supply | 20 | 28 |
| Imports | 40 | 24 |

- Loss of consumer surplus: $2 \times (60 + 52)/2 = 112$
- Gain in producer surplus: $2 \times (20 + 28)/2 = 48$
- Tariff revenue: $2 \times 24 = 48$
- Deadweight loss: $112 - 48 - 48 = 16$, made up of a production loss of $0.5 \times 2 \times 8 = 8$ and a consumption loss of $0.5 \times 2 \times 8 = 8$.

The country is worse off by **16** in net welfare.

## Common exam traps
- Deciding trade patterns by absolute advantage. Always compare opportunity costs.
- Inverting the opportunity cost ratio. The cost of X is what you give up of Y per unit of X.
- Assuming a quota has the same welfare effect as a tariff. The price effect can match, but the rent may go to licence holders or, under a VER, to foreign exporters.
- Mixing up a free trade area and a customs union. Only a customs union has a common external tariff.

## Key takeaways
- Comparative advantage, based on opportunity cost, determines the gains from trade.
- Ricardian trade comes from productivity differences; Heckscher-Ohlin trade comes from factor endowments.
- A tariff in a small country raises the domestic price and creates a deadweight loss equal to the consumer loss minus producer gain and tariff revenue.
- Under a VER the importing country loses the quota rent to foreign exporters.
- Integration deepens from free trade area to customs union, common market, economic union and monetary union.
