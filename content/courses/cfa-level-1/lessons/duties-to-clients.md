---
title: "Standard III: Duties to Clients"
---

## Learning goals
- Identify who the client is and explain the duty of loyalty, prudence and care, including proxy voting and soft dollars.
- Distinguish fair dealing from equal treatment in disseminating recommendations and allocating trades.
- Apply the suitability requirement using an investment policy statement, including unsolicited trades.
- Describe what fair, accurate and complete performance presentation requires.
- State when confidential client information may be disclosed.

## Explanation
Standard III is the heart of the client relationship. Its five parts all express one idea: **the client's interests come first.**

### A. Loyalty, prudence and care
You owe clients loyalty and must act with reasonable care and prudent judgment, placing their interests before your employer's and your own.

- **Identify the client.** For a pension plan, the duty runs to the plan's **beneficiaries**, not to the sponsoring company's management. For a fund, the client is the fund and its mandate.
- **Proxy voting** is part of managing assets. Vote in clients' interests, but you need not vote every proxy if a cost-benefit analysis shows voting is not worth it.
- **Soft dollars** (commissions that buy research) belong to the client. Use them only for research that benefits the client, and seek best execution.

### B. Fair dealing
Deal fairly and objectively with all clients when disseminating recommendations or taking investment action. **Fair does not mean equal.** Firms may offer premium service levels, provided they are disclosed and available to anyone who pays, and the timing of recommendation changes does not disadvantage other clients.

- Disseminate new recommendations and changes so that all clients who should receive them get them as close to simultaneously as possible.
- Allocate block trades **pro rata** (or by another fair, disclosed method) across participating accounts.
- In an oversubscribed IPO, do not favour your own or family accounts over clients.

### C. Suitability
In an advisory relationship, before recommending or acting, make reasonable inquiry into the client's investment experience, risk and return objectives, and constraints (liquidity, time horizon, tax, legal and unique circumstances). Record these in an **investment policy statement (IPS)** and review it at least annually or when circumstances change.

- Judge suitability in the context of the **whole portfolio**, not each security in isolation.
- **Unsolicited trades**: if a client requests a trade that is unsuitable, explain the mismatch before executing. If the trade is small relative to the portfolio, you may proceed after the discussion. If it would materially change the portfolio, consider updating the IPS with the client or, if the client refuses, decide whether the relationship can continue.
- For a **fund manager**, suitability means sticking to the fund's stated mandate and strategy.

### D. Performance presentation
When communicating investment performance, make reasonable efforts to ensure it is **fair, accurate and complete**. No cherry-picking the best accounts or periods, no presenting simulated or back-tested results as actual, and clearly disclose when results are for a single account or a model. Brief presentations are fine if fuller detail is available on request. Complying with the Global Investment Performance Standards is the recommended way to meet this duty.

### E. Preservation of confidentiality
Keep information about current, former and prospective clients confidential unless:
1. it concerns **illegal activities** by the client,
2. disclosure is **required by law**, or
3. the client **permits** disclosure.

Sharing information with CFA Institute's professional conduct staff during an investigation is also permitted.

## Worked example
**Scenario.** Ji-woo Park, CFA, manages accounts at the fictional Silverbirch Advisors. Three issues arise in one week.

**Issue 1.** Ji-woo buys 30,000 shares of a mid-cap stock as a block for three discretionary accounts that ordered 20,000, 15,000 and 15,000 shares (total 50,000). Only 30,000 are filled.
- Fill ratio = 30,000 / 50,000 = 0.6.
- Allocations: 20,000 × 0.6 = **12,000**; 15,000 × 0.6 = **9,000**; 15,000 × 0.6 = **9,000**. Total 30,000.
- Giving the largest client all 20,000 would breach fair dealing.

**Issue 2.** A conservative retiree, whose IPS specifies capital preservation, asks Ji-woo to put 2% of the portfolio into a speculative crypto fund. Ji-woo explains that it does not fit the IPS. Because the amount is small relative to the portfolio, he may execute after documenting the discussion.

**Issue 3.** A journalist asks Ji-woo whether a well-known client is a Silverbirch customer. No law requires disclosure and the client has not consented, so he declines to comment.

**Conclusion: allocate the partial fill pro rata (12,000 / 9,000 / 9,000), execute the small unsolicited trade only after discussing its unsuitability, and keep client identity confidential.**

## Common exam traps
- Treating the pension plan sponsor's managers as the client rather than the beneficiaries.
- Thinking fair dealing requires identical treatment of every client. Disclosed premium service tiers are allowed.
- Refusing every unsolicited unsuitable trade. The requirement is to discuss it first; small trades can proceed.
- Believing all proxies must be voted. A reasonable cost-benefit analysis can justify not voting.
- Forgetting the three exceptions to confidentiality, or that cooperating with CFA Institute investigations is allowed.

## Key takeaways
- Client interests come before employer and personal interests.
- Identify the true client, use soft dollars only for client benefit, and vote proxies in clients' interests.
- Fair dealing means fair, disclosed, timely and pro rata - not identical.
- Suitability rests on a current IPS and is judged at the portfolio level.
- Performance must be fair, accurate and complete; GIPS compliance is the recommended route.
- Confidentiality has narrow exceptions: illegal activity, legal requirement or client consent.
