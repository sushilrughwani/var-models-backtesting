## Learning goals
- Describe the roles and objectives of a central bank and the qualities that make it effective.
- Explain how open market operations, the policy rate and reserve requirements work, and calculate the money multiplier.
- Trace the transmission of a policy rate change to spending and inflation, and use the neutral rate to judge the policy stance.
- Explain the limits of monetary policy and how fiscal and monetary policy combine.

## Explanation

### What a central bank does
A central bank is the sole issuer of the national currency, banker to the government and to commercial banks, lender of last resort in a crisis, supervisor of the banking and payments systems, manager of foreign reserves, and the authority that conducts monetary policy. Its main objective is almost always **price stability**, typically defined as an inflation target such as 2%. Some central banks also have explicit goals for employment or financial stability.

Policy works best when the central bank is **independent** (free from political pressure to print money before elections), **credible** (people believe it will hit its target), and **transparent** (it explains its decisions and forecasts). Credibility is powerful: if people expect 2% inflation, wage and price setting tends to deliver it.

### How banks create money
Under fractional reserve banking, banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. The loan is spent and redeposited, and the process repeats. With a reserve requirement $r$:

$$
\text{Money multiplier} = \frac{1}{r}
$$

where $r$ is the required reserve ratio. A new deposit of $D$ can support up to $D / r$ of total deposits in the banking system.

### Money and prices
The quantity theory of money starts from the identity

$$
M \times V = P \times Y
$$

where $M$ is the money supply, $V$ is the velocity of circulation, $P$ is the price level and $Y$ is real output. If velocity is stable, then in growth-rate terms money growth ≈ inflation + real output growth. The **Fisher effect** says the nominal interest rate equals the real rate plus expected inflation.

### The tools
- **Open market operations.** Buying government bonds from banks adds reserves and lowers short-term rates; selling bonds drains reserves.
- **The policy rate.** The rate at which the central bank lends to banks (or targets in the interbank market). Raising it makes borrowing more expensive throughout the economy.
- **Reserve requirements.** Raising the required ratio shrinks the money multiplier. This tool is used less often in developed markets.

### Transmission
A cut in the policy rate lowers short-term market rates and bank lending rates; raises asset prices (bonds, equities, property), creating a wealth effect; improves expectations and confidence; and tends to weaken the domestic currency, boosting exports. All four channels raise aggregate demand, which eventually puts upward pressure on inflation. The effect arrives with long and variable lags.

### The neutral rate
The neutral policy rate is the rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy:

$$
R_{\text{neutral}} = \text{trend real growth} + \text{inflation target}
$$

A policy rate above neutral is **contractionary**; below neutral is **expansionary**.

### Limits of monetary policy
- Long-term rates are set by markets. If investors fear future inflation, long yields may rise even as the central bank cuts.
- In a **liquidity trap**, rates are near zero and extra money is simply hoarded. Central banks then turn to **quantitative easing**, buying longer-term bonds and other assets in large amounts.
- Deflation is especially hard to fight because nominal rates cannot fall far below zero, so real rates stay high.
- Banks may be unwilling to lend, and households unwilling to borrow, however low rates go.

### Fiscal and monetary policy together
| | Easy fiscal | Tight fiscal |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy monetary** | Strongly expansionary; both private and public demand rise | Private sector grows relative to public sector |
| **Tight monetary** | Higher interest rates, public sector grows relative to private | Strongly contractionary; demand falls |

## Worked example
**Part A - Policy stance.** A central bank targets 2.0% inflation. Trend real GDP growth is estimated at 2.0%. The current policy rate is 5.5%.

- Neutral rate = 2.0% + 2.0% = 4.0%.
- The policy rate of 5.5% is 1.5 percentage points above neutral.

Policy is **contractionary**.

**Part B - Quantity theory.** Money supply grows at 6% a year, velocity is stable and real output grows at 2.5%.

- Approximate inflation: $6\% - 2.5\% = 3.5\%$.
- Exact version: $1.06 / 1.025 - 1 = 3.41\%$.

**Part C - Money creation.** The reserve requirement is 10% and a bank receives a new deposit of 100 million.

- Money multiplier = 1 / 0.10 = 10.
- Maximum total deposits supported = 100 × 10 = **1,000 million**, of which 900 million is newly created through lending.

## Common exam traps
- Comparing the policy rate with the inflation target instead of with the neutral rate (growth plus inflation target).
- Believing that a central bank controls long-term yields directly; it sets short rates, and markets set long rates.
- Saying that selling bonds in open market operations adds reserves. Selling drains reserves; buying adds them.
- Forgetting that a higher reserve requirement reduces the money multiplier.
- Assuming lower rates always revive spending; in a liquidity trap they may not.

## Key takeaways
- Central banks aim mainly at price stability; independence, credibility and transparency make policy effective.
- Tools: open market operations, the policy rate and reserve requirements; money multiplier = 1/r.
- Rate changes work through market rates, asset prices, expectations and the exchange rate, with long lags.
- Neutral rate = trend real growth + inflation target; above it is contractionary.
- Liquidity traps and deflation limit conventional policy, leading to quantitative easing.
- Easy fiscal plus easy monetary is strongly expansionary; tight plus tight is strongly contractionary.
