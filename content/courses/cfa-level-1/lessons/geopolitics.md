## Learning goals
- Identify state and non-state actors and the motives that drive their behaviour.
- Explain how cooperation versus non-cooperation and globalisation versus nationalism combine into four broad geopolitical regimes.
- Describe the national security, economic and financial tools of geopolitics.
- Distinguish event, exogenous and thematic geopolitical risk and assess them by likelihood, velocity and size of impact.
- Explain how geopolitical risk reaches portfolios through cash flows, discount rates and volatility.

## Explanation

### What geopolitics studies
Geopolitics examines how geography, politics and economics shape the relations between countries and the other players who operate across borders. For investors, the point is practical: a trade war, a sanctions package or an election in a major economy can change the earnings and risk of a portfolio almost overnight. This lesson builds on the fiscal and monetary policy lessons by looking at forces that governments use beyond their own borders.

### The actors
- **State actors** are national governments and their agencies. They pursue national security, economic prosperity and the protection of their political system and culture.
- **Non-state actors** include multinational companies, international organisations, non-governmental organisations, influential individuals and even militant groups. They can move capital, shape rules or disrupt supply chains independently of governments.

### Cooperation and globalisation
A state is **cooperative** when it seeks shared rules and agreements with others, for example on trade, standards or the environment. It is **non-cooperative** when it acts alone or pursues its interests at others' expense. Separately, the world economy can lean toward **globalisation** (open flows of goods, capital, people and ideas) or toward **nationalism** (self-reliance and protection of domestic industry).

Crossing the two dimensions gives four broad regimes:

| | Nationalism | Globalisation |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-cooperative** | Autarky: self-sufficiency, little trade | Hegemony: a dominant power sets the rules |
| **Cooperative** | Bilateralism: deals between pairs of countries | Multilateralism: many countries share common rules |

Each regime has investment consequences. Multilateralism tends to lower costs through efficient global supply chains, while autarky raises costs and fragments markets but can reduce exposure to foreign shocks.

### The tools
- **National security tools**: armed conflict, espionage, alliances and military cooperation.
- **Economic tools**: cooperative ones such as free trade areas and common markets; non-cooperative ones such as tariffs, quotas and nationalisation of foreign-owned assets.
- **Financial tools**: currency reserves and access to payment systems, sanctions, restrictions on foreign investment and capital controls.

Financial tools have grown in importance because they are fast and hard to avoid when a currency dominates world payments.

### Types of geopolitical risk
- **Event risk** arises around known dates, such as elections or treaty deadlines.
- **Exogenous risk** is a sudden, unexpected shock, such as an invasion or a coup.
- **Thematic risk** is a long-running trend, such as climate change, cyber threats or the gradual fragmentation of trade.

Each risk is assessed on three dimensions: its **likelihood**, its **velocity** (how quickly markets feel it), and the **size and nature of its impact** (high and broad versus contained). A high-velocity exogenous shock hits prices immediately; a thematic risk builds slowly and is better handled through strategic allocation.

### How risk reaches portfolios
Geopolitical shocks change expected **cash flows** (lost sales, higher input costs, seized assets), **discount rates** (higher risk premiums), and **volatility**, often triggering a flight to safe-haven assets. Analysts use scenario analysis and identify **signposts**, observable signals that a scenario is becoming more or less likely.

## Worked example
An analyst covers a manufacturer that buys 30% of its components from a country facing possible sanctions. Current EPS is forecast at 5.00 and the stock trades on a P/E of 12.

**Step 1 - Classify the risk.** A sanctions decision is expected at a scheduled summit next quarter, so this is **event risk**, with a plausible escalation tail that would behave like an exogenous shock.

**Step 2 - Build scenarios.**

| Scenario | Probability | EPS |
|---|---|---|
| Status quo | 70% | 5.00 |
| Targeted sanctions | 20% | 4.20 |
| Broad escalation | 10% | 3.00 |

**Step 3 - Probability-weighted EPS.**

$$
0.70(5.00) + 0.20(4.20) + 0.10(3.00) = 3.50 + 0.84 + 0.30 = 4.64
$$

This is 7.2% below the status-quo forecast. Holding the P/E at 12, the implied value falls from 60.00 to $12 \times 4.64 = 55.68$. If the risk also raised the required return, the multiple itself would fall, lowering value further.

**Step 4 - Signposts and actions.** Watch summit statements, shipping data and the company's progress in qualifying alternative suppliers. Because velocity could be high, the analyst recommends trimming the position now rather than waiting.

The probability-weighted EPS is **4.64**, and the risk is primarily event risk with a fat tail.

## Common exam traps
- Labelling a scheduled election as exogenous risk. Known-date risks are event risk; exogenous risk is unanticipated.
- Assuming non-state actors are irrelevant. Multinationals and international organisations can be powerful geopolitical players.
- Treating cooperation and globalisation as the same dimension. A country can be cooperative yet nationalist, for example through bilateral deals.
- Ignoring velocity. Two risks with the same likelihood and impact need different responses if one hits in a day and the other over a decade.

## Key takeaways
- Actors are states and non-state players such as multinationals and international organisations.
- Cooperation versus non-cooperation and globalisation versus nationalism define four regimes: autarky, hegemony, bilateralism and multilateralism.
- Tools of geopolitics are national security, economic and financial.
- Risks are event, exogenous or thematic, assessed by likelihood, velocity and impact.
- Geopolitical risk affects cash flows, discount rates and volatility; scenario analysis and signposts help manage it.
