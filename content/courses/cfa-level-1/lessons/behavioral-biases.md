title: "The Behavioral Biases of Individuals"
---

## Learning goals
- Distinguish cognitive errors from emotional biases and explain why the distinction matters for advice.
- Identify belief-perseverance biases and information-processing biases from realistic behaviour.
- Identify the main emotional biases, including loss aversion, overconfidence and the endowment effect.
- Recommend whether to moderate or adapt to a given bias.
- Explain how individual biases can contribute to market anomalies such as bubbles and momentum.

## Explanation

### Why biases matter
Earlier lessons assumed investors maximise utility rationally. Real people take mental shortcuts and react to feelings, and their portfolios suffer for it: they hold losers too long, trade too much, and under-diversify. A behavioural bias is a systematic deviation from rational decision-making - systematic, so it can be predicted and managed.

### Cognitive errors versus emotional biases
- **Cognitive errors** come from faulty reasoning, memory or information processing. Because they stem from a lack of understanding, they can often be **corrected** with education, better information and decision checklists.
- **Emotional biases** come from feelings, impulses and intuition. They are hard to change by explanation alone, so advisers more often **adapt** to them - building a portfolio the client can live with.

A useful rule of thumb: moderate cognitive errors; adapt to emotional biases. Two refinements apply. A wealthier client can afford to have their biases accommodated, while a client whose biases threaten their standard of living needs more moderation. And even emotional biases can sometimes be moderated if their cost is large.

### Cognitive errors - belief perseverance
These involve clinging to existing beliefs.
- **Conservatism** - failing to update enough when new information arrives.
- **Confirmation** - seeking evidence that supports a view and ignoring evidence against it.
- **Representativeness** - judging by resemblance to a stereotype, for example assuming a good company is a good stock, or ignoring base rates.
- **Illusion of control** - believing one can influence outcomes that one cannot, which encourages concentrated, actively traded positions.
- **Hindsight** - believing past events were predictable, leading to overestimating one's forecasting skill.

### Cognitive errors - information processing
These involve mishandling information.
- **Anchoring and adjustment** - sticking too closely to an initial number, such as a purchase price or a first forecast.
- **Mental accounting** - treating money differently depending on which "bucket" it sits in, ignoring correlations across buckets.
- **Framing** - answering differently depending on how a question is posed (gains versus losses).
- **Availability** - judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind, such as recent or vivid events.

### Emotional biases
- **Loss aversion** - losses feel roughly twice as painful as equal gains feel good. Produces the **disposition effect**: selling winners too early and holding losers too long.
- **Overconfidence** - overestimating knowledge (prediction overconfidence) or ability (certainty overconfidence); leads to excessive trading and under-diversification. It has cognitive elements but is classified as emotional because it is hard to correct.
- **Self-control** - favouring spending today over saving for long-term goals.
- **Status quo** - doing nothing when change would help.
- **Endowment** - valuing an asset more simply because one owns it, often an inherited stock.
- **Regret aversion** - avoiding decisions for fear of regret; can cause herding into popular investments.

### From individuals to markets
When many investors share a bias, prices can be affected. Overconfidence and confirmation feed **bubbles**; herding and under-reaction to news help explain **momentum**; and preference for familiar or "glamour" companies contributes to the **value effect**.

## Worked example
An adviser meets Priya, 45, who holds 40% of her savings in shares of her former employer. Her notes record three statements:

1. "I know this company better than any analyst. I can tell when the price will rise." 
2. "The stock fell from 80 to 55, so I'll sell once it gets back to 80."
3. "My father gave me the first shares, so I couldn't sell them."

**Step 1 - identify each bias.**
- Statement 1: overconfidence and illusion of control - she believes superior insight lets her time the stock.
- Statement 2: anchoring on the 80 purchase level, together with loss aversion (refusing to realise a loss).
- Statement 3: endowment bias - she values the gifted shares more because she owns them.

**Step 2 - classify.** Illusion of control and anchoring are cognitive; overconfidence, loss aversion and endowment are emotional. The emotional biases dominate.

**Step 3 - assess the stakes.** A 40% single-stock position tied to her former employer threatens her retirement goal, so the cost of the biases is high.

**Step 4 - recommend.** Moderate the cognitive pieces with education (show that 80 is irrelevant to future returns and present the base rate of single-stock losses). Adapt to the emotional pieces with a plan she can accept: reduce the position gradually over two years, perhaps keeping the original gifted shares.

**Conclusion: the dominant biases are emotional, so the adviser should adapt with a staged reduction while moderating the anchoring and illusion of control through education.**

## Common exam traps
- Classifying overconfidence as cognitive; it is treated as an emotional bias.
- Confusing conservatism (under-reacting to new information) with status quo bias (not acting).
- Mixing up anchoring (fixed reference number) and representativeness (stereotype matching).
- Assuming every bias should be corrected; emotional biases are usually adapted to, especially for wealthy clients.
- Attributing the disposition effect to mental accounting rather than loss aversion.

## Key takeaways
- Cognitive errors come from faulty reasoning and are usually moderated.
- Emotional biases come from feelings and are usually adapted to.
- Belief-perseverance and information-processing biases are the two cognitive families.
- Loss aversion drives the disposition effect; overconfidence drives excessive trading.
- Shared biases help explain bubbles, momentum and the value effect.
