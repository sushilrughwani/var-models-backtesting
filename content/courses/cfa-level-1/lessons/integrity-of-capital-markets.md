---
title: "Standard II: Integrity of Capital Markets"
---

## Learning goals
- Define "material" and "nonpublic" and test whether information meets both conditions.
- Apply the mosaic theory to distinguish legitimate research from misuse of inside information.
- Describe firm controls such as information barriers and restricted lists.
- Distinguish information-based from transaction-based market manipulation.

## Explanation
Markets work when prices reflect information that everyone can access and when trading reflects genuine supply and demand. Standard II protects both conditions. Its two parts deal with **material nonpublic information** and **market manipulation**.

### A. Material nonpublic information
You must not act, or cause others to act, on information that is both material and nonpublic. Both tests must be met.

- **Material**: disclosure would likely affect the security's price, or a reasonable investor would want it before deciding. Reliability matters too - a vague rumour from an unreliable source is less likely to be material than a specific statement from the CFO.
- **Nonpublic**: the information has not been disseminated to the market. Information shared with a select group, such as analysts on a private call, is still nonpublic until it is broadly released.

Typical examples: unannounced earnings, a pending takeover, a regulatory approval or rejection, a large contract win or loss, a dividend change.

"Causing others to act" matters. Passing a tip to a friend or relative violates the standard even if you never trade yourself.

If you receive material nonpublic information, you should not trade or recommend the security. A sensible response is to encourage the issuer to make the information public and to inform your compliance department.

### The mosaic theory
Analysts are paid to dig. The **mosaic theory** says you may reach a conclusion that would be material **if** you build it from public information plus **nonmaterial** nonpublic information. For example, combining store-traffic counts, satellite images of car parks, supplier interviews about general trends and published data to conclude that a retailer's sales are falling is legitimate. You should keep records of how you built the mosaic.

What you may not do is slot a single piece of material nonpublic information into the mosaic and call it research.

### Firm controls
Firms that hold sensitive information, such as investment banks, use:
- **Information barriers (firewalls)** between deal teams and research or trading.
- **Watch lists** (monitored internally) and **restricted lists** (trading and recommendations limited).
- Pre-clearance of personal trades and review of proprietary trading.

These controls help, but they do not shift personal responsibility.

### B. Market manipulation
You must not engage in practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume with the intent to mislead market participants. Two families:

- **Information-based manipulation**: spreading false or misleading rumours to move a price - for example, posting false takeover rumours online, or a "pump and dump" where a promoter hypes a stock before selling.
- **Transaction-based manipulation**: trades that create a false impression of price or volume, such as trading back and forth between your own accounts to fake activity, or cornering supply of a security to squeeze short sellers.

**Intent is the key.** Legitimate strategies - tax-loss selling, arbitrage, a large trade that happens to move the price - are not manipulation if the purpose is not to mislead.

## Worked example
**Scenario.** Nadia Petrov, CFA, an analyst at the fictional Fenwick Asset Management, covers a listed shipping company, Arcturus Lines. She takes three steps:

1. She analyses public port-traffic statistics showing Arcturus's vessel calls fell 12% this quarter.
2. She interviews a former Arcturus dock manager, who says general demand in the region "feels soft" but shares no company figures.
3. At a dinner, Arcturus's treasurer tells her, before any announcement, that the company will cut its dividend next month.

**Step 1 - Classify each item.**
- Port statistics: public.
- Former manager's view: nonpublic but nonmaterial (general opinion, no specific figures).
- Dividend cut: nonpublic and clearly material - dividend changes typically move prices.

**Step 2 - Mosaic test.** Items 1 and 2 alone support a negative view. Building a "sell" recommendation on those is legitimate under the mosaic theory.

**Step 3 - The dividend tip.** Nadia must not trade, recommend a sale, or pass the information on because of it. She should tell compliance and encourage Arcturus to disclose the cut publicly. If her negative view was already documented from items 1 and 2 before the dinner, she should still consult compliance before acting, since the firm may restrict the stock.

**Conclusion: the port data and interview support a legitimate mosaic, but the dividend information is material nonpublic and cannot be used.**

## Common exam traps
- Treating information as public because "many analysts heard it" on a private call. Selective disclosure is still nonpublic.
- Forgetting that tipping others is a violation even without personal trading.
- Assuming the mosaic theory allows any conclusion. It covers only public plus nonmaterial nonpublic inputs.
- Labelling any trade that moves a price as manipulation. Intent to mislead is required.
- Believing firewalls remove personal responsibility; individuals remain accountable.

## Key takeaways
- Information must be both material and nonpublic for the prohibition to apply.
- Do not trade, recommend or tip on material nonpublic information; involve compliance.
- The mosaic theory protects diligent research built from public and nonmaterial inputs; document it.
- Firewalls, watch lists and restricted lists are standard controls.
- Manipulation, whether through information or transactions, requires intent to mislead.
