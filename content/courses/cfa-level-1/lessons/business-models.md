## Learning goals
- Describe the main building blocks of a business model: customers, value proposition, delivery and value capture.
- Distinguish common pricing approaches and revenue models, such as value-based pricing, tiered pricing, bundling, razor-and-blades, freemium and subscriptions.
- Explain how network effects, platforms and asset-light structures shape a firm's economics.
- Calculate customer lifetime value and payback for a subscription business and interpret the results.

## Explanation

### What a business model answers
A business model is a description of how a company makes money. An analyst should be able to answer four questions in plain language:

1. **Who are the customers?** Which segments, where, and through what channels are they reached?
2. **What is the value proposition?** What problem does the product solve, and why would a customer choose it over alternatives?
3. **How is the value delivered?** The **value chain**: the activities, assets, suppliers and partners the firm uses to produce and distribute the offering.
4. **How is value captured?** The pricing approach and revenue model that turn customer value into profit.

If you cannot explain a business in a few sentences using these questions, you are not ready to forecast it.

### Pricing approaches
- **Value-based pricing** sets price according to what the product is worth to the customer. It works best when the offering is differentiated.
- **Cost-plus pricing** adds a markup to cost. It is simple but ignores willingness to pay.
- **Competitive (market) pricing** follows rivals, typical in commodity-like markets where the firm is a price taker.

The firm's **pricing power** depends on market structure, differentiation and switching costs, linking this topic back to market structures in Economics.

### Pricing tactics and revenue models
- **Price discrimination / tiered pricing**: different prices for different customers or volumes (student discounts, good-better-best tiers, peak versus off-peak).
- **Bundling**: selling products together (a software suite, a cable package) to raise total spend and reduce churn.
- **Razor-and-blades**: sell the base product cheaply, sometimes at a loss, and earn profit on recurring consumables (printers and ink, consoles and games).
- **Freemium**: a free basic version attracts users; a fraction convert to paid premium tiers.
- **Subscription**: recurring fees for ongoing access, producing predictable revenue.
- **Dynamic pricing and auctions**: prices that move with demand in real time.
- **Penetration pricing** (low initial price to gain share) versus **price skimming** (high initial price to early adopters, cut later).

### Network effects and platforms
A product has **network effects** when it becomes more valuable as more people use it. **Platform** (two-sided) businesses connect two groups, such as buyers and sellers or drivers and riders, and charge one or both sides. Strong network effects can create winner-takes-most markets and durable competitive advantage, but building the network often requires heavy early losses.

### Asset-light versus asset-heavy
**Asset-light** models outsource production or rely on others' assets (franchising, marketplaces, contract manufacturing), giving high returns on invested capital and fast scaling but less control. **Asset-heavy** models own factories, networks or fleets, creating barriers to entry but higher fixed costs and operating leverage.

### Unit economics for recurring revenue
For subscription businesses, analysts compare what a customer is worth with what it costs to acquire them. A simple, undiscounted version of customer lifetime value (LTV) is

$$
\text{LTV} = \frac{\text{ARPU} \times m}{c}
$$

where ARPU is average monthly revenue per user, $m$ is the gross margin and $c$ is the monthly churn rate, so $1/c$ is the expected customer lifetime in months. Compare LTV with **customer acquisition cost (CAC)**; a ratio comfortably above 1 means growth creates value.

## Worked example
StreamLeaf sells a music-learning app at USD 20 per month. Its gross margin is 70%, 4% of subscribers cancel each month, and marketing spends USD 200 to acquire each new subscriber.

**Step 1 - Monthly gross profit per subscriber.** 20 × 0.70 = 14 dollars.

**Step 2 - Expected lifetime.** 1 / 0.04 = 25 months.

**Step 3 - Lifetime value.** LTV = 14 / 0.04 = 14 × 25 = **USD 350**.

**Step 4 - LTV to CAC.** 350 / 200 = **1.75**. Each subscriber returns 1.75 dollars of gross profit for every dollar spent acquiring them.

**Step 5 - CAC payback.** 200 / 14 = **14.3 months** to recover acquisition cost.

**Step 6 - Interpret.** The model creates value, but the margin of safety is thin: LTV ignores discounting and other operating costs, and payback takes over a year. Reducing churn from 4% to 3% would raise LTV to 14 / 0.03 = 466.67, showing why subscription firms obsess over retention. Classifying the model: subscription revenue, value-based pricing, asset-light delivery.

## Common exam traps
- Using revenue instead of gross profit in LTV, which overstates customer value.
- Confusing razor-and-blades (cheap base, profitable consumables) with freemium (free base, paid upgrade).
- Assuming network effects guarantee profitability; many platforms burn cash for years.
- Treating cost-plus pricing as value maximising; it ignores what customers are willing to pay.
- Judging an asset-light firm on revenue growth alone without checking control and partner risks.

## Key takeaways
- A business model explains who the customers are, what value they get, how it is delivered and how value is captured.
- Value-based pricing suits differentiated products; cost-plus and competitive pricing suit weaker pricing power.
- Revenue models include tiered pricing, bundling, razor-and-blades, freemium and subscriptions.
- Network effects and platforms can create durable advantage but need costly scale-building.
- Unit economics such as LTV/CAC and payback show whether customer growth creates value.
