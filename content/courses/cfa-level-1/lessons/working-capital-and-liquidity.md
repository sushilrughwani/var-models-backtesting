title: "Working Capital and Liquidity"
---

## Learning goals
- Calculate days of inventory, days of receivables, days of payables and the cash conversion cycle.
- Compute and interpret the current, quick and cash ratios.
- Distinguish primary from secondary sources of liquidity and identify drags and pulls on liquidity.
- Calculate the annualised cost of forgoing a trade-credit discount and compare short-term financing choices.
- Contrast conservative, moderate and aggressive working capital approaches.

## Explanation

### Why working capital matters
A profitable firm can still run out of cash. It pays suppliers and workers before customers pay it, so money is tied up in inventory and receivables. **Working capital management** is about how much cash gets tied up, for how long, and how the gap is financed.

### The cash conversion cycle
Measure each stage in days:

$$
\text{DIO} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{COGS}/365}, \quad
\text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Receivables}}{\text{Revenue}/365}, \quad
\text{DPO} = \frac{\text{Payables}}{\text{COGS}/365}
$$

$$
\text{CCC} = \text{DIO} + \text{DSO} - \text{DPO}
$$

DIO is days inventory outstanding (how long goods sit before sale), DSO is days sales outstanding (how long customers take to pay) and DPO is days payables outstanding (how long the firm takes to pay suppliers; purchases is the ideal denominator, COGS a common proxy). The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)** is the number of days cash is tied up in operations. A shorter cycle means less financing is needed, but a cycle that is too short may signal lost sales from tight credit or strained suppliers.

### Liquidity ratios

$$
\text{Current} = \frac{\text{CA}}{\text{CL}}, \quad
\text{Quick} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{ST investments} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{CL}}, \quad
\text{Cash} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{ST investments}}{\text{CL}}
$$

where CA is current assets and CL is current liabilities. Each ratio is stricter than the last: the quick ratio drops inventory, the cash ratio also drops receivables.

### Sources of liquidity, drags and pulls
**Primary sources** are the normal, everyday ones: cash balances, short-term investments, cash generated by operations, trade credit and bank lines. **Secondary sources** are used under stress and usually change the firm's financial or operating position: renegotiating debt, selling assets, or filing for bankruptcy protection.

A **drag** on liquidity delays inflows (slow-paying customers, obsolete inventory). A **pull** accelerates outflows (suppliers demanding earlier payment, lenders cutting credit lines).

### Short-term financing choices
**Trade credit.** Terms such as "2/10, net 30" offer a 2% discount if paid within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due on day 30. Skipping the discount is effectively a loan, and its annual cost is

$$
\text{Cost} = \left(1 + \frac{d}{1-d}\right)^{365/(N - D)} - 1
$$

where $d$ is the discount rate, $N$ the net period and $D$ the discount period. If this exceeds the firm's other borrowing costs, it should take the discount.

Other sources include **bank lines of credit** (uncommitted, committed with a commitment fee, or revolving), **secured loans** backed by receivables or inventory, **factoring** (selling receivables), and, for strong-credit firms, **commercial paper**, which is usually the cheapest short-term debt but needs backup credit lines.

### Working capital approaches
A **conservative** approach holds generous current assets and finances them mostly with long-term funds: safe but costly. An **aggressive** approach keeps current assets lean and relies on short-term debt: cheaper but exposed to refinancing risk. A **moderate** approach matches the maturity of financing to the life of the assets.

## Worked example
Orca Supplies reports (USD): revenue 7,300,000; COGS 5,110,000; receivables 800,000; inventory 700,000; payables 420,000; cash 250,000; short-term investments 150,000; other current liabilities 380,000. Its supplier offers terms of 2/10, net 30.

**Step 1 - Daily figures.** Revenue per day = 7,300,000 / 365 = 20,000. COGS per day = 5,110,000 / 365 = 14,000.

**Step 2 - Days measures.** DSO = 800,000 / 20,000 = 40 days. DIO = 700,000 / 14,000 = 50 days. DPO = 420,000 / 14,000 = 30 days.

**Step 3 - Cash conversion cycle.** CCC = 50 + 40 - 30 = **60 days**.

**Step 4 - Liquidity ratios.** Current assets = 250,000 + 150,000 + 800,000 + 700,000 = 1,900,000. Current liabilities = 420,000 + 380,000 = 800,000.
- Current ratio = 1,900,000 / 800,000 = 2.375
- Quick ratio = (250,000 + 150,000 + 800,000) / 800,000 = 1.50
- Cash ratio = 400,000 / 800,000 = 0.50

**Step 5 - Cost of skipping the discount.** d/(1 - d) = 0.02 / 0.98 = 0.020408. Exponent = 365 / (30 - 10) = 18.25.

$$
(1.020408)^{18.25} - 1 = 0.4459
$$

Forgoing the discount costs about **44.59% a year**. Unless Orca has no other funding, it should borrow on its bank line and pay suppliers on day 10.

## Common exam traps
- Adding DPO instead of subtracting it in the CCC.
- Using revenue in the denominator for DIO or DPO; inventory and payables relate to cost, not sales.
- Using the simple annualisation (0.02/0.98 × 365/20 = 37.24%) when the question asks for the effective annual cost.
- Using the full 30 days instead of the 20 extra days of credit in the trade-credit exponent.
- Assuming a higher current ratio is always better; excess current assets earn little and may hide slow-moving inventory.

## Key takeaways
- CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO measures how long cash is tied up in operations.
- Current, quick and cash ratios progressively exclude less liquid assets.
- Primary liquidity sources are everyday; secondary sources signal stress and change the firm's position.
- Forgoing trade discounts is usually very expensive financing.
- Conservative, moderate and aggressive approaches trade safety against cost.
