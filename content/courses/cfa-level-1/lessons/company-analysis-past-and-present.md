## Learning goals
- Describe the elements of a company research report and why understanding the business model comes first.
- Decompose revenue into volume and price drivers and calculate growth from those drivers.
- Calculate each segment's contribution to total revenue growth.
- Analyse operating performance using margins, incremental margins and operating leverage.
- Identify company-specific KPIs and explain how they connect to financial results.

## Explanation

### Start with the business model
Before forecasting anything, an analyst must explain in plain words how the company makes money: who its customers are, what it sells, how it prices, how it delivers and what it spends to do so. A typical company research report covers the business model, the industry and competitive position, historical financial performance, forecasts, valuation and risks. This lesson covers the "past and present" part: what has driven results so far.

Good sources include annual reports, segment disclosures, earnings call transcripts, investor presentations, industry data, and conversations with customers, suppliers and competitors.

### Revenue drivers
Revenue is almost always volume times price:

$$
\text{Revenue} = \text{Volume} \times \text{Average price}
$$

Volume might be units shipped, stores, subscribers or passengers; price might be average selling price, revenue per store or average revenue per user. Growth in each compounds:

$$
g_{\text{rev}} = (1 + g_{\text{vol}})(1 + g_{\text{price}}) - 1
$$

where $g_{\text{vol}}$ and $g_{\text{price}}$ are the growth rates of volume and price. Price itself can be split into pure price changes and **mix** (selling more of expensive or cheap products). Decomposing revenue this way tells you whether growth came from more customers, higher prices, a better mix or acquisitions, and each has different sustainability.

### Segment contributions
When a firm has several segments, the contribution of segment $i$ to total growth is

$$
\text{Contribution}_i = \frac{\text{Rev}_{i,t} - \text{Rev}_{i,t-1}}{\text{Total rev}_{t-1}}
$$

Contributions add up to total growth. A fast-growing but small segment may contribute less than a slow-growing large one.

### Operating performance
Look at the cost structure: cost of sales, selling and administrative expenses and R&D, each as a percentage of revenue. Key measures:
- **Gross margin** and **operating (EBIT) margin**: profit per unit of revenue.
- **Incremental margin**: change in operating profit divided by change in revenue. It shows how profitable the *extra* sales were.
- **Operating leverage**: the percentage change in EBIT divided by the percentage change in revenue. Firms with high fixed costs see profits move much more than sales.

Separate one-off items (restructuring charges, impairments, gains on asset sales) from recurring operations before drawing conclusions.

### KPIs and capital
Company-specific **key performance indicators** link operations to financials: same-store sales for retailers, load factor for airlines, churn for subscription businesses, backlog for manufacturers. Also review working capital needs, capital spending and returns on invested capital, since growth that consumes a lot of capital is worth less.

## Worked example
A coffee chain has two segments. Retail: 400 stores averaging USD 1.50 million revenue each last year, 440 stores averaging USD 1.56 million this year. Wholesale: revenue fell from USD 200 million to USD 190 million. EBIT rose from USD 96.0 million to USD 113.9 million.

1. Retail revenue: last year $400 \times 1.50 = 600$; this year $440 \times 1.56 = 686.4$.
2. Retail growth $= 1.10 \times 1.04 - 1 = 14.40\%$ (10% more stores, 4% more per store).
3. Total revenue: 800.0 to 876.4. Total growth $= 876.4/800 - 1 = 9.55\%$.
4. Contributions: retail $= 86.4/800 = 10.80$ percentage points; wholesale $= -10/800 = -1.25$ points. Check: $10.80 - 1.25 = 9.55$.
5. EBIT margin: $96.0/800 = 12.00\%$ last year; $113.9/876.4 = 13.00\%$ this year.
6. Incremental margin $= (113.9 - 96.0)/(876.4 - 800) = 17.9/76.4 = 23.43\%$.
7. EBIT growth $= 113.9/96.0 - 1 = 18.65\%$; operating leverage $= 18.65/9.55 =$ **1.95**.

Conclusion: growth came from store expansion and higher revenue per store in retail, partly offset by wholesale decline. Extra sales were far more profitable than average, so margins expanded and profit grew about twice as fast as sales.

## Common exam traps
- Adding volume and price growth (10% + 4% = 14%) instead of compounding them (14.4%).
- Dividing a segment's change by its own prior revenue when asked for its contribution to total growth; use total prior revenue.
- Assuming margin expansion will continue; check whether it came from operating leverage, pricing or a one-off item.
- Ignoring mix: average price can rise even if no individual price changes.
- Treating reported growth from an acquisition as organic growth.

## Key takeaways
- Understand the business model before analysing numbers.
- Revenue = volume × price; growth rates compound: $(1+g_v)(1+g_p) - 1$.
- Segment contributions to growth use total prior-year revenue and sum to total growth.
- Incremental margins and operating leverage show how profits respond to extra sales.
- KPIs connect operating activity to reported results and help judge sustainability.
