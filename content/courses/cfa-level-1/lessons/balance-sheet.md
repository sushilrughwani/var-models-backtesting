title: "Analyzing Balance Sheets"
---

## Learning goals
- Classify assets and liabilities as current or non-current and explain why the distinction matters.
- Describe the main measurement bases used on the balance sheet, including historical cost, amortised cost and fair value.
- Explain the classification of financial assets and where their gains and losses are reported.
- Prepare a common-size balance sheet and calculate liquidity and solvency ratios from it.

## Explanation

### What the balance sheet does and does not show
The balance sheet lists the resources a company controls and the claims against them at one date. It is **not** a statement of what the company is worth. Many valuable resources never appear (an internally built brand, a skilled workforce), and many items are carried at historical cost far below current value. Book equity is therefore a residual of accounting choices, not a market value.

### Current versus non-current
An asset is **current** if it is expected to be converted to cash or used up within one year or one operating cycle, whichever is longer: cash, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses. Liabilities are current if they are due within the same horizon: payables, accrued expenses, short-term borrowings and the **current portion of long-term debt**. Everything else is non-current (property, plant and equipment, intangibles, long-term debt, deferred taxes).

The split lets analysts judge liquidity. **Working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities. IFRS allows a liquidity-based presentation (no current/non-current split) when that is more relevant, as for banks.

### Measurement bases
- **Historical cost**: property and most inventory start at purchase cost, then are depreciated or written down.
- **Amortised cost**: debt held to collect contractual cash flows, carried at the effective-interest value.
- **Fair value**: the price that would be received in an orderly sale; used for trading securities and derivatives.

Intangibles deserve care. Identifiable intangibles bought separately or in an acquisition (patents, licences) are recognised; internally generated brands are generally not. **Goodwill** arises only in an acquisition: the price paid minus the fair value of net identifiable assets. It is not amortised but tested for impairment.

### Financial asset categories (IFRS view)
- **Amortised cost**: business model is to hold and collect principal and interest. Fair value changes are ignored.
- **Fair value through OCI (FVOCI)**: debt held to collect *and* sell, or equities irrevocably designated. Unrealised gains go to OCI.
- **Fair value through profit or loss (FVPL)**: everything else, including trading. Unrealised gains go straight to net income.

US GAAP uses similar labels for debt (held-to-maturity, available-for-sale, trading), while equity investments generally go through net income.

### Equity components
Equity includes contributed capital, retained earnings, treasury shares (a deduction), **accumulated other comprehensive income** and non-controlling interest (the share of consolidated subsidiaries owned by outsiders).

### Common-size analysis and ratios
A **common-size balance sheet** divides every line by total assets, removing size effects so that firms and years can be compared. Key ratios:

$$
\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
$$

$$
\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
$$

$$
\text{Debt-to-equity} = \frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{Total equity}}
$$

The quick ratio excludes inventory and prepaid items because they are the hardest current assets to turn into cash. Total debt means interest-bearing borrowings, not every liability.

## Worked example
Ashby Retail reports (USD millions): cash 40, receivables 110, inventory 150, net PP&E 500, goodwill 200; payables 120, short-term debt 80, accrued expenses 50, long-term debt 300, equity 450.

**Step 1 - Totals.** Total assets = 40 + 110 + 150 + 500 + 200 = 1,000. Current assets = 300; current liabilities = 120 + 80 + 50 = 250. Liabilities plus equity = 250 + 300 + 450 = 1,000, so the sheet balances.

**Step 2 - Common-size.** Divide each line by 1,000: cash 4%, receivables 11%, inventory 15%, PP&E 50%, goodwill 20%; payables 12%, short-term debt 8%, accrued 5%, long-term debt 30%, equity 45%. Goodwill at 20% of assets is a flag: one impairment could wipe out a large share of equity.

**Step 3 - Liquidity.** Current ratio = 300 / 250 = **1.20**. Quick ratio = (40 + 110) / 250 = **0.60**. Liquidity depends heavily on selling inventory.

**Step 4 - Solvency.** Total debt = 80 + 300 = 380, so debt-to-equity = 380 / 450 = **0.84**. Tangible equity = 450 - 200 = 250, which gives a much higher tangible leverage of 380 / 250 = 1.52.

## Common exam traps
- Forgetting that the current portion of long-term debt is a current liability.
- Including inventory in the quick ratio.
- Assuming book equity equals market value, or that internally developed brands appear as assets.
- Sending FVPL unrealised gains to OCI, or FVOCI gains to net income.
- Counting payables and accruals as "debt" in debt-to-equity.

## Key takeaways
- Current items are realised or settled within one year or the operating cycle, if longer.
- Balance sheets mix historical cost, amortised cost and fair value, so book value is not market value.
- Financial asset category decides whether fair value changes hit net income, OCI or nothing.
- Goodwill comes only from acquisitions and is tested for impairment, not amortised.
- Common-size statements and liquidity and leverage ratios make companies comparable.
