## Learning goals
- Describe the main participants in the foreign exchange market and the products they trade.
- Distinguish nominal from real exchange rates and calculate a change in the real exchange rate.
- Compare exchange rate regimes from hard pegs to free floats.
- Explain the types of capital flows, why governments impose capital controls, and the impossible trinity.

## Explanation

### What the FX market does
The foreign exchange market, the world's largest financial market, trades currencies around the clock. It exists because the previous lesson's trade flows must be paid for, and because investors move capital across borders to buy foreign stocks, bonds, companies and property. In practice, capital flows and hedging transactions far outweigh payments for goods.

### Participants
- **Sell side**: large dealer banks that quote two-way prices and make markets, plus smaller regional banks.
- **Buy side**: corporations paying for imports or hedging export revenue; real money investors such as pension funds and insurers; leveraged accounts such as hedge funds; retail traders; governments; central banks, which manage reserves and sometimes intervene; and sovereign wealth funds.

### Products
- **Spot**: exchange of currencies for settlement in two business days for most pairs.
- **Forward**: an agreement today to exchange at a fixed rate on a future date, used for hedging.
- **FX swap**: a spot trade combined with an offsetting forward, used to roll hedges and manage funding. Swaps make up the largest share of daily volume.
- **Futures and options**: exchange-traded standardised forwards, and the right without obligation to exchange.

### Quote notation
We write $S_{A/B}$ for the number of units of currency A per one unit of currency B. So $S_{USD/EUR} = 1.10$ means one euro costs 1.10 US dollars. If the quote rises, the base currency (B) has **appreciated**.

### Nominal versus real exchange rates
The nominal rate tells you how many units of currency you exchange. The **real** exchange rate tells you how many baskets of domestic goods you give up for a basket of foreign goods:

$$
R_{DC/FC} = S_{DC/FC} \times \frac{P_{FC}}{P_{DC}}
$$

where $S_{DC/FC}$ is the nominal rate in domestic currency per unit of foreign currency, $P_{FC}$ is the foreign price level and $P_{DC}$ is the domestic price level. A higher real rate means foreign goods have become more expensive in real terms, so domestic exporters gain competitiveness. The real rate rises when the foreign currency appreciates nominally, when foreign prices rise faster, or when domestic prices rise more slowly.

### Exchange rate regimes
From the most rigid to the most flexible:

1. **No separate legal tender**: dollarisation or a monetary union; no independent monetary policy.
2. **Currency board**: domestic currency is fully backed by a reserve currency at a fixed rate, by law.
3. **Fixed peg**: the central bank holds the rate within a narrow band, usually ±1%.
4. **Target zone**: a wider band.
5. **Crawling peg**: the peg is adjusted in small steps, often for inflation.
6. **Managed float**: the rate floats, but the authority intervenes to influence it.
7. **Independent float**: the market sets the rate.

### Capital flows
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** buys lasting control of businesses. It is long-term, brings technology and is hard to reverse.
- **Portfolio flows** buy stocks and bonds. They are liquid and can reverse quickly; short-term speculative flows are often called hot money.

Governments may impose **capital controls** to keep domestic savings at home, keep the exchange rate stable, preserve room for independent monetary policy, or protect strategic industries. The cost is a less efficient allocation of capital and reduced investor confidence.

### The impossible trinity
A country can have at most two of: a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility and independent monetary policy. A eurozone member has a fixed rate and free capital but no independent rate setting; a country with capital controls can peg and still set its own rates.

## Worked example
A US-based analyst tracks the euro. Over one year the spot rate moves from $S_{USD/EUR} = 1.10$ to 1.12. US prices rise 3% and euro-area prices rise 1%. Take both price indexes as 100 at the start, so they are 103 (US) and 101 (euro area) at year end.

**Step 1 - Nominal change.** $1.12/1.10 - 1 = 1.82\%$. The euro appreciated nominally against the dollar.

**Step 2 - Real exchange rate at the start.** $R = 1.10 \times 100/100 = 1.1000$.

**Step 3 - Real exchange rate at the end.**

$$
R = 1.12 \times \frac{101}{103} = 1.0983
$$

**Step 4 - Real change.** $1.0983/1.1000 - 1 = -0.16\%$.

**Interpretation.** Despite the dollar's 1.82% nominal depreciation, higher US inflation more than offset it. The **real USD/EUR rate fell by about 0.16%**, so US goods became very slightly less competitive against euro-area goods.

## Common exam traps
- Calling a rise in $S_{USD/EUR}$ a dollar appreciation. The quoted rate is dollars per euro; a rise means the euro appreciated.
- Putting the price levels the wrong way round. Foreign prices go in the numerator for $R_{DC/FC}$.
- Assuming a pegged currency can coexist with free capital flows and an independent policy rate. The impossible trinity rules that out.
- Treating FDI and portfolio flows as equally volatile. Portfolio flows reverse much faster.

## Key takeaways
- The FX market is dominated by capital flows and hedging, with FX swaps the largest product by volume.
- $S_{A/B}$ is units of A per unit of B; a higher quote means B has appreciated.
- Real exchange rate = nominal rate × foreign price level / domestic price level.
- Regimes range from no separate legal tender and currency boards to managed and independent floats.
- FDI is stable and long-term; portfolio flows are liquid and can reverse suddenly.
- The impossible trinity forces a choice of two among a fixed rate, open capital and independent monetary policy.
