## Learning goals
- Classify real estate investments by public versus private and debt versus equity.
- Describe the main property types and their risk characteristics.
- Calculate net operating income and value a property with the direct capitalisation method.
- Explain the income, cost and sales comparison approaches to property valuation.
- Describe the features, categories and risks of infrastructure investments.

## Explanation

### Why real assets?
Real estate and infrastructure are tangible, long-lived assets that produce rental or usage income. Investors hold them for steady cash flows, potential inflation protection (rents and tolls are often indexed) and diversification. The trade-offs are illiquidity, large lot sizes, high transaction costs and valuations that rely on appraisals.

### Forms of real estate investment
A useful map is a 2 × 2 grid:

| | Equity | Debt |
|---|---|---|
| **Private** | Direct ownership, private real estate funds | Mortgages, private construction loans |
| **Public** | Shares of REITs and real estate operating companies | Mortgage-backed securities, mortgage REITs |

**Real estate investment trusts (REITs)** are listed companies that own income-producing property and must distribute most of their taxable income, which typically lets them avoid tax at the corporate level. Public forms offer liquidity and small ticket sizes, but their prices move with the stock market in the short run. Private equity ownership gives control but is illiquid and concentrated. Debt investors receive contractual interest and have priority, but give up most of the upside.

### Property types
- **Residential**: apartments and single-family homes; demand driven by population and household formation.
- **Office**: sensitive to employment; long leases but heavy re-letting costs.
- **Retail**: shopping centres; rents may include a percentage of tenant sales.
- **Industrial and logistics**: warehouses and distribution; demand tied to trade and e-commerce.
- **Hospitality**: hotels; effectively daily leases, so the most cyclical.

Shorter lease terms mean income responds faster to the economy, which raises risk.

### Valuation approaches
1. **Income approach**: value the income stream. The **direct capitalisation** method divides a single year's net operating income by a capitalisation (cap) rate. A **discounted cash flow (DCF)** method projects several years of income plus a sale price and discounts them.
2. **Cost approach**: value equals the cost of buying the land and constructing an equivalent building today, less depreciation and obsolescence.
3. **Sales comparison approach**: value is based on recent sale prices of similar properties, adjusted for differences in size, age, location and condition.

$$
\text{NOI} = \text{Rental income} - \text{Vacancy and collection loss} + \text{Other income} - \text{Operating expenses}
$$

$$
V_0 = \frac{\text{NOI}_1}{\text{Cap rate}}, \qquad \text{Cap rate} \approx r - g
$$

where $V_0$ is today's value, $\text{NOI}_1$ is next year's NOI, $r$ is the investor's required return and $g$ is the expected constant growth rate of NOI. Operating expenses exclude financing costs (interest) and income taxes. Note the link to the constant-growth dividend model from equity valuation.

### Infrastructure
Infrastructure assets provide essential services: roads, airports, ports, pipelines, power grids, water systems, schools and hospitals. They are typically long-lived, capital-intensive and often monopolistic or regulated, which gives stable, inflation-linked cash flows.

- **Economic infrastructure** supports economic activity: transport, utilities, communications.
- **Social infrastructure** serves communities: education, healthcare, social housing, often paid for by the government through **availability payments**.
- **Greenfield** projects are new builds, with construction risk and higher expected returns. **Brownfield** projects are existing, operating assets with an income history and lower risk.

Infrastructure is accessed through direct investment, unlisted funds, listed infrastructure companies and public-private partnerships (PPPs). Key risks are construction and operating risk, regulatory and political risk (tariff changes), demand risk for usage-based revenue, and high leverage.

## Worked example
An office building has gross potential rent of USD 2,000,000 for the coming year. Expected vacancy and collection loss is 5%, other income (parking) is 50,000 and operating expenses are 600,000. Comparable buildings trade at a 6.5% cap rate.

1. Vacancy loss: $0.05 \times 2{,}000{,}000 = 100{,}000$.
2. Effective gross income: $2{,}000{,}000 - 100{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 1{,}950{,}000$.
3. NOI: $1{,}950{,}000 - 600{,}000 = 1{,}350{,}000$.
4. Value: $1{,}350{,}000 / 0.065 =$ **USD 20,769,231** (about 20.77 million).

**Extension**: suppose instead that 1,350,000 is this year's NOI, NOI grows at 2% a year and investors require 8.5%. Then $\text{NOI}_1 = 1{,}377{,}000$, the implied cap rate is $8.5\% - 2\% = 6.5\%$, and value is $1{,}377{,}000 / 0.065 = 21{,}184{,}615$. The higher figure reflects applying the cap rate to next year's grown NOI.

## Common exam traps
- Deducting interest expense or depreciation when computing NOI; NOI is before financing and taxes.
- Applying the cap rate to gross rent instead of NOI.
- Forgetting that a higher cap rate means a lower value (they are inversely related).
- Confusing greenfield (new construction, higher risk) with brownfield (existing, operating asset).
- Assuming REITs behave like direct property in the short run; their prices track equity markets closely.

## Key takeaways
- Real estate can be held as public or private, equity or debt; REITs are the main public equity route.
- Lease length drives cyclicality: hotels are most cyclical, long-leased offices least in the short run.
- Value = NOI divided by the cap rate; the cap rate approximates required return minus growth.
- The three valuation approaches are income, cost and sales comparison.
- Infrastructure offers stable, often inflation-linked cash flows; greenfield carries construction risk, brownfield does not.
