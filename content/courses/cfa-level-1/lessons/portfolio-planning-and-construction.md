## Learning goals
- Describe the purpose and main components of an investment policy statement (IPS).
- Distinguish ability to take risk from willingness to take risk and reconcile conflicts between them.
- Calculate a client's required return from spending, inflation and cost assumptions.
- Identify the five main investment constraints.
- Explain strategic asset allocation and how a candidate allocation is tested against the IPS.

## Explanation

### The investment policy statement
Earlier lessons built the machinery for choosing efficient portfolios. Planning decides which point on that machinery suits a particular client. The **IPS** is the written agreement between client and manager that records the client's objectives and constraints and the rules of the relationship. It protects both sides: it disciplines the manager, gives the client realistic expectations, and survives changes in staff. A typical IPS contains an introduction describing the client, a statement of purpose, the duties of each party, the investment objectives and constraints, review procedures (how often, against which benchmark), and rebalancing guidelines. Many also include an appendix with the strategic asset allocation.

### Objectives: return and risk
**Risk objectives** may be absolute (for example, "no more than a 10% loss in any year", or a maximum standard deviation) or relative to a benchmark (a tracking-error limit). Risk tolerance has two dimensions:

- **Ability to take risk** - an objective measure based on time horizon, wealth relative to needs, income stability and insurance. A 30-year-old with secure income and modest spending needs relative to assets has high ability.
- **Willingness to take risk** - a subjective, psychological measure revealed by questionnaires and past behaviour.

When the two disagree, the adviser generally follows the **lower** of the two. If willingness is below ability, the adviser may try to educate the client, but the final decision respects the client's comfort. If willingness exceeds ability, ability wins - a client cannot safely take risk they cannot afford.

**Return objectives** must be consistent with the risk objective. A required return often comes from spending needs:

$$
R_{\text{required}} = (1 + s)(1 + \pi)(1 + c) - 1
$$

where $s$ is the spending rate (annual withdrawal divided by portfolio value), $\pi$ is expected inflation and $c$ is the annual cost or fee rate. Adding the three rates gives a quick approximation that is slightly too low.

### Constraints
Remember them with the acronym **TTLLU**:
- **Time horizon** - can be single- or multi-stage (for example, working years then retirement).
- **Taxes** - affect asset location and choice of tax-efficient vehicles.
- **Liquidity** - planned and emergency cash needs.
- **Legal and regulatory** - rules on pension funds, insurers or trusts.
- **Unique circumstances** - ethical or ESG preferences, concentrated stock, restrictions on specific holdings.

ESG considerations can be implemented through negative screening (excluding sectors), positive or best-in-class screening, thematic investing, impact investing and engagement.

### Asset allocation and construction
The **strategic asset allocation (SAA)** is the long-term policy mix of asset classes that best meets the IPS given capital market expectations. Asset classes should be internally homogeneous, distinct from each other (low correlation), and large enough to absorb investment. The SAA usually comes with permitted ranges; deliberate short-term deviations within those ranges are **tactical asset allocation**. **Security selection** is choosing individual holdings within each class. A **risk budget** sets how much active risk each source may consume. Portfolio construction can be organised as a core of passive holdings plus active satellites.

## Worked example
Anika, 62, has a portfolio of USD 1,500,000 and needs USD 60,000 a year for living costs. Expected inflation is 2.5% and fees are 0.5% a year. Her IPS caps portfolio standard deviation at 14%. Capital market expectations: equities 9% return, 18% standard deviation; bonds 4% return, 6% standard deviation; correlation 0.20.

**Step 1 - spending rate:** $60{,}000 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 4.0\%$.

**Step 2 - required return:** $(1.04)(1.025)(1.005) - 1 = 7.13\%$. (Additive shortcut: 7.0%.)

**Step 3 - test three mixes.**

| Equity / Bonds | Expected return | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| 60 / 40 | 7.0% | 11.52% |
| 70 / 30 | 7.5% | 13.08% |
| 80 / 20 | 8.0% | 14.69% |

For 70/30: return $0.7(9) + 0.3(4) = 7.5\%$; variance $0.49(0.0324) + 0.09(0.0036) + 2(0.7)(0.3)(0.2)(0.18)(0.06) = 0.017107$, so σ = 13.08%.

**Step 4 - decide.** 60/40 misses the 7.13% return target; 80/20 breaches the 14% risk cap. **The 70/30 allocation is the only one that meets both objectives.**

## Common exam traps
- Following the higher of ability and willingness - the adviser follows the lower.
- Adding spending, inflation and fees when the question asks for the precise (compounded) required return.
- Listing "return" as a constraint - return and risk are objectives; TTLLU are constraints.
- Treating ESG exclusions as a legal constraint when they are usually a unique circumstance.
- Confusing tactical allocation (temporary deviation within ranges) with a change in the strategic allocation.

## Key takeaways
- The IPS documents objectives, constraints and the rules of the client-manager relationship.
- Risk tolerance combines ability and willingness; use the lower when they conflict.
- Required return compounds spending, inflation and costs.
- Constraints: time horizon, taxes, liquidity, legal/regulatory, unique circumstances.
- The strategic asset allocation is chosen to meet return and risk objectives simultaneously.
