title: "The Firm and Market Structures"

## Learning goals
- Distinguish perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly by their key features.
- Apply the profit-maximising rule MR = MC in each structure.
- Determine breakeven and shutdown points using average total cost and average variable cost.
- Calculate the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index and a concentration ratio, and explain their limits.

## Explanation

### Why market structure matters
An analyst forecasting a company's margins needs to know how much pricing power it has. That depends mostly on the structure of its market: how many sellers there are, how similar their products are, and how hard it is for new firms to enter.

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of sellers | Many | Many | Few | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Entry barriers | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Pricing power | None | Some | Significant, interdependent | Considerable |
| Non-price competition | None | Heavy (advertising, branding) | Some | Some |

### The universal rule: MR = MC
Every firm, in every structure, maximises profit by producing where marginal revenue (MR) equals marginal cost (MC). What differs is the shape of demand facing the firm.

- In **perfect competition** the firm is a price taker. Its demand curve is horizontal at the market price, so $P = MR$, and it produces where $P = MC$. In the long run, entry and exit push economic profit to zero: $P = \min ATC$.
- In **monopolistic competition** each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve because its product is a little different. Short-run profits are possible, but low barriers let rivals enter until economic profit is zero in the long run. Price stays above MC, so there is excess capacity.
- In **oligopoly** firms are interdependent: each must guess how rivals will react. Models include the kinked demand curve (rivals match price cuts but not increases, making prices sticky), Cournot (firms choose quantities), Stackelberg (a leader moves first) and Nash equilibrium (no firm gains by changing strategy alone). Collusion, such as a cartel, can raise profits but is unstable because each member is tempted to cheat.
- A **monopoly** faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve. Because it must cut price on all units to sell one more, $MR < P$. It restricts output and charges more than a competitive market would, creating a deadweight loss.

For a linear demand curve $P = a - bQ$, revenue is $aQ - bQ^2$, so

$$
MR = a - 2bQ
$$

MR has the same intercept as demand and twice the slope.

### Breakeven and shutdown
Let ATC be average total cost and AVC be average variable cost.

- **Breakeven:** $P = ATC$. Economic profit is zero.
- **Short run:** if $AVC \le P < ATC$, the firm loses money but should keep operating, because revenue covers all variable cost and part of fixed cost. If $P < AVC$, it should shut down: operating would add variable-cost losses on top of the fixed costs it pays anyway.
- **Long run:** all costs are variable, so the firm exits if $P < ATC$.

### Measuring concentration
The **N-firm concentration ratio** sums the market shares of the largest N firms. The **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index** sums squared shares:

$$
HHI = \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i^2
$$

where $s_i$ is firm $i$'s market share in percent. A pure monopoly scores 10,000. Squaring gives more weight to large firms, so the HHI reacts to a merger between big players, while a concentration ratio may not change at all if the merging firms are both already in the top N. Neither measure captures barriers to entry or potential competition, so a concentrated market with easy entry may still behave competitively.

## Worked example
**Part A - Monopoly pricing.** A monopolist faces demand $P = 100 - 2Q$ and has constant marginal cost of 20 per unit, with no fixed costs.

1. Marginal revenue: $MR = 100 - 4Q$.
2. Set $MR = MC$: $100 - 4Q = 20$, so $Q = 20$.
3. Price from the demand curve: $P = 100 - 2(20) = 60$.
4. Profit: $(60 - 20) \times 20 = 800$.
5. Competitive benchmark: $P = MC = 20$ gives $100 - 2Q = 20$, so $Q = 40$.
6. Deadweight loss (the triangle between demand and MC from Q = 20 to 40): $0.5 \times (40 - 20) \times (60 - 20) = 400$.

The monopolist produces **20 units at a price of 60**, half the competitive output.

**Part B - Shutdown decision.** A competitive firm produces 1,000 units. The market price is 12, ATC is 15 and AVC is 10.

- Operate: revenue 12,000 minus total cost 15,000 gives a loss of 3,000.
- Shut down: the firm still pays fixed cost of $(15 - 10) \times 1{,}000 = 5{,}000$.

Since 3,000 < 5,000, the firm should **keep operating in the short run**, but it will exit in the long run unless price rises to at least 15.

**Part C - HHI.** Four firms have shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%. $HHI = 1{,}600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3{,}000$. If the 20% and 10% firms merge, $HHI = 1{,}600 + 900 + 900 = $ **3,400**, while the three-firm concentration ratio rises only from 90% to 100%.

## Common exam traps
- Using $P = MC$ for a monopolist. The monopolist sets $MR = MC$ and then reads price off the demand curve.
- Shutting down whenever $P < ATC$. In the short run the test is $P < AVC$.
- Forgetting that monopolistic competition earns zero economic profit in the long run, just like perfect competition.
- Entering market shares as decimals in the HHI (0.4 squared) and then comparing the result with thresholds quoted on the 10,000 scale.

## Key takeaways
- Market structure is defined by number of sellers, product differentiation, barriers to entry and pricing power.
- All firms maximise profit where MR = MC; only price-takers have P = MR.
- Breakeven is P = ATC; short-run shutdown is P < AVC; long-run exit is P < ATC.
- A monopolist restricts output, raises price and creates a deadweight loss.
- Oligopolists are interdependent, and cartels tend to be unstable.
- HHI weights large firms more heavily than a concentration ratio, but neither captures entry barriers.
