---
title: "Standard IV: Duties to Employers"
---

## Learning goals
- Explain the duty of loyalty to an employer, including what is allowed when preparing to leave.
- Distinguish permissible preparation from solicitation and misappropriation of employer property.
- Determine when written consent is needed for additional compensation arrangements.
- Describe the responsibilities of supervisors to prevent and detect violations.

## Explanation
Employers invest in their staff and trust them with clients, information and systems. Standard IV protects that relationship - but never at the expense of clients. Remember the ordering from earlier lessons: clients first, employer second, self last.

### A. Loyalty
In matters related to your employment, act for the benefit of your employer and do not deprive it of the advantage of your skills and abilities, divulge confidential information, or otherwise cause it harm.

**Independent practice.** If you plan to do work that competes with your employer - for example, managing money for private clients on the side - you must notify your employer, describe the services and compensation, and obtain consent before starting.

**Leaving an employer.** You may make arrangements to leave - registering a new business name, leasing an office, interviewing - as long as you do so outside working hours and do not breach your duty while still employed. Before you leave you must **not**:
- solicit current clients or prospects to follow you,
- take client lists, models, research or other records (paper or electronic copies),
- misuse confidential information, or
- encourage colleagues to leave en masse in breach of duty.

After you leave, you may contact former clients **unless** a valid non-compete or non-solicitation agreement prevents it, and you may use **knowledge and skills in your head** and public information (such as directories). Records stay with the employer, even ones you created.

**Whistleblowing.** Loyalty to an employer does not override duties to clients or the integrity of markets. Reporting genuine illegal or unethical conduct is not disloyalty, provided it is done in good faith and not for personal gain.

**Nature of employment.** The standard applies to employees and, by extension, to contractors and others in a trust relationship with a firm, regardless of whether the work is full-time or paid.

### B. Additional compensation arrangements
Do not accept gifts, benefits, compensation or consideration that competes with, or might reasonably be expected to create a conflict with, your employer's interest unless you obtain **written consent from all parties involved**. In practice this means getting written consent from your employer **before** accepting, and disclosing the nature, amount and duration of the arrangement.

A common case: a client offers a bonus tied to portfolio performance. Because it could encourage favouring that client over others, you need your employer's written consent. Contrast this with a modest gift from a client for past service, which only needs disclosure to the employer under the independence and objectivity standard.

### C. Responsibilities of supervisors
If you supervise others, make reasonable efforts to ensure they comply with laws, rules, regulations and the Code and Standards. You may delegate tasks, but not the responsibility.

- Ensure **adequate compliance procedures** exist and are enforced. If they are inadequate, decline supervisory responsibility in writing until the firm adopts adequate procedures.
- If you suspect a violation, **investigate promptly**, take steps to stop it (such as limiting the employee's activities or increasing monitoring), and do not rely solely on the employee's assurance that it will not happen again.
- The duty covers everyone a member supervises, whether or not those staff are CFA Institute members or candidates themselves.

## Worked example
**Scenario.** Carmen Ruiz, CFA, is a portfolio manager at the fictional Oakhurst Investments. She has decided to start her own advisory firm.

**Week 1.** In the evenings, Carmen registers a company name, signs an office lease and builds a website that is not yet live. *Assessment:* permissible preparation outside working hours.

**Week 2.** While still employed, she emails ten Oakhurst clients saying she is leaving and invites them to move with her. *Assessment:* violation - solicitation of employer's clients before departure.

**Week 3.** On her last day, she copies her client contact spreadsheet to a USB stick. *Assessment:* violation - the records belong to Oakhurst, even though she compiled them.

**After leaving.** With no non-compete in place, she contacts former clients using names she remembers and public directories. *Assessment:* permissible.

Separately, while at Oakhurst, a wealthy client offered Carmen a personal bonus of 1% of any gains above 10% per year. *Assessment:* she needed written consent from Oakhurst before accepting.

**Conclusion: Carmen's week 1 preparation and post-departure contacts are acceptable; soliciting before leaving, copying client records and taking an unapproved performance bonus are violations.**

## Common exam traps
- Thinking you cannot prepare to leave at all. Preparation outside work hours is allowed; solicitation and taking records are not.
- Assuming records you created are yours. Firm records remain firm property.
- Believing verbal permission suffices for additional compensation. Written consent is required, obtained before acceptance.
- Assuming a supervisor's duty ends after warning an employee. Supervisors must investigate and take action to prevent recurrence.
- Treating whistleblowing on genuine misconduct as a loyalty violation.

## Key takeaways
- Loyalty to the employer ranks below duties to clients and markets.
- Prepare to leave on your own time; do not solicit or take records before you go.
- Independent competing practice needs notice and consent.
- Additional compensation that may create a conflict requires prior written consent.
- Supervisors must have adequate compliance systems, act promptly on suspected violations and decline responsibility in writing if procedures are inadequate.
