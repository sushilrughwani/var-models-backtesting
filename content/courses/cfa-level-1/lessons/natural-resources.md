## Learning goals
- Describe the main commodity sectors and the ways investors gain commodity exposure.
- Explain contango and backwardation and why the futures curve can slope either way.
- Decompose a commodity futures return into spot, roll and collateral components.
- Compare the return drivers and risks of farmland and timberland.
- Evaluate the role of natural resources as diversifiers and inflation hedges.

## Explanation

### What counts as natural resources?
Natural resources include **commodities** (raw materials that are traded in standardised form) and **land-based assets** such as farmland and timberland. Commodities fall into broad sectors: energy (crude oil, natural gas), industrial metals (copper, aluminium), precious metals (gold, silver), grains (wheat, corn, soybeans), livestock (cattle, hogs) and softs (coffee, cocoa, sugar).

Commodities themselves produce no cash flow. A barrel of oil pays no dividend and costs money to store and insure. So most investors gain exposure through **futures contracts** rather than physical holdings, via commodity index funds, exchange-traded products or managed futures funds. A less direct route is buying shares of producers such as miners or oil companies, but those shares also carry equity market and company-specific risk.

### The futures curve
A futures price reflects the spot price plus the cost of carrying the commodity until delivery (storage, insurance and financing) minus the **convenience yield**, the benefit of physically holding the commodity when supplies are tight.

- **Contango**: futures prices are *above* the spot price and longer-dated contracts cost more. Typical when inventories are ample and storage costs dominate.
- **Backwardation**: futures prices are *below* the spot price. Typical when supplies are scarce, so holding the physical good today carries a high convenience yield.

Several theories explain the curve's shape. The **insurance theory** (normal backwardation) says producers hedge by selling futures and pay speculators a premium, pushing futures below expected spot prices. The **hedging pressure** view extends this: whether producers or consumers dominate hedging decides the slope. The **theory of storage** focuses on inventories and convenience yield.

### Sources of futures returns
A long-only futures investor never takes delivery. Before each contract expires, the investor sells it and buys a longer-dated one, called **rolling**. The total return has three parts:

$$
R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{spot}} + R_{\text{roll}} + R_{\text{collateral}}
$$

$$
R_{\text{roll}} = \frac{F_{\text{near}} - F_{\text{far}}}{F_{\text{near}}} \times \text{fraction of position rolled}
$$

where $R_{\text{spot}}$ is the percentage change in the spot price, $F_{\text{near}}$ is the price of the expiring contract sold, $F_{\text{far}}$ is the price of the new contract bought, and $R_{\text{collateral}}$ is the interest earned on the cash held as margin collateral (futures are fully collateralised in index products). In backwardation the investor sells high and buys low, so roll return is positive; in contango it is negative.

### Farmland and timberland
Both are real land assets with a biological return component.

**Farmland** returns come from crop income (or rent paid by a farmer) plus changes in land value. Harvests must happen when crops mature, so production cannot be delayed to wait for better prices. Risks include weather, crop disease and commodity price swings.

**Timberland** returns come from three sources: **biological growth** of the trees, changes in timber prices, and changes in land value. A key advantage is flexibility: trees can be left standing ("stored on the stump") and keep growing when prices are low, then harvested when prices recover. Risks include fire, pests, storms and long investment horizons.

Both assets are illiquid, have high minimum investments and are valued by appraisal, but they have shown low correlation with stocks and bonds and some protection against inflation. They can also support sustainability goals, such as carbon sequestration in managed forests.

### Commodities in a portfolio
Commodity prices often rise with unexpected inflation and supply shocks, when stocks and bonds may struggle, so commodities can diversify a traditional portfolio. However, they are very volatile and, without a positive roll return, long-run returns may be modest.

## Worked example
An investor holds a fully collateralised long position in crude oil futures for one year and rolls the entire position once, just before expiry.

- Spot price: 80.00 at the start, 84.00 at the end.
- At the roll date, the expiring contract trades at 84.00 and the next contract at 82.32 (backwardation).
- Collateral earns 4% for the year.

1. Spot return: $(84.00 - 80.00) / 80.00 = 5.00\%$.
2. Roll return: $(84.00 - 82.32) / 84.00 \times 100\% = 2.00\%$.
3. Collateral return: $4.00\%$.
4. Total return: $5.00\% + 2.00\% + 4.00\% =$ **11.00%**.

**Contango contrast**: if the spot price fell from 60.00 to 57.00 (−5%), the expiring contract were at 57.00 and the new one at 58.71, roll return would be $(57.00 - 58.71) / 57.00 = -3.00\%$. With 3% collateral, total return is $-5\% - 3\% + 3\% = -5.00\%$: the negative roll wipes out the collateral income.

## Common exam traps
- Mixing up contango (futures above spot, negative roll) and backwardation (futures below spot, positive roll).
- Forgetting collateral return; index returns assume the full notional is held in cash or T-bills.
- Dividing the roll difference by the far contract when the formula uses the near contract.
- Saying timberland must be harvested on schedule; that inflexibility applies to farmland crops, not trees.
- Treating producer equities as pure commodity exposure; they also carry equity and company risk.

## Key takeaways
- Commodities produce no income, so investors usually use fully collateralised futures.
- Futures curves are in contango when storage costs dominate and backwardation when convenience yield is high.
- Total futures return = spot return + roll return + collateral return.
- Roll return is positive in backwardation and negative in contango.
- Farmland earns crop income and land appreciation; timberland adds biological growth and harvest flexibility.
