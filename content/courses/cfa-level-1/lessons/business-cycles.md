title: "Understanding Business Cycles"

## Learning goals
- Describe the phases of the business cycle and how output, employment, inflation and inventories behave in each.
- Explain how credit cycles form and why they amplify the business cycle.
- Distinguish leading, coincident and lagging economic indicators and use them to judge where the economy stands.
- Calculate the unemployment rate and participation rate, and explain why the unemployment rate lags the cycle.

## Explanation

### What a business cycle is
A business cycle is a recurring but irregular swing in aggregate economic activity around its long-run trend. "Recurring" means expansions and contractions keep happening; "irregular" means no two cycles have the same length or depth. The useful anchor is the **output gap**: actual real GDP minus potential (trend) GDP. A positive gap means the economy is running hot; a negative gap means spare capacity.

### The four phases
A convenient framework splits the cycle into four phases defined by the level and direction of activity relative to trend.

1. **Recovery** - activity is below trend but rising after the trough. Firms first get more out of existing staff (longer hours, overtime), then begin rehiring. Inventories have been run down, so restocking boosts production. Inflation is usually still falling or low.
2. **Expansion** - activity is above trend and rising. Hiring accelerates, consumer spending on durables and business investment pick up, and inflation starts to rise as spare capacity disappears.
3. **Slowdown** - activity is still above trend but growth is fading after the peak. Inflation often peaks late. Unsold goods build up and the inventory-to-sales ratio rises.
4. **Contraction** - activity is below trend and falling. Firms first cut hours, then lay off workers; investment and durable purchases are slashed. A severe and prolonged contraction is a depression.

Two patterns recur on the exam. **Inventories** amplify the cycle: firms over-produce at the top, then cut output sharply to clear stock. **Labour** lags: firms are slow to fire at the start of a downturn (hiring is costly) and slow to hire at the start of a recovery (they wait for confirmation). That is why the unemployment rate often keeps rising after the trough.

### Credit cycles
Credit cycles are swings in the availability and cost of borrowing. In good times lenders loosen standards, collateral values (especially property) rise, and that higher collateral supports even more lending. In bad times the process reverses: defaults rise, collateral falls in value, and lenders tighten. Credit cycles tend to be longer and deeper than business cycles, and when a credit contraction coincides with an economic contraction the recession is usually more severe and recovery slower.

### Economic indicators
Indicators help an analyst locate the economy in its cycle.

- **Leading indicators** turn before the economy: building permits, new orders for capital goods, average weekly hours in manufacturing, initial jobless claims, stock prices, the yield curve spread, and consumer expectations.
- **Coincident indicators** move with the economy: industrial production, real personal income, manufacturing and trade sales, and payroll employment.
- **Lagging indicators** turn after the economy: average duration of unemployment, the inventory-to-sales ratio, unit labour costs, the bank prime rate and outstanding commercial loans.

Composite indexes combine several indicators to reduce noise. A **diffusion index** shows the share of components that are rising, which measures how broad a move is.

### Measuring unemployment
The **labour force** is people who are working plus people actively looking for work. Then

$$
\text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour force}} \qquad \text{Participation rate} = \frac{\text{Labour force}}{\text{Working-age population}}
$$

**Discouraged workers** have stopped looking, so they drop out of the labour force entirely. In a deep recession this can make the unemployment rate look better than reality. Unemployment is also classified as **frictional** (between jobs), **structural** (skills or location mismatch) and **cyclical** (caused by weak demand in a downturn).

## Worked example
A country has a working-age population of 200 million. The labour force is 130 million, of whom 6.5 million are unemployed.

**Step 1 - Unemployment rate.** $6.5 / 130 = 0.05$, or 5.0%.

**Step 2 - Participation rate.** $130 / 200 = 0.65$, or 65.0%.

**Step 3 - Discouraged workers.** During a downturn, 1 million unemployed people give up searching. They leave the labour force, so the labour force becomes 129 million and the unemployed count becomes 5.5 million.

$$
\frac{5.5}{129} = 0.0426
$$

The unemployment rate **falls to about 4.26%** even though not one extra person found a job, and participation drops to $129/200 = 64.5\%$.

**Step 4 - Reading the cycle.** Suppose at the same time building permits and new orders have risen for three months, industrial production is flat, and the inventory-to-sales ratio is still elevated. Leading indicators are turning up, coincident indicators have stabilised, and a lagging indicator still reflects the past downturn. The best reading is that the economy is near the **trough, moving into recovery**.

## Common exam traps
- Treating a falling unemployment rate as proof of a strengthening labour market; discouraged workers leaving the labour force can produce the same result.
- Classifying the inventory-to-sales ratio as leading. It is lagging: it rises after sales have already weakened.
- Expecting inflation to peak at the same time as output. Inflation typically peaks late, during the slowdown.
- Assuming firms hire as soon as the recovery begins. They first increase hours for existing workers.

## Key takeaways
- The cycle has four phases: recovery, expansion, slowdown and contraction, defined relative to trend output.
- Inventory adjustments amplify the cycle, and employment changes lag it.
- Credit cycles are typically longer than business cycles and deepen recessions that coincide with credit contractions.
- Leading indicators turn first, coincident indicators confirm, lagging indicators turn last.
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force; discouraged workers can make it understate weakness.
