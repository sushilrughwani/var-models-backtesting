## Learning goals
- Explain the principal-agent problem and the main forms it takes between shareholders, managers, directors and creditors.
- Describe board structure, board committees and shareholder mechanisms, including straight and cumulative voting.
- Calculate the number of shares needed to guarantee board seats under cumulative voting.
- Evaluate the risks of weak governance and the benefits of strong governance.
- Explain how environmental, social and governance (ESG) factors enter investment analysis.

## Explanation

### The principal-agent problem
A **principal** hires an **agent** to act on their behalf. Because the agent has different goals and better information, the agent may not act in the principal's best interest. In a corporation, shareholders (principals) delegate to directors and managers (agents). Typical symptoms:

- **Insufficient effort** and perks consumption.
- **Empire building**: growing the firm for prestige rather than value.
- **Entrenchment**: resisting takeovers or board changes that would remove them.
- **Excessive or poorly designed pay**, such as bonuses tied to short-term earnings.
- **Information asymmetry**: managers know more than owners and can shade disclosures.

Other agency-style conflicts include controlling versus minority shareholders (related-party transactions, unequal voting rights through dual-class shares) and shareholders versus creditors (risk shifting, covered in the previous lesson).

### Governance mechanisms
**The board of directors.** The board appoints, monitors and if necessary replaces the CEO, approves strategy and oversees risk and reporting. A **one-tier** board mixes executive and non-executive directors; a **two-tier** board separates a supervisory board (non-executives) from a management board. Good practice emphasises **independent directors**, separation of the CEO and chair roles, and regular director elections. A **staggered (classified) board**, where only a fraction of seats is elected each year, makes it harder to replace the board and can entrench management.

**Board committees.** The **audit committee** oversees financial reporting and auditors; the **remuneration (compensation) committee** designs executive pay; the **nomination committee** recruits directors; risk and ESG committees are increasingly common. Key committees should be composed mainly or entirely of independent directors.

**Shareholder mechanisms.** Shareholders vote at general meetings on directors, auditors, major transactions and often on executive pay ("say on pay"). They can submit proposals, use proxy voting, launch activist campaigns and, in the end, sell their shares or support a takeover.

**Creditor, employee and external mechanisms.** Covenants and collateral protect lenders. Employment contracts and labour law protect workers. Auditors, regulators, the media and the market for corporate control add external discipline.

### Straight versus cumulative voting
Under **straight voting**, each share carries one vote per seat and each seat is decided separately, so a majority holder elects every director. Under **cumulative voting**, a shareholder may pool all votes (shares × seats) on one or a few candidates, giving minority holders a chance at representation. The number of shares needed to guarantee $n$ seats is

$$
\text{Shares needed} = \frac{n \times S}{D + 1} + 1
$$

where $S$ is total shares voting, $D$ is the number of seats being elected and $n$ is the seats the holder wants. Round the fraction down before adding one.

### Risks and benefits
Weak governance creates **legal, regulatory and reputational risk** (fraud, fines, lawsuits), **poor decision-making** (value-destroying acquisitions, excessive risk), weak **internal controls** and misstated accounts, and ultimately a higher cost of capital. Strong governance improves **operational efficiency**, **control** and **financial reporting quality**, and it tends to lower the firm's cost of debt and equity because investors demand less compensation for agency risk.

### ESG considerations
ESG investing incorporates environmental (emissions, climate risk, waste, resource use), social (labour practices, product safety, data privacy, community relations) and governance factors into analysis. The emphasis is on **material** factors: issues that can affect cash flows, risk or the cost of capital. Examples include **stranded assets** (fossil-fuel reserves that may never be produced), fines for pollution, and reputational damage from supply-chain abuses. Approaches range from **negative screening** (excluding sectors) to **ESG integration** (adjusting forecasts and discount rates), **thematic investing** and **impact investing**. Data inconsistency and "greenwashing" remain challenges.

## Worked example
Harbor Corp has 1,000,000 voting shares and is electing 5 directors. A minority investor, Priya, owns 300,000 shares; the remaining 700,000 are held by a founder bloc.

**Step 1 - Straight voting.** Each seat is a separate contest. The founder bloc casts 700,000 votes per seat against Priya's 300,000, so Priya elects **0 directors**.

**Step 2 - Cumulative voting, total votes.** Priya has 300,000 × 5 = 1,500,000 votes; all shareholders together have 1,000,000 × 5 = 5,000,000.

**Step 3 - Shares needed for one seat.**

$$
\frac{1 \times 1{,}000{,}000}{5 + 1} + 1 = 166{,}666.67 + 1 \rightarrow 166{,}667
$$

**Step 4 - Shares needed for two seats.**

$$
\frac{2 \times 1{,}000{,}000}{6} + 1 = 333{,}333.33 + 1 \rightarrow 333{,}334
$$

**Step 5 - Conclusion.** Priya's 300,000 shares exceed the 166,667 needed for one seat but fall short of the 333,334 needed for two. Under cumulative voting she can guarantee **1 director** by concentrating all 1,500,000 votes on a single candidate.

## Common exam traps
- Dividing by $D$ instead of $D+1$ in the cumulative voting formula.
- Assuming independent directors eliminate agency problems; independence helps oversight but does not guarantee it.
- Treating a staggered board as a shareholder-friendly feature; it usually entrenches incumbents.
- Thinking ESG analysis means excluding industries. Integration of material factors into valuation is the broader approach.

## Key takeaways
- Agency problems arise because agents have different goals and more information than principals.
- Boards, independent committees, shareholder voting, covenants and external markets are the main governance mechanisms.
- Cumulative voting lets minority holders win seats: shares needed = $nS/(D+1) + 1$.
- Weak governance raises legal, reputational and financial risk; strong governance can lower the cost of capital.
- ESG analysis focuses on material environmental, social and governance factors that affect value and risk.
