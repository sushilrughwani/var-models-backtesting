## Learning goals
- Explain how industries are classified and the strengths and limits of each approach.
- Calculate the N-firm concentration ratio and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index and interpret them.
- Apply the five competitive forces to judge an industry's profit potential.
- Describe the stages of the industry life cycle and their typical financial characteristics.
- Distinguish cost leadership, differentiation and focus strategies.

## Explanation

### Why industry analysis matters
A company's results depend heavily on the economics of the industry it competes in. A mediocre airline and an excellent airline both face fierce price competition and high fixed costs; a mediocre software firm may still enjoy high margins. Industry analysis tells you how much profit is available to share, and competitive analysis tells you how much of it this company is likely to capture. Together they turn the historical picture from company analysis into a view about the future.

### Classifying industries
Commercial classification systems group companies by their **principal business activity**, usually the segment producing the most revenue, in a hierarchy from broad sector down to sub-industry. Firms can also be grouped by **sensitivity to the business cycle**: cyclical firms (autos, construction, luxury goods) see earnings swing with the economy, while non-cyclical or defensive firms (utilities, food staples, healthcare) are steadier. Statistical approaches cluster firms whose returns move together, but such groupings can shift over time and may lump unrelated firms. The aim is to find a **peer group**: firms with similar business activities, customers and economic drivers.

### Measuring concentration
Two common measures use market shares:

$$
CR_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i, \qquad HHI = \sum_{i=1}^{M} s_i^2
$$

where $s_i$ is firm $i$'s market share in percent, $N$ is the number of largest firms included and $M$ is the total number of firms. The concentration ratio ignores how share is split among the top firms; the HHI squares shares, so large firms get extra weight. An HHI near 10,000 means monopoly; very low values mean fragmentation. Dividing 10,000 by the HHI gives the "equivalent number" of equal-sized firms. When two firms with shares $a$ and $b$ merge, the HHI rises by $2ab$.

Concentration hints at pricing power but is not proof: a concentrated industry with a commodity product and high fixed costs can still be brutally competitive.

### Five competitive forces
Industry profitability is shaped by:
1. **Threat of new entrants:** high barriers (scale, regulation, brand, network effects, switching costs) protect profits.
2. **Rivalry among existing competitors:** slow growth, high fixed costs, undifferentiated products and high exit barriers intensify price wars.
3. **Bargaining power of suppliers:** concentrated suppliers of critical inputs squeeze margins.
4. **Bargaining power of buyers:** concentrated or price-sensitive buyers with low switching costs push prices down.
5. **Threat of substitutes:** products from other industries that do the same job cap prices.

### The industry life cycle
- **Embryonic:** slow growth, high prices, high risk and heavy investment.
- **Growth:** rapidly rising demand, improving profitability, limited price competition because demand is plentiful.
- **Shakeout:** growth slows, overcapacity appears, price competition intensifies and weaker firms fail or merge.
- **Mature:** little growth, consolidated industry, strong cash flow; firms compete for share.
- **Decline:** demand shrinks, excess capacity, price cutting and exits.

Shocks such as new technology can restart or shorten the cycle.

### Competitive strategy
Firms win by **cost leadership** (lowest cost, competing on price), **differentiation** (unique features customers pay more for) or a **focus** on a narrow niche with either approach. A strategy matters only if it is defensible; look for durable advantages such as scale, patents, brands or network effects.

## Worked example
An industry has five firms with market shares of 35%, 25%, 20%, 12% and 8%.

1. Four-firm concentration ratio: $35 + 25 + 20 + 12 = 92\%$.
2. HHI: $35^2 + 25^2 + 20^2 + 12^2 + 8^2 = 1{,}225 + 625 + 400 + 144 + 64 = 2{,}458$.
3. Equivalent number of equal-sized firms: $10{,}000/2{,}458 = 4.07$.
4. The two smallest firms propose to merge. HHI change $= 2 \times 12 \times 8 = 192$, so the new HHI is **2,650**. The four-firm ratio becomes $35 + 25 + 20 + 20 = 100\%$.

Interpretation: the industry was already highly concentrated, and the merger raises concentration further. The analyst should then ask the five-forces questions: are the products differentiated, is demand growing, and do buyers have alternatives? Concentration alone does not settle whether firms can sustain high margins.

## Common exam traps
- Writing shares as decimals in the HHI (0.35² …) and comparing the result with thresholds quoted on the 0-10,000 scale.
- Assuming high concentration always means high profits; commodity products and high fixed costs can still drive price wars.
- Confusing the shakeout stage (slowing growth, overcapacity, price cuts) with the mature stage (low growth, consolidated, stable).
- Treating substitutes as rivals within the industry; substitutes come from other industries.
- Classifying a firm by its smallest segment; classification uses the principal business activity.

## Key takeaways
- Industry economics set the profit pool; competitive position sets each firm's share of it.
- Classify by principal activity or cyclicality to build a meaningful peer group.
- $CR_N$ adds the top N shares; HHI sums squared shares and rises by $2ab$ when firms with shares $a$ and $b$ merge.
- The five forces (entry, rivalry, suppliers, buyers, substitutes) determine sustainable profitability.
- Life-cycle stage shapes growth, pricing, investment needs and cash flow.
