## Learning goals
- Explain what each primary financial statement tells an analyst and how the statements connect.
- Describe the role of the notes, management commentary and other disclosures beyond the statements themselves.
- Distinguish the types of audit opinion and explain what an audit does and does not guarantee.
- Apply a step-by-step framework to structure any financial analysis assignment.

## Explanation

### Why analysts read financial statements
A company's financial statements are the most detailed, standardised and legally accountable description of its economic life. Equity analysts use them to value shares, credit analysts to judge repayment, and suppliers and regulators to decide whether to keep dealing with the firm. The common thread is that every user wants to understand **past performance**, **current financial position** and the **ability to generate cash in the future**.

### The four core statements
Think of the statements as four camera angles on the same business:

- **Balance sheet (statement of financial position)**: a snapshot at one date of what the company controls (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the residual belonging to owners (equity).
- **Income statement (statement of profit or loss)**: a video over a period showing revenues, expenses and the resulting profit. Many companies also present **other comprehensive income (OCI)**, which captures certain gains and losses that bypass profit, together forming *total comprehensive income*.
- **Statement of changes in equity**: explains how each equity account moved from the start to the end of the period.
- **Statement of cash flows**: shows actual cash coming in and going out, split into operating, investing and financing activities.

They are tied together by two identities:

$$
\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}
$$

$$
\text{Ending equity} = \text{Beginning equity} + \text{Net income} + \text{OCI} - \text{Dividends} + \text{Shares issued} - \text{Shares repurchased}
$$

Profit is not cash: accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, so the cash flow statement reconciles the two.

### Beyond the face of the statements
The headline numbers are only the start. The **notes** explain the accounting policies chosen (for example, the inventory method or depreciation lives), the key estimates and judgements, segment results, commitments, contingencies and fair value measurements. **Management commentary** (often called MD&A) discusses results, trends, risks and capital resources from management's point of view; it is useful but unaudited and naturally optimistic. Other sources include interim reports, earnings calls and industry data.

### The audit report
An independent auditor gives **reasonable assurance**, not absolute certainty, that the statements are free of material misstatement and comply with the reporting framework (IFRS or US GAAP). The opinion types are:

- **Unqualified (clean)**: statements are fairly presented.
- **Qualified**: fairly presented *except for* a specific, contained issue.
- **Adverse**: statements are materially misstated and not fairly presented.
- **Disclaimer**: the auditor could not obtain enough evidence to form an opinion.

Modern reports also describe **key audit matters** (areas of highest audit judgement) and may flag material uncertainty about the firm's ability to continue as a **going concern**.

### A framework for any analysis
1. **Articulate the purpose and context**: who needs the answer, what decision it supports, and by when.
2. **Collect data**: statements, notes, industry and economic information, management discussions.
3. **Process data**: adjust for accounting differences, build common-size statements, compute ratios.
4. **Analyse and interpret**: explain *why* the numbers look the way they do.
5. **Develop and communicate conclusions**: a report or recommendation suited to the audience.
6. **Follow up**: update the view as new information arrives.

## Worked example
An analyst is asked by a bank's credit committee whether to renew a three-year loan to Larkfield Tools. The company reports beginning equity of USD 400 million, net income of USD 60 million, dividends of USD 15 million and new shares issued of USD 25 million. The auditor issued a qualified opinion relating to the valuation of one overseas subsidiary.

**Step 1 - Purpose and context.** The audience is a credit committee; the question is repayment capacity over three years, so cash flow and leverage matter more than share valuation.

**Step 2 - Check the equity roll-forward.**

$$
400 + 60 - 15 + 25 = 470
$$

Ending equity should be **USD 470 million**. If the balance sheet shows a different number, the gap must be explained by OCI or other owner transactions in the statement of changes in equity.

**Step 3 - Read the audit report.** A qualified opinion means the rest of the statements are fairly presented, but the subsidiary's valuation is unreliable. The analyst reads the note on that subsidiary and stress-tests the loan decision assuming its assets are worth materially less.

**Step 4 - Conclude.** The analyst recommends renewal only with a covenant on minimum equity and quarterly reporting, and plans to follow up when the next audit is issued.

## Common exam traps
- Confusing the balance sheet (a point in time) with the income and cash flow statements (a period of time).
- Thinking an unqualified opinion guarantees there is no fraud; an audit gives reasonable, not absolute, assurance.
- Mixing up a qualified opinion (one contained exception) with an adverse opinion (statements as a whole are misleading).
- Treating management commentary as audited; it generally is not.
- Forgetting that OCI items change equity without passing through net income.

## Key takeaways
- The four statements describe position, performance, equity changes and cash flows, and they reconcile to one another.
- The notes contain the policies, estimates and details that often matter more than the headline numbers.
- Audit opinions range from unqualified to qualified, adverse and disclaimer; read key audit matters and any going-concern warning.
- Accrual profit and cash flow differ, which is why both statements are needed.
- Start every analysis by defining its purpose, then collect, process, interpret, communicate and follow up.
