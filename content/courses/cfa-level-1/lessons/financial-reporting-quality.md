title: "Financial Reporting Quality"

## Learning goals
- Distinguish financial reporting quality from earnings quality and place a set of reports on the quality spectrum.
- Explain the conditions (motivation, opportunity, rationalisation) that make low-quality reporting more likely.
- Identify common manipulation techniques involving revenue, expenses, the balance sheet and cash flow classification.
- Calculate and interpret warning-sign metrics such as days sales outstanding, the CFO-to-net-income ratio and an accruals ratio.

## Explanation

### Two different questions
**Financial reporting quality** asks whether the reports faithfully describe what actually happened. **Earnings quality** (or results quality) asks whether the results themselves are sustainable and adequate. A company can report a terrible year with perfect honesty (high reporting quality, low earnings quality). The reverse - high reported earnings built on weak disclosure - is what analysts most need to catch, because those earnings cannot be relied on.

### The quality spectrum
Think of a ladder from best to worst:
1. GAAP-compliant, decision-useful reporting with sustainable, adequate earnings.
2. GAAP-compliant and decision-useful, but earnings are low or unsustainable.
3. GAAP-compliant but **biased** choices: aggressive (inflating current results) or conservative (understating them, often to build reserves that can be released later).
4. GAAP-compliant but using accounting choices to manage earnings - for example timing discretionary spending or engineering transactions.
5. Departures from GAAP: non-compliant reporting.
6. Fictitious transactions: outright fraud.

Conservative bias is not automatically "safe": reserves built in good years can be quietly released to smooth weak years, which is still a distortion.

### Why it happens: the fraud triangle
Low-quality reporting usually needs three ingredients: **motivation** (meeting analyst targets, debt covenants, bonus thresholds), **opportunity** (weak internal controls, a dominant CEO, complex transactions, accounting estimates with wide ranges) and **rationalisation** (management convincing itself the distortion is temporary or harmless). Markets, auditors, regulators and good governance push back, but each has limits: an audit is based on sampling and is paid for by the company being audited.

### Where to look: common techniques
- **Revenue:** recognising sales too early, bill-and-hold arrangements, channel stuffing near period end, or recording gross instead of net revenue as an agent.
- **Expenses:** capitalising costs that should be expensed, stretching useful lives, raising salvage values, and under-reserving for bad debts, warranties or inventory obsolescence.
- **Balance sheet:** off-balance-sheet structures, goodwill that is never impaired, and inventory or receivables that keep growing faster than sales.
- **Cash flow:** shifting items into operating cash flow (CFO) - for example stretching payables, selling receivables, or classifying operating outflows as investing.

### Warning-sign metrics
Rising receivables relative to sales are a classic flag:

$$
\text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Accounts receivable}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 365
$$

where DSO is days sales outstanding.

Earnings that are not backed by cash are another. Compare CFO with net income (NI); a ratio persistently below 1 deserves scrutiny. The cash-flow version of the **accruals ratio** scales the non-cash part of earnings:

$$
\text{Accruals ratio} = \frac{NI - CFO - CFI}{\text{Average net operating assets}}
$$

where CFI is cash flow from investing (normally negative) and net operating assets are operating assets minus operating liabilities. Higher accruals mean earnings rely more on estimates, which historically tend to reverse.

Other flags: frequent "non-recurring" charges, non-GAAP metrics that exclude more each year, changes of auditor, related-party transactions, and a sudden change in accounting policies or estimates.

## Worked example
Kestrel Ltd reports the following (in millions):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,000 | 1,100 |
| Accounts receivable | 120 | 200 |
| Net income | 80 | 95 |
| Cash flow from operations | 90 | 40 |

**Step 1 - Growth comparison.** Revenue grew (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = 10%. Receivables grew (200 − 120) ÷ 120 = 66.7%. Receivables are growing more than six times faster than sales.

**Step 2 - DSO.** Year 1: 120 ÷ 1,000 × 365 = 43.8 days. Year 2: 200 ÷ 1,100 × 365 = 66.4 days. Customers are suddenly taking about three weeks longer to pay, or sales are being recognised before they are earned.

**Step 3 - Cash backing.** CFO ÷ NI: Year 1 = 90 ÷ 80 = 1.13; Year 2 = 40 ÷ 95 = 0.42.

**Step 4 - Accrual component.** NI − CFO = 95 − 40 = 55 in Year 2 versus 80 − 90 = −10 in Year 1. If average total assets are about 1,000, accruals equal roughly 5.5% of assets, up from negative.

**Conclusion: reported earnings rose 18.75% while cash generation collapsed and DSO jumped from 43.8 to 66.4 days - a pattern consistent with aggressive revenue recognition that warrants a close reading of the revenue note.**

## Common exam traps
- Equating "GAAP-compliant" with "high quality". Biased and managed earnings can be fully compliant.
- Treating conservative accounting as always good; it can create cookie-jar reserves.
- Reading a single year in isolation. Flags appear in trends and in comparisons with peers.
- Forgetting that CFO itself can be managed (stretching payables, selling receivables, classification choices).
- Confusing earnings quality (sustainability) with reporting quality (faithfulness).

## Key takeaways
- Reporting quality is about faithful representation; earnings quality is about sustainability and adequacy.
- The spectrum runs from decision-useful reporting through biased and managed earnings to non-compliance and fraud.
- Motivation, opportunity and rationalisation together raise the risk of manipulation.
- Revenue recognition, capitalisation choices, reserves and CFO classification are the main battlegrounds.
- DSO trends, CFO-to-NI and accruals ratios are quick, powerful screens for low-quality earnings.
