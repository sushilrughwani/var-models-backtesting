## Lecture outline

- 02:00 - Aims of fiscal policy and the spending and revenue tools
- 05:00 - The multiplier: intuition of successive spending rounds, then the formula
- 09:00 - Worked example: spending versus tax cut versus balanced budget
- 13:00 - Automatic stabilisers, discretionary policy, structural versus cyclical deficits
- 16:00 - Limits: lags, crowding out, Ricardian equivalence, supply constraints, debt

## Slides
### Slide 1: What Fiscal Policy Does
- Stabilise aggregate demand over the cycle
- Redistribute income and wealth
- Provide public goods

### Slide 2: Spending Tools
- Transfer payments: pensions, unemployment benefits
- Current spending: wages, goods consumed now
- Capital spending: infrastructure that lifts future capacity

### Slide 3: Revenue Tools
- Direct taxes: income, wealth, corporate profits
- Indirect taxes: VAT, sales tax, excise duties
- Good tax: simple, efficient, fair, sufficient

### Slide 4: The Multiplier
- Spending becomes income, part is taxed, part is spent again
- Multiplier = 1 / [1 − c(1 − t)]
- Higher c, bigger multiplier; higher t, smaller multiplier

### Slide 5: Spending vs Tax Changes
- Spending change: ΔY = multiplier × ΔG
- Tax change: ΔY = multiplier × c × ΔT
- Balanced-budget expansion is still positive

### Slide 6: Automatic Stabilisers
- Taxes fall and benefits rise in recessions without new laws
- Cushion disposable income
- Make the budget balance move with the cycle

### Slide 7: Structural vs Cyclical Deficit
- Cyclical: due to output below potential
- Structural: would remain at full employment
- Change in structural balance shows the policy stance

### Slide 8: Policy Lags
- Recognition lag: spotting the problem
- Action lag: agreeing and legislating
- Impact lag: time for the economy to respond

### Slide 9: Other Limits
- Crowding out of private investment
- Ricardian equivalence: households save tax cuts
- Near full employment, demand mostly raises prices
- Debt service burden and higher future taxes

### Slide 10: Exam Traps
- Include the tax rate in the multiplier
- Tax cut uses c × ΔT
- Bigger deficit is not always deliberate stimulus

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Base case.** c = 0.80, t = 25%, ΔG = +10 billion.
   - Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.80 × 0.75) = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
   - ΔY = 25 billion; extra tax = 0.25 × 25 = 6.25 billion; deficit rises 3.75 billion
   - Lump-sum tax cut of 10 billion: ΔY = 0.80 × 10 × 2.5 = 20 billion
   - Balanced budget (+10 spending, +10 tax): ΔY = 25 − 20 = 5 billion
2. **Higher propensity to consume.** c = 0.90, t = 20%, ΔG = +20 billion.
   - Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.90 × 0.80) = 1 / 0.28 = 3.5714
   - ΔY = 3.5714 × 20 = 71.43 billion

## Questions to ask students
- What happens to the multiplier if the tax rate rises? (It falls, because more income leaks out each round.)
- Why is a tax cut weaker than an equal spending increase? (Part of the tax cut is saved before it enters the spending chain.)
- Unemployment benefits rising in a recession are an example of what? (An automatic stabiliser.)
- Which lag reflects the time it takes parliament to pass a budget? (The action lag.)
- Under Ricardian equivalence, what do households do with a debt-financed tax cut? (Save it to pay expected future taxes.)

## Analogies
- The multiplier is a ripple in a pond: each ring is smaller than the last because taxes and saving absorb some energy.
- Automatic stabilisers are a car's shock absorbers: they work on every bump without the driver doing anything.
- Policy lags are steering a supertanker: by the time the turn takes effect, the bend in the river may be behind you.

## Recording tips
- Build the multiplier with a numeric chain (10, then 6, then 3.6...) before showing the formula; students remember the story better than the algebra.
- Show spending, tax cut and balanced budget results side by side so the ranking sticks.
- Stress structural versus cyclical deficit with a real-feeling scenario; it is a frequent conceptual question.
