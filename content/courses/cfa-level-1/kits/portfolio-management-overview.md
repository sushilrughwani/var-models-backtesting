## Slides
### Slide 1: Why portfolios, not single stocks
- Individual stocks carry large company-specific shocks
- Holdings react differently to the same news, so shocks partly offset
- Evaluate each asset by its contribution to total portfolio risk and return
### Slide 2: Diversification ratio
- Ratio = portfolio σ / average single-security σ
- Lower ratio = bigger diversification benefit
- Works whenever correlation is below +1
### Slide 3: The n-stock formula
- $\sigma_p^2 = \sigma^2[1/n + (1-1/n)\rho]$
- As n grows, σp falls toward $\sigma\sqrt{\rho}$
- Example: σ = 30%, ρ = 0.2: 1 stock 30%, 2 stocks 23.24%, 10 stocks 15.87%, floor 13.42%
### Slide 4: What diversification cannot do
- Cannot remove systematic (market) risk
- Correlations rise in crises, so protection weakens when most needed
- Still the closest thing to a free lunch in finance
### Slide 5: The portfolio management process
- Planning: client objectives and constraints, IPS, capital market expectations, strategic allocation
- Execution: analysis, security selection, construction, trading
- Feedback: monitoring, rebalancing, performance measurement and reporting
### Slide 6: Pension plans
- DB: employer promises benefit, employer bears investment risk
- DC: employer promises contribution, employee bears investment risk and chooses investments
- DB plans with many retirees need more liquidity
### Slide 7: Institutions at a glance
- Endowments/foundations: perpetual horizon, high risk tolerance, steady spending
- Banks: short horizon, low risk tolerance, high liquidity
- Life insurers: long horizon; P&C insurers: shorter horizon, more liquidity
- Sovereign wealth funds: long horizon, government owned
### Slide 8: The asset management industry
- Buy side manages money; sell side provides brokerage and research
- Active vs passive; passive gaining share; consolidation; technology
### Slide 9: Pooled vehicles
- Open-end funds transact at NAV; closed-end funds trade on exchange at premium or discount
- ETFs, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital
### Slide 10: Recap
- Portfolio view, diversification floor, three-step process, investor types, DB vs DC

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Two-stock diversification.** σA = σB = 30%, ρ = 0.20, 50/50 weights. Variance = 0.25(0.09) + 0.25(0.09) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.20)(0.30)(0.30) = 0.0225 + 0.0225 + 0.009 = 0.054. σp = √0.054 = 23.24%. Diversification ratio = 23.24 / 30 = 0.775.
2. **Adding stocks.** Same σ and ρ. n = 5: σp² = 0.09(0.2 + 0.8 × 0.2) = 0.0324, σp = 18.00%. n = 10: 0.09(0.1 + 0.9 × 0.2) = 0.0252, σp = 15.87%. n = 100: σp = 13.68%. Floor: 0.30 × √0.2 = 13.42%. Point out the diminishing marginal benefit.

## Questions to ask students
- If correlation were exactly +1, what would the diversification ratio be for equal-volatility stocks? (1.0 - no benefit at all.)
- Who bears investment risk in a defined contribution plan? (The employee.)
- Why does a bank have low risk tolerance? (Its liabilities are short-term deposits that must be repaid at par on demand.)
- Which step of the process includes rebalancing? (Feedback.)
- Why might a closed-end fund trade below NAV? (Its units trade on an exchange, so price is set by supply and demand rather than redemption at NAV.)

## Analogies
- Diversification is like carrying both sunscreen and an umbrella: whichever weather arrives, one item pays off. But neither helps in an earthquake - that is systematic risk.
- A DB plan is a restaurant set menu with a fixed price promised by the chef (employer carries the risk of ingredient costs); a DC plan is a grocery voucher - you get a fixed amount and cook for yourself.
- The portfolio process is a thermostat: set a target (planning), heat or cool (execution), measure the room and adjust (feedback).

## Recording tips
- Draw the curve of σp against n on screen; students remember the flattening shape better than the formula.
- Spend extra time on DB versus DC - it is examined repeatedly and the "who bears risk" question trips people up.
- Keep the investor-type table on screen while talking; candidates like a single comparison grid.
