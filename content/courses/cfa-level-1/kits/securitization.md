## Slides
### Slide 1: Fixed-Income Securitization
- Turning illiquid loans into tradable bonds
- Builds on the credit risk lessons: these bonds get their credit quality from pool losses
- Next lesson: mortgage and asset-backed securities in detail

### Slide 2: The Securitisation Chain
- Originator makes loans and sells the pool
- SPE buys the pool and issues ABS
- Servicer collects payments; trustee protects investors

### Slide 3: True Sale and Bankruptcy Remoteness
- Loans legally leave the originator's balance sheet
- The originator's creditors cannot touch the pool
- ABS credit quality depends on the loans, not the originator

### Slide 4: Tranching
- Senior, mezzanine and equity (residual) tranches
- Losses flow bottom-up
- Risk is reallocated, not destroyed

### Slide 5: Measuring Subordination
- Subordination = par of junior tranches / pool balance
- Example: Class A 400 of 500 gives 100/500 = 20%
- More subordination means more protection and a higher rating

### Slide 6: Loss Waterfall Results
- Loss 20: equity −66.7%; B and A untouched
- Loss 50: equity −100%; B −28.6%; A untouched
- Loss 120: equity and B wiped out; A −5.0%

### Slide 7: Other Credit Enhancement
- Internal: overcollateralisation, excess spread, reserve accounts
- External: letters of credit, financial guarantees
- External protection carries the guarantor's own risk

### Slide 8: Who Benefits
- Issuers: cheaper funding and balance-sheet capacity
- Investors: diversified pools and tailored risk
- Economy: liquid loans and more efficient capital allocation

### Slide 9: Risks and Incentives
- Complexity and opacity
- Originate-to-distribute can weaken lending standards
- Risk retention rules keep originators' skin in the game

### Slide 10: Covered Bonds vs ABS
- Covered bond: the pool stays on the bank's balance sheet
- Dual recourse to the cover pool and the bank
- Bank must replace bad loans in the cover pool

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - three-tranche waterfall.** Pool USD 500m: A 400, B 70, equity 30.
- Subordination: A = 100/500 = 20%; B = 30/500 = 6%; equity = 0%.
- Loss 20m: equity loses 20 (66.7%).
- Loss 50m: equity loses 30 (100%), B loses 20 (28.6%).
- Loss 120m: equity 30 (100%), B 70 (100%), A 20 (5.0%).

**Example 2 - overcollateralisation.** A pool of USD 520m backs USD 500m of bonds.
- OC = 20m, or 20/520 = 3.85% of the pool, absorbs the first losses before any bond tranche is hit.
- Combined with the structure above, Class A now survives pool losses of 120m (20 + 100) before taking a loss.

## Questions to ask students
- Why must the sale to the SPE be a true sale? (So that the originator's creditors cannot claim the pool in a bankruptcy.)
- In a deal with tranches 70/20/10, what is the senior tranche's subordination? (30%.)
- Does tranching lower the pool's total expected loss? (No, it only reallocates loss among tranches.)
- Who has more recourse, a covered bond investor or an ABS investor? (The covered bond investor, who has dual recourse.)
- Name one internal and one external credit enhancement. (Internal: subordination, OC, excess spread or a reserve account; external: a letter of credit or guarantee.)

## Analogies
- Champagne tower: cash pours into the top glasses (senior) first and overflows downward, while losses drain from the bottom glasses (equity) first.
- Moving house: a true sale is like actually moving out, not just lending the house. Your creditors cannot chase the house once it is legally someone else's.
- Shock absorbers: each junior tranche is a cushion that must be fully compressed before the senior tranche feels the bump.

## Recording tips
- Draw the tranches as a stacked bar and shade losses from the bottom. Students grasp the waterfall instantly.
- Emphasise that subordination counts only junior tranches; this is a frequent slip.
- Briefly mention that misaligned incentives contributed to the 2007-2009 crisis to show why risk retention exists, without dwelling on history.
