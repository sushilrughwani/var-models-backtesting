## Slides
### Slide 1: Capitalise or Expense?
- Multi-period benefit: capitalise and depreciate
- Current-period benefit: expense
- Purchase, delivery, installation capitalised; routine repairs expensed
### Slide 2: Effects of Capitalising (Year Incurred)
- Higher net income, assets and equity
- Higher CFO, lower CFI; total cash unchanged
- Lower profit in later years as depreciation runs
### Slide 3: Interest and Intangibles
- Interest during construction is capitalised
- IFRS: expense research, capitalise qualifying development
- US GAAP: generally expense R&D (software exceptions)
### Slide 4: Straight-Line and Units of Production
- SL = (cost - residual) / life
- Units of production = (cost - residual) per unit × units used
- Expense follows time or usage
### Slide 5: Double-Declining Balance
- Rate = 2 / N applied to opening book value
- Ignore residual in the formula, but never go below it
- Front-loads expense
### Slide 6: Torres Logistics DDB
- 40,000; 24,000; 14,400; 8,640; 2,960
- Year 5 capped to stop at residual 10,000
- Total 90,000, same as straight-line
### Slide 7: Estimates Matter
- Longer life, higher residual: lower depreciation, higher profit
- Compare with peers
- IFRS component depreciation
### Slide 8: Impairment - IFRS
- Recoverable amount = max(fair value less costs to sell, value in use)
- Loss = carrying amount - recoverable amount
- Reversals allowed (not goodwill)
### Slide 9: Impairment - US GAAP
- Step 1: carrying amount vs undiscounted cash flows
- Step 2: loss = carrying amount - fair value
- No reversals for assets held for use
### Slide 10: Revaluation Model (IFRS Only)
- Assets carried at fair value
- Increases to OCI (revaluation surplus) unless reversing a prior loss in profit
- Decreases to profit unless reversing a prior surplus
### Slide 11: Key Takeaways
- Capitalising shifts cash from CFO to CFI
- Know the DDB floor
- The same asset can be impaired under IFRS and not under US GAAP

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Torres Logistics.** Cost 100,000; residual 10,000; life 5 years.
- SL = 18,000 per year.
- DDB at 40%: 40,000 (BV 60,000); 24,000 (36,000); 14,400 (21,600); 8,640 (12,960); year 5 capped at **2,960** to reach 10,000.

**Example 2 - Impairment comparison.** Carrying 50,000; undiscounted cash flows 52,000; value in use 45,000; fair value 40,000; costs to sell 2,000.
- US GAAP: 52,000 > 50,000, **no impairment**.
- IFRS: recoverable = max(38,000, 45,000) = 45,000; loss **5,000**.

**Example 3 - Second impairment.** Carrying 120,000; undiscounted cash flows 125,000; fair value less costs to sell 95,000; value in use 104,000. IFRS loss = 120,000 - 104,000 = **16,000**; US GAAP: no impairment because 125,000 > 120,000.

## Questions to ask students
- A company capitalises a cost it could have expensed. What happens to CFO in that year? (It rises; the outflow moves to investing.)
- Why is residual value ignored in the DDB formula? (The rate is applied to book value; residual only acts as a floor.)
- Under IFRS, what is recoverable amount? (The higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use.)
- Why does US GAAP trigger impairment less often? (Its first test uses undiscounted cash flows, which are larger.)
- Can a US GAAP company use the revaluation model for PP&E? (No; only the cost model.)

## Analogies
- Capitalising is like buying a season ticket: you spread its cost over every match rather than charging it all to the first game.
- DDB is a new car's value: it loses the most in the first year, then the declines get smaller.
- The US GAAP recoverability test is a lenient bouncer who only checks whether you have enough cash in total, ignoring when it arrives; the IFRS test is stricter and discounts the future.

## Recording tips
- Build the DDB table live and pause at year 5 so students predict the cap themselves.
- Put the IFRS and US GAAP impairment steps side by side; students habitually blend them.
- Stress that total depreciation and total cash are unchanged by method choices; only timing and classification move.
