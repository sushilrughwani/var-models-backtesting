## Slides
### Slide 1: Why Financial Statement Analysis?
- Statements are the most detailed, audited description of a business
- Users: equity analysts, credit analysts, lenders, suppliers, regulators
- Common goal: past performance, current position, future cash generation
### Slide 2: The Balance Sheet
- Snapshot at a single date
- Assets = Liabilities + Equity
- What the firm controls, owes and the owners' residual claim
### Slide 3: The Income Statement and OCI
- Performance over a period: revenues minus expenses
- Other comprehensive income bypasses profit but changes equity
- Net income + OCI = total comprehensive income
### Slide 4: Changes in Equity and Cash Flows
- Equity roll-forward explains every movement in owners' capital
- Cash flow statement: operating, investing, financing
- Bridges accrual profit to actual cash
### Slide 5: How the Statements Link
- Net income flows into retained earnings
- Ending cash on the cash flow statement = cash on the balance sheet
- Ending equity = beginning + NI + OCI - dividends + issuance - buybacks
### Slide 6: Read the Notes
- Accounting policies and key estimates
- Segments, contingencies, commitments, fair value detail
- Often where the real story sits
### Slide 7: Management Commentary and Other Sources
- MD&A: results, trends, liquidity, risks from management's view
- Usually unaudited and optimistic
- Also: interim reports, proxies, earnings calls, industry data
### Slide 8: The Audit Opinion
- Unqualified (clean), qualified (except for), adverse, disclaimer
- Reasonable assurance, not a guarantee against fraud
- Key audit matters and going-concern paragraphs
### Slide 9: Six-Step Analysis Framework
- Purpose and context, collect data, process data
- Analyse and interpret, communicate conclusions, follow up
- Purpose drives which data and ratios matter
### Slide 10: Key Takeaways
- Four statements, two identities
- Notes and audit report are essential reading
- Always begin with the question you are answering

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Equity roll-forward.** Beginning equity USD 400m, net income USD 60m, dividends USD 15m, shares issued USD 25m, no OCI. Ending equity = 400 + 60 - 15 + 25 = **USD 470m**. Show the two classic errors: forgetting the issuance (445) and forgetting dividends (485).

**Example 2 - Accounting equation.** Total assets USD 900m, total liabilities USD 540m. Equity = 900 - 540 = **USD 360m**. Then a USD 50m loan is used to buy equipment: assets 950, liabilities 590, equity unchanged at 360. Emphasise that borrowing does not create equity.

**Example 3 - Match the opinion.** (a) Auditor could not access subsidiary records: disclaimer. (b) Everything fair except one inventory valuation issue: qualified. (c) Revenue materially overstated throughout: adverse.

## Questions to ask students
- Which statement is measured at a point in time? (The balance sheet.)
- Can a profitable company run out of cash? (Yes; profit is accrual-based, e.g. large receivables or inventory build.)
- Where would you find the depreciation method used? (In the notes, accounting policies section.)
- Does a clean audit opinion prove there is no fraud? (No; it gives reasonable assurance only.)
- What is the first step of any analysis? (Define the purpose and context of the analysis.)

## Analogies
- The statements are four camera angles on one event: a photo (balance sheet), two videos (income and cash flow) and a replay of the owners' account (changes in equity).
- The audit opinion is like a vehicle inspection certificate: it says the car was roadworthy when checked, not that it can never break down.
- The notes are the ingredient list on a food label: the front of the packet is marketing, the small print tells you what is really inside.

## Recording tips
- Show a real-looking (fictional) set of statements on screen and physically trace net income into retained earnings and ending cash into the balance sheet.
- Students mix up qualified and adverse opinions; slow down and use the "except for" phrase as the memory hook.
- Keep this lesson brisk; it is a free preview, so end with a clear promise of what the next lessons will build.
