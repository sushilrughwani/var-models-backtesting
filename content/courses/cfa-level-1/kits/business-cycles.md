## Lecture outline
- 00:00 - Hook: the economy is "recovering", yet unemployment just hit a new high. How can both be true?
- 02:00 - Definition: recurring but irregular swings around trend; the output gap
- 04:00 - The four phases and what output, hiring, inventories and inflation do in each
- 09:00 - Credit cycles: collateral, lending standards and why they amplify recessions
- 12:00 - Leading, coincident and lagging indicators; composite and diffusion indexes
- 16:00 - Unemployment measurement, discouraged workers and types of unemployment (worked example)
- 20:00 - Recap: reading the cycle from indicators; exam traps

## Slides
### Slide 1: What Is a Business Cycle?
- Recurring but irregular swings in aggregate activity
- Measured relative to potential (trend) GDP
- Output gap = actual real GDP minus potential GDP

### Slide 2: The Four Phases
- Recovery: below trend, rising
- Expansion: above trend, rising
- Slowdown: above trend, growth fading
- Contraction: below trend, falling

### Slide 3: How Firms Behave Through the Cycle
- Labour: hours change first, headcount later
- Inventories: rebuilt in recovery, pile up in slowdown, cut in contraction
- Capital spending and consumer durables swing the most

### Slide 4: Inflation Through the Cycle
- Low or falling in recovery
- Rising in expansion as slack disappears
- Often peaks late, during the slowdown

### Slide 5: Credit Cycles
- Easy credit raises collateral values, which supports more lending
- Reversal: defaults, falling collateral, tighter standards
- Longer and deeper than business cycles
- Recessions with credit busts are more severe

### Slide 6: Leading Indicators
- Building permits, new capital goods orders
- Average weekly manufacturing hours, initial jobless claims
- Stock prices, yield curve spread, consumer expectations

### Slide 7: Coincident and Lagging Indicators
- Coincident: industrial production, real personal income, payrolls, sales
- Lagging: duration of unemployment, inventory-to-sales ratio
- Lagging: unit labour costs, prime rate, commercial loans outstanding

### Slide 8: Measuring Unemployment
- Labour force = employed + actively seeking work
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force
- Participation rate = labour force / working-age population
- Discouraged workers leave the labour force

### Slide 9: Types of Unemployment
- Frictional: between jobs
- Structural: skills or location mismatch
- Cyclical: weak demand in a downturn

### Slide 10: Exam Traps
- Falling unemployment rate can hide weakness
- Inventory-to-sales ratio is lagging
- Inflation peaks late
- Firms add hours before adding staff

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Unemployment arithmetic.** Working-age population 200m, labour force 130m, unemployed 6.5m.
   - Unemployment rate = 6.5/130 = 5.0%; participation = 130/200 = 65.0%
   - 1m discouraged workers stop searching: labour force 129m, unemployed 5.5m
   - New unemployment rate = 5.5/129 = 4.26%; participation = 64.5%
   - The rate fell with no new jobs created.
2. **Locate the cycle.** Building permits up three months running, industrial production flat, inventory-to-sales ratio still high, duration of unemployment still rising.
   - Leading turning up, coincident flat, lagging still reflecting the downturn
   - Conclusion: near the trough, entering recovery.

## Questions to ask students
- In which phase is activity above trend but growth slowing? (Slowdown.)
- Is average duration of unemployment a leading or lagging indicator? (Lagging.)
- Why do firms increase overtime before hiring in a recovery? (Hiring is costly; they wait to confirm the recovery is real.)
- What happens to the unemployment rate if many unemployed people stop looking for work? (It falls, because they leave the labour force.)
- Why do credit busts make recessions worse? (Falling collateral and tighter lending cut spending further, creating a feedback loop.)

## Analogies
- Indicators are like weather signs: falling barometer pressure (leading) warns of rain, the rain itself (coincident), and puddles that remain after the storm (lagging).
- The credit cycle is a ladder that grows as you climb: higher house prices let you borrow more, which lifts house prices further, until the ladder starts shrinking from under you.
- Inventories are a bullwhip: a small change in consumer demand becomes a big swing in factory orders.

## Recording tips
- Draw the cycle as a wave around a rising trend line and label the four phases on it; refer back to this drawing throughout.
- Pause on the discouraged-worker example; it is a favourite conceptual trap and students often miss why the rate falls.
- Put the three indicator lists side by side on screen so students can see them as a set.
