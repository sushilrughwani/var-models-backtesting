## Slides
### Slide 1: Why Countries Trade
- Specialise in what you do relatively well
- Gains: lower prices, variety, scale, competition, technology transfer
- Costs: concentrated losses in import-competing industries

### Slide 2: Absolute vs Comparative Advantage
- Absolute: fewer resources per unit
- Comparative: lower opportunity cost
- Comparative advantage decides who exports what

### Slide 3: Computing Opportunity Cost
- Cost of X = units of Y given up per unit of X
- Compare across countries for each good
- Terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs

### Slide 4: Ricardian vs Heckscher-Ohlin
- Ricardian: productivity and technology differences
- Heckscher-Ohlin: factor endowments (capital versus labour)
- H-O: trade helps the abundant factor, hurts the scarce factor

### Slide 5: Tariffs
- Tax on imports raises domestic price
- More domestic output, less consumption, fewer imports
- Government collects revenue; deadweight loss remains

### Slide 6: Quotas, Subsidies, VERs
- Quota: quantity limit; rent goes to licence holders
- Export subsidy: raises domestic price, costs taxpayers
- VER: rent goes to foreign exporters, worst for the importer

### Slide 7: Tariff Welfare Accounting
- Consumer surplus loss = producer gain + revenue + deadweight loss
- Deadweight loss = production triangle + consumption triangle

### Slide 8: Trading Blocs
- Free trade area, customs union, common market
- Economic union, monetary union
- Each step adds a layer of integration

### Slide 9: Trade Creation vs Diversion
- Creation: cheaper member imports replace costly domestic output
- Diversion: imports switch from cheap outsider to costlier member
- Diversion can reduce welfare

### Slide 10: Exam Traps
- Use opportunity cost, not absolute advantage
- Customs union means a common external tariff
- Quota rent ownership differs from a tariff

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Comparative advantage.** Per labour hour: A makes 4 cloth or 2 wine; B makes 1 cloth or 1 wine.
   - A: 1 wine costs 2 cloth; 1 cloth costs 0.5 wine
   - B: 1 wine costs 1 cloth; 1 cloth costs 1 wine
   - A exports cloth, B exports wine; mutually beneficial terms are 1 to 2 cloth per wine
2. **Tariff welfare.** $Q_d = 100 - 4P$, $Q_s = -20 + 4P$, world price 10, tariff 2.
   - At 10: demand 60, supply 20, imports 40; at 12: demand 52, supply 28, imports 24
   - Consumer loss = 2 × (60 + 52)/2 = 112
   - Producer gain = 2 × (20 + 28)/2 = 48; revenue = 2 × 24 = 48
   - Deadweight loss = 112 − 96 = 16 (8 production + 8 consumption)

## Questions to ask students
- Country X can make everything more cheaply than Country Y. Can both still gain from trade? (Yes, if their opportunity costs differ.)
- Which bloc first introduces a common external tariff? (Customs union.)
- Under a voluntary export restraint, who captures the quota rent? (Foreign exporters.)
- According to Heckscher-Ohlin, what does a labour-abundant country export? (Labour-intensive goods.)
- Which tariff effect is a gain to the domestic government? (Tariff revenue on remaining imports.)

## Analogies
- The lawyer and the typist: even if the lawyer types faster, each hour typing costs her billable fees, so the typist has the comparative advantage.
- A tariff is a toll booth on a bridge: some travellers pay, some stop crossing, and the lost trips are the deadweight loss.
- Trading blocs are like house-sharing arrangements: a free trade area shares the kitchen, a monetary union shares the bank account.

## Recording tips
- Do the opportunity cost table slowly on the whiteboard; inverting the ratio is the most common error.
- Draw the tariff diagram and shade the four areas in different colours, then match each to its number.
- List the five blocs as a staircase on screen so students can see each added layer.
