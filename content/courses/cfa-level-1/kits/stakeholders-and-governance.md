## Slides
### Slide 1: Everyone Has a Stake
- A stakeholder is anyone affected by how the company performs
- Interests overlap on survival and growth, but collide on risk, payout and pay
- Today: map the groups, then map the conflicts

### Slide 2: The Stakeholder Map
- Shareholders: residual claim, voting rights
- Creditors: fixed contractual claim
- Board and managers: oversight and execution
- Employees, customers, suppliers: ongoing relationships
- Governments and regulators: taxes, law, public interest

### Slide 3: Payoffs at Maturity
- Debt: $\min(V, F)$, capped upside, exposed downside
- Equity: $\max(V-F, 0)$, unlimited upside, floor at zero
- Equity looks like a call option on firm assets

### Slide 4: Why Owners Like Risk
- More volatility raises the value of the upside owners keep
- Creditors absorb losses below face value
- Asset substitution: switching to riskier projects after borrowing

### Slide 5: Kestrel's Choice
- Safe: value 120; Risky: 180 or 40 (50/50), expected 110
- Equity: 20 safe vs 40 risky
- Debt: 100 safe vs 70 risky
- Owners pick the value-destroying project

### Slide 6: Creditor-Shareholder Flashpoints
- Risk shifting into volatile projects
- Large dividends or buybacks that drain assets
- New debt that dilutes existing lenders
- Creditors' defence: covenants, collateral, higher rates

### Slide 7: Other Conflicts
- Managers versus shareholders: empire building, excessive pay, too little risk
- Controlling versus minority shareholders: related-party deals
- Owners versus employees, customers, society: cost cuts that hurt safety or quality

### Slide 8: Two Views of Corporate Purpose
- Shareholder theory: maximise equity value; contracts protect others
- Stakeholder theory: balance all groups' interests
- Over long horizons the two tend to converge

### Slide 9: Governance Is the Referee
- Rules, practices and controls that direct the company
- Each group has tools: votes, covenants, contracts, law, reputation
- The board sits at the centre; details next lesson

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Kestrel Ltd (asset substitution).** Debt face 100. Safe project: firm worth 120. Risky project: 180 or 40, each with probability 0.5.
- Expected firm value: safe 120; risky 0.5 × 180 + 0.5 × 40 = 110.
- Equity: safe 120 - 100 = 20; risky 0.5 × 80 + 0.5 × 0 = 40.
- Debt: safe 100; risky 0.5 × 100 + 0.5 × 40 = 70.
- Owners gain 20, creditors lose 30, society loses 10 of value.

**Example 2 - Dividend drain.** A firm has assets of 150 and debt of 100 due next year. Owners vote a special dividend of 60.
- Assets fall to 90, now below the 100 owed.
- Shareholders pocketed 60 in cash; creditors now face a shortfall of 10 if assets do not grow.
- Ask: what covenant would have prevented this? (A restriction on dividends or a minimum net worth/asset coverage covenant.)

## Questions to ask students
- Which group has a fixed claim, and which has a residual claim? (Creditors fixed; shareholders residual.)
- In the bad state, firm value is 40 and debt is 100. What do shareholders get? (Zero, because of limited liability.)
- Why might a manager reject a positive-NPV but risky project? (Job security and undiversified personal wealth tied to the firm.)
- Would bondholders cheer a large share buyback? (No; it moves cash to shareholders and weakens creditor protection.)
- Does stakeholder theory say ignore shareholders? (No, it balances them with other groups.)

## Analogies
- Equity as a lottery ticket bought with borrowed money: if the bet fails, the lender eats the loss; if it pays, you keep the jackpot.
- A creditor is like a landlord: wants the rent and the house returned intact, gets nothing extra if the tenant becomes rich.
- Governance as a referee in a match with many teams: it does not stop competition, it keeps it within the rules.

## Recording tips
- Draw the hockey-stick payoff diagrams for debt and equity side by side; the picture makes the risk-preference conflict obvious.
- Students often compute negative equity in the bad state. Pause on that step and stress limited liability.
- Keep governance mechanisms to an overview here; signal clearly that board structure and ESG come in the next lesson.
