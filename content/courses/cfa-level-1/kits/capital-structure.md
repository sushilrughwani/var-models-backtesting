## Slides
### Slide 1: The Debt Paradox
- Debt has a lower required return than equity
- Yet firms rarely exceed moderate leverage
- Today's journey: MM theory, then the real-world frictions

### Slide 2: MM Assumptions
- No taxes, no bankruptcy or transaction costs
- Symmetric information
- Investors borrow and lend at the firm's rate
- Investment decisions fixed

### Slide 3: MM Without Taxes
- Proposition I: $V_L = V_U$
- Proposition II: $r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)\frac{D}{E}$
- WACC constant at $r_0$; cheaper debt offset by riskier equity

### Slide 4: Adding Corporate Taxes
- Interest is deductible: tax shield = $tD$ for perpetual debt
- Proposition I: $V_L = V_U + tD$
- Proposition II: $r_e = r_0 + (r_0 - r_d)(1-t)\frac{D}{E}$
- WACC falls as leverage rises

### Slide 5: Gannet Corp
- $V_U$ = 500m, D = 200m at 6%, t = 25%, $r_0$ = 10%
- $V_L$ = 550m, E = 350m
- $r_e$ = 11.71%, WACC = 9.09%

### Slide 6: Costs of Financial Distress
- Direct: legal and administrative fees
- Indirect: lost customers, suppliers, staff and investment opportunities
- Higher for intangible assets and volatile cash flows

### Slide 7: Agency Costs and Debt
- Debt disciplines managers by committing free cash flow
- Too much debt invites risk shifting and underinvestment
- Net effect depends on leverage level

### Slide 8: Static Trade-Off Theory
- $V_L = V_U + tD - \text{PV(distress costs)}$
- Optimum where marginal tax benefit = marginal distress cost
- Target capital structure minimises WACC

### Slide 9: Pecking Order and Signalling
- Managers know more than investors
- Equity issue can signal overvaluation
- Order: internal funds, then debt, then equity

### Slide 10: What Drives Leverage
- Business risk and asset tangibility
- Taxes, ratings, covenants, interest rate environment
- Management preferences and peer norms

### Slide 11: The Life Cycle
- Start-up: equity, little or no debt
- Growth: some secured or convertible debt
- Mature: stable cash flows, highest debt capacity

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Gannet Corp (MM with taxes).** $V_U$ = 500m, $r_0$ = 10%, t = 25%, D = 200m at 6%.
- $V_L$ = 500 + 0.25 × 200 = 550m; E = 350m; D/E = 0.5714.
- $r_e$ = 0.10 + 0.04 × 0.75 × 0.5714 = 11.71%.
- WACC = (200/550)(6%)(0.75) + (350/550)(11.71%) = 1.64% + 7.45% = 9.09%.
- Check: $r_0(1 - tD/V_L)$ = 10% × (1 − 50/550) = 9.09%.

**Example 2 - Same firm, no taxes.**
- $V_L$ = 500m; E = 300m; D/E = 0.6667.
- $r_e$ = 0.10 + 0.04 × 0.6667 = 12.67%.
- WACC = (200/500)(6%) + (300/500)(12.67%) = 2.4% + 7.6% = 10%. Unchanged.

**Example 3 - Quick MM II drill.** $r_0$ = 9%, $r_d$ = 5%, D/E = 0.6, no taxes.
- $r_e$ = 9% + 4% × 0.6 = 11.40%. With a 30% tax rate it would be 9% + 4% × 0.7 × 0.6 = 10.68%.

## Questions to ask students
- Under MM without taxes, what happens to WACC as D/E rises? (Nothing; it stays at $r_0$.)
- Unlevered value 800, debt 300, tax 30%: levered value under MM with taxes? (890.)
- Why does the cost of equity rise less steeply with taxes? (The (1 − t) factor; the tax shield absorbs part of the risk transfer.)
- Which firm can carry more debt: a utility or a biotech start-up? (The utility: stable cash flows, tangible assets.)
- A firm announces a large equity issue. Typical share price reaction and why? (Negative; signal that managers think shares are overvalued.)
- What is the pecking order? (Internal funds, debt, new equity.)

## Analogies
- MM I is the pizza: cut it into four slices or eight, you still have one pizza.
- The tax shield is a government subsidy for borrowing; distress costs are the price of having too many creditors at the door.
- Signalling is a homeowner who suddenly wants to sell a share of their house: you wonder what they know about the roof.

## Recording tips
- Draw the classic chart: $r_e$ rising, $r_d$ flat, WACC flat (no taxes), then redraw with WACC dipping and rising for trade-off theory.
- Stress market values in D/E and the $(1-t)$ placement; these drive most wrong answers.
- Close with the life-cycle slide; it turns the theory into an intuitive story candidates can reason from.
