## Lecture outline

- 05:00 - The futures curve: carrying costs, convenience yield, contango vs backwardation, the three theories
- 09:00 - Return decomposition: spot + roll + collateral
- 12:00 - Whiteboard: backwardation and contango examples

- 18:00 - Portfolio role, exam traps and recap

## Slides
### Slide 1: Natural Resources
- Commodities: energy, metals, grains, livestock, softs
- Land-based: farmland and timberland
- Real assets with inflation sensitivity

### Slide 2: How to Get Exposure
- Physical holdings: storage and insurance costs, no income
- Futures via index funds, ETPs, managed futures
- Producer equities: also carry stock-market and company risk

### Slide 3: Futures Price Building Blocks
- Spot + storage + insurance + financing - convenience yield
- Convenience yield: value of having the physical good when supply is tight

### Slide 4: Contango vs Backwardation
- Contango: futures above spot; ample inventories; negative roll
- Backwardation: futures below spot; scarce supply; positive roll

### Slide 5: Theories of the Curve
- Insurance theory (normal backwardation): producers pay speculators to hedge
- Hedging pressure: whether producers or consumers dominate
- Theory of storage: inventories and convenience yield

### Slide 6: Total Return Decomposition
- R_total = R_spot + R_roll + R_collateral
- R_roll = (F_near - F_far) / F_near x fraction rolled
- Collateral earns the T-bill rate on the full notional

### Slide 7: Farmland
- Crop income or rent plus land value changes
- Harvest timing fixed by crop maturity
- Weather, disease and price risk

### Slide 8: Timberland
- Biological growth + timber prices + land value
- Trees can be "stored on the stump" when prices are weak
- Fire, pest and storm risk; long horizons

### Slide 9: Portfolio Role
- Tends to rise with unexpected inflation and supply shocks
- Low correlation with stocks and bonds
- Highly volatile; long-run return depends heavily on roll

### Slide 10: Recap
- Learn the sign rule: backwardation helps, contango hurts
- Never forget collateral return
- Timberland's edge is harvest flexibility

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1: Backwardation.** Spot 80.00 -> 84.00. Roll: sell expiring at 84.00, buy next at 82.32. Collateral 4%.
- Spot return = 4/80 = 5.00%.
- Roll return = (84.00 - 82.32)/84.00 = 2.00%.
- Total = 5% + 2% + 4% = 11.00%.

**Example 2: Contango.** Spot 60.00 -> 57.00. Roll: sell at 57.00, buy at 58.71. Collateral 3%.
- Spot = -5.00%. Roll = (57.00 - 58.71)/57.00 = -3.00%. Total = -5% - 3% + 3% = -5.00%.

**Example 3: Partial roll.** Expiring 70.00, next 66.50, 50% of the position rolled: roll return = 5% x 0.5 = 2.50%.

## Questions to ask students
- In contango, is roll return positive or negative? (Negative: you sell the cheaper expiring contract and buy a dearer one.)
- What causes a high convenience yield? (Scarce inventories; users value having the physical good now.)
- Why do index returns include collateral return? (Futures need little cash, so the unused notional is assumed invested in T-bills.)
- What are timberland's three return sources? (Biological growth, timber price changes, land value changes.)
- Why are miners' shares an imperfect commodity proxy? (They also carry equity market, operating and company-specific risk.)

## Analogies
- Rolling futures in contango is like renewing a gym membership that costs more every month: you keep paying up just to stay in.
- Convenience yield is the premium you pay for an umbrella when it is already raining.
- Timberland is a savings account that keeps accruing while you wait for a better time to withdraw.

## Recording tips
- Draw an upward-sloping (contango) and downward-sloping (backwardation) curve side by side and show the roll as an arrow from near to far.
- Write the sign rule in large letters; most students lose points on the sign of roll return.
- Keep the theories brief; the exam focuses on the curve shape and return components.
