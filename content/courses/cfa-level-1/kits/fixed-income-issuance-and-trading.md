## Slides
### Slide 1: Fixed-Income Issuance and Trading
- How bonds are created and who buys them first
- How they trade afterwards
- How banks and companies fund themselves short term

### Slide 2: Classifying Bond Markets
- Issuer type: government, corporate, securitised
- Credit: investment grade vs high yield
- Maturity: money market (one year or less) vs capital market
- Currency, geography and coupon type

### Slide 3: Primary Market Methods
- Underwritten (firm commitment): bank bears unsold risk
- Best efforts: bank is a broker; issuer bears risk
- Book-building and grey market price discovery
- Private placement: few qualified buyers, custom terms

### Slide 4: Auctions and Shelf Registration
- Sovereigns auction on a regular calendar
- Single-price auction: all winners pay the price set by the highest accepted yield
- Primary dealers bid in every auction
- Shelf registration: one filing, many tranches over time

### Slide 5: Secondary Markets
- Mostly over the counter through dealers
- Bid-ask spread = liquidity gauge
- On-the-run government bonds: tightest spreads
- Issuer receives no cash from secondary trades

### Slide 6: Government Issuers
- Sovereigns: bills, notes, bonds; benchmark curve
- General obligation bonds: backed by taxing power
- Revenue bonds: backed by a project's cash flows
- Agencies and supranationals: often government-supported

### Slide 7: Corporate Debt
- Bilateral and syndicated bank loans
- Public bonds and medium-term note programmes
- Match maturity to assets; stagger refinancing

### Slide 8: Short-Term Corporate Funding
- Credit lines and receivables financing
- Commercial paper: unsecured, discount, short maturity
- Backup liquidity line covers rollover risk

### Slide 9: Bank Funding
- Retail deposits: stable and cheap
- Wholesale: interbank deposits, certificates of deposit, central bank funds
- Repurchase agreements

### Slide 10: Repo Mechanics
- Sell security now, buy back later at higher price
- Economically a collateralised loan
- Cash lent = collateral × (1 − haircut)
- Interest = cash lent × repo rate × days / 360

### Slide 11: What Drives Repo Rates and Haircuts
- Riskier, less liquid, longer collateral: higher
- Longer repo term: higher
- Weaker counterparty: higher
- Scarce collateral in high demand: lower repo rate

### Slide 12: Recap
- Primary = issuer raises money; secondary = investors swap ownership
- Sovereign auctions anchor the yield curve
- CP and repo keep the short-term funding machine running

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - 30-day repo.** Collateral USD 20,000,000, haircut 2%, repo rate 2.80%, 30 days, actual/360.
Cash lent = 20,000,000 × 0.98 = 19,600,000. Interest = 19,600,000 × 0.028 × 30/360 = 45,733.33. Repurchase price = **USD 19,645,733.33**.

**Example 2 - short repo without haircut.** USD 10,000,000 lent for 7 days at 3.00%. Interest = 10,000,000 × 0.03 × 7/360 = **USD 5,833.33**. Show the trap: using 365 days gives 5,753.42.

**Example 3 - who bears the risk?** An issuer sells USD 500 million of bonds. Under a firm commitment the bank buys all 500 million and resells; if only 450 million sell at the target price, the bank holds the 50 million. Under best efforts, the issuer simply raises 450 million.

## Questions to ask students
- In an underwritten offering, who bears the risk that investors do not buy the whole issue? (The underwriting bank.)
- Why do most bonds trade OTC rather than on exchanges? (Huge number of distinct issues, each trading infrequently; dealers supply liquidity.)
- What is the difference between a general obligation bond and a revenue bond? (GO is backed by the government's taxing power; revenue bond only by a specific project's income.)
- Why do rating agencies care whether a CP issuer has a backup credit line? (It covers rollover risk if investors refuse to buy new paper.)
- Would a repo backed by high-yield corporate bonds carry a higher or lower haircut than one backed by government bills? (Higher.)

## Analogies
- Primary versus secondary market: buying a new car from the factory versus buying a used car from a neighbour; only the first sends money to the manufacturer.
- A repo is a pawn shop for bonds: hand over the bond, get slightly less cash than it is worth, pay a fee to get it back.
- On-the-run bonds are the busiest checkout line in the supermarket: many people, quick service, tight spreads.
