## Slides
### Slide 1: The Profit Pool
- Industry structure decides how much profit exists
- Competitive position decides who captures it
- Both feed the forecast
### Slide 2: Classifying Industries
- Principal business activity (largest revenue source)
- Cyclical vs non-cyclical (defensive)
- Goal: a peer group with similar economic drivers
### Slide 3: Concentration Ratio
- CR_N = sum of top N market shares
- Simple, but ignores how share is split
- Two firms at 40/40 and 70/10 look the same
### Slide 4: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index
- HHI = sum of squared shares (in percent)
- 10,000 = monopoly; 10,000 / HHI = equivalent firms
- Merger of shares a and b adds 2ab
### Slide 5: Entry and Rivalry
- Barriers: scale, regulation, brands, network effects
- Rivalry worsens with slow growth and high fixed costs
- High exit barriers keep capacity in the market
### Slide 6: Suppliers, Buyers, Substitutes
- Concentrated suppliers of critical inputs squeeze margins
- Powerful buyers with low switching costs push prices down
- Substitutes from other industries cap prices
### Slide 7: Life Cycle - Early Stages
- Embryonic: slow growth, high prices, high risk
- Growth: rapid demand growth, rising profits
- Little price competition while demand outruns supply
### Slide 8: Life Cycle - Later Stages
- Shakeout: slowing growth, overcapacity, failures
- Mature: consolidated, low growth, strong cash flow
- Decline: shrinking demand, price cutting, exits
### Slide 9: Competitive Strategy
- Cost leadership vs differentiation
- Focus on a niche with either approach
- Only durable advantages protect returns
### Slide 10: Exam Traps
- Use percent, not decimals, in the HHI
- Concentration does not guarantee profits
- Shakeout is not maturity

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - concentration.** Shares 35, 25, 20, 12, 8.
- CR4 = 92%. HHI = 1,225 + 625 + 400 + 144 + 64 = 2,458. Equivalent firms = 4.07.
- Merger of 12% and 8% firms: HHI rises by 2 x 12 x 8 = 192 to 2,650.

**Example 2 - concentration ratio blind spot.** Industry A: two firms at 40% and 40% plus twenty firms at 1%. Industry B: 70% and 10% plus twenty at 1%.
- Both CR2 = 80%.
- HHI A = 1,600 + 1,600 + 20 = 3,220. HHI B = 4,900 + 100 + 20 = 5,020. The HHI correctly flags B as more dominated.

## Questions to ask students
- Shares are 30, 30, 20, 10, 10. What is the HHI? (2,400.)
- Which life-cycle stage typically shows intense price cutting as growth slows and capacity is excess? (Shakeout.)
- How do high switching costs for customers affect industry profitability? (They reduce buyer power and deter entry, supporting profits.)
- Is video conferencing a rival or a substitute for airlines? (A substitute - it comes from a different industry.)
- Why might a concentrated industry still earn poor returns? (Undifferentiated products and high fixed costs can trigger price wars.)

## Analogies
- The five forces are five hands reaching into the same pie: the more of them that are strong, the less pie the industry keeps.
- The life cycle is like a gold rush: few prospectors at first, a crowd during the boom, a shakeout when the easy gold runs out, then a few big mining companies.
- The HHI squares shares the way a magnifying glass enlarges the biggest objects most.

## Recording tips
- Use Example 2 to show why the HHI beats the concentration ratio - it lands better than definitions.
- Draw the life cycle as an S-curve with annotations for prices, profits and capex at each stage.
- Ask students to pick an industry they know and score the five forces live; it keeps the segment practical.
