## Slides
### Slide 1: The Balance Sheet Is Not a Valuation
- Resources controlled and claims against them at one date
- Internally created brands and talent usually absent
- Book equity is an accounting residual
### Slide 2: Current vs Non-Current
- Current: realised or settled within one year or operating cycle, if longer
- Current portion of long-term debt is current
- Working capital = current assets - current liabilities
### Slide 3: Measurement Bases
- Historical cost (PP&E, most inventory)
- Amortised cost (debt held to collect)
- Fair value (trading securities, derivatives)
### Slide 4: Intangibles and Goodwill
- Purchased identifiable intangibles recognised
- Internally generated brands generally not recognised
- Goodwill = price paid - fair value of net identifiable assets; impairment-tested
### Slide 5: Financial Assets
- Amortised cost: hold to collect; fair value changes ignored
- FVOCI: unrealised gains and losses to OCI
- FVPL: unrealised gains and losses to net income
### Slide 6: Equity
- Contributed capital, retained earnings, treasury shares
- Accumulated OCI
- Non-controlling interest
### Slide 7: Common-Size Balance Sheet
- Every line divided by total assets
- Compares firms of different sizes and trends over time
- Highlights concentration, e.g. goodwill share of assets
### Slide 8: Liquidity Ratios
- Current ratio = CA / CL
- Quick ratio = (cash + securities + receivables) / CL
- Cash ratio = (cash + securities) / CL
### Slide 9: Solvency Ratios
- Debt-to-equity = total debt / equity
- Debt-to-assets = total debt / total assets
- Debt means interest-bearing borrowings
### Slide 10: Ashby Retail Results
- Current ratio 1.20, quick ratio 0.60
- Debt-to-equity 0.84
- Goodwill 20% of assets: impairment risk to equity
### Slide 11: Key Takeaways
- Classification drives liquidity analysis
- Measurement bases differ line by line
- Common-size first, ratios second

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Ashby Retail.** Cash 40, receivables 110, inventory 150, PP&E 500, goodwill 200; payables 120, short-term debt 80, accrued 50, long-term debt 300, equity 450 (USD millions).
- Total assets 1,000; CA 300; CL 250.
- Current ratio 300/250 = **1.20**; quick ratio 150/250 = **0.60**.
- Debt-to-equity 380/450 = **0.84**; tangible equity 250, tangible leverage 1.52.

**Example 2 - Tangible book value per share.** Equity 800, goodwill 150, other intangibles 50, 40 shares (millions). Book value per share = 20.00; tangible book value per share = (800 - 200) / 40 = **15.00**.

**Example 3 - Classify the gain.** A company holds a bond to collect and sell (FVOCI) and a trading equity portfolio (FVPL). Each rises USD 5m. Net income rises 5 (trading only); OCI rises 5 (bond).

## Questions to ask students
- A 10-year loan has USD 20m due next year. Where does that USD 20m go? (Current liabilities, as current portion of long-term debt.)
- Why is inventory excluded from the quick ratio? (It is the least liquid current asset and may need discounting to sell.)
- Can a company recognise its own internally built brand as an asset? (Generally no.)
- Where do unrealised gains on an FVPL security go? (Net income.)
- Is goodwill amortised under IFRS? (No; it is tested for impairment.)

## Analogies
- Book value is like the price you paid for your house ten years ago: accurate history, poor guide to today's value.
- The quick ratio is the "fire drill" test: if bills came due tomorrow, what could you pay without holding a clearance sale of inventory?
- Goodwill is the premium you pay for a famous restaurant over the value of its tables and ovens; if the customers leave, that premium evaporates.

## Recording tips
- Show the common-size percentages side by side for two fictional retailers so students see why scaling matters.
- Students often confuse FVOCI and FVPL; put a simple three-row table on screen and keep it visible.
- Pause on the current portion of long-term debt; it appears in many exam distractors.
