## Slides
### Slide 1: Real Estate and Infrastructure
- Tangible, long-lived, income-producing assets
- Steady cash flows, inflation linkage, diversification
- Costs: illiquidity, lot size, transaction costs, appraisal-based values

### Slide 2: The Real Estate Grid
- Private equity: direct ownership, private funds
- Private debt: mortgages, construction loans
- Public equity: REITs, real estate operating companies
- Public debt: MBS, mortgage REITs

### Slide 3: REITs
- Listed owners of income-producing property
- Distribute most taxable income; usually untaxed at corporate level
- Liquid and small ticket size, but trade like equities short term

### Slide 4: Property Types
- Residential, office, retail, industrial/logistics, hospitality
- Short leases = income reacts quickly = more cyclical
- Hotels most cyclical (nightly "leases")

### Slide 5: Three Valuation Approaches
- Income: direct capitalisation or DCF
- Cost: replacement cost less depreciation and obsolescence
- Sales comparison: adjusted prices of similar properties

### Slide 6: Net Operating Income
- Rent - vacancy and collection loss + other income - operating expenses
- Excludes interest, depreciation and income taxes
- The cash the property itself generates

### Slide 7: Direct Capitalisation
- Value = NOI1 / cap rate
- Cap rate approximately r - g
- Higher cap rate -> lower value

### Slide 8: Infrastructure Categories
- Economic: transport, utilities, communications
- Social: schools, hospitals, social housing; often availability payments
- Greenfield (new build) vs brownfield (operating asset)

### Slide 9: Infrastructure Access and Risks
- Direct, unlisted funds, listed companies, public-private partnerships
- Construction, operating, regulatory/political, demand risk
- Often high leverage

### Slide 10: Recap
- Classify any real estate exposure on the grid
- Build NOI carefully, then divide by the cap rate
- Greenfield = more risk, more expected return

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1: Office building.** Gross potential rent 2,000,000; vacancy 5%; other income 50,000; operating expenses 600,000; cap rate 6.5%.
- Vacancy loss 100,000. Effective gross income 1,950,000. NOI 1,350,000.
- Value = 1,350,000 / 0.065 = 20,769,231.
- Extension: if 1,350,000 is current NOI growing at 2% and required return is 8.5%, NOI1 = 1,377,000, cap rate = 6.5%, value = 21,184,615.

**Example 2: Sales comparison via cap rate.** A comparable sold for 15m with NOI 900,000, implying a 6% cap rate. Subject property NOI 1.2m -> value 1.2 / 0.06 = 20m.

**Example 3: Cap rate sensitivity.** NOI 1,350,000 at a 7.5% cap rate gives 18,000,000; at 5.5% gives 24,545,455. A 2-point cap rate swing changes value by about 6.5m.

## Questions to ask students
- Is mortgage interest deducted when computing NOI? (No, NOI is before financing costs.)
- Which property type is most cyclical and why? (Hotels, because room rates reset daily.)
- What happens to value when cap rates rise? (Value falls.)
- Where on the grid is an equity REIT? (Public equity.)
- Which infrastructure project carries construction risk? (Greenfield.)
- What is an availability payment? (A government payment for keeping an asset available, regardless of usage.)

## Analogies
- A cap rate is the property's earnings yield: like the inverse of a P/E ratio for a building.
- Lease length is a shock absorber: long leases smooth out economic bumps, nightly hotel rates feel every pothole.
- Brownfield infrastructure is buying a running restaurant with a customer list; greenfield is building one in a new neighbourhood and hoping diners come.

## Recording tips
- Put the NOI build-up in a vertical table on screen so students see each line being added or subtracted.
- Stress that NOI excludes financing; show the trap number (with interest deducted) and cross it out.
- Link the cap rate formula back to the constant-growth model from equity valuation; it reduces memorisation.
