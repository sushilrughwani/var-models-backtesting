## Slides
### Slide 1: Two investors, one decision
- Rational model: maximise expected utility with all information
- Real investors: shortcuts and feelings
- Biases are systematic, so they can be anticipated
### Slide 2: Two families of bias
- Cognitive errors: faulty reasoning, memory, processing
- Emotional biases: impulses and feelings
- Cognitive → moderate; emotional → adapt
### Slide 3: When to moderate or adapt
- Biases threatening the client's goals: moderate more
- Wealthy client with small stakes: adapting is affordable
### Slide 4: Belief perseverance
- Conservatism, confirmation, representativeness
- Illusion of control, hindsight
### Slide 5: Information processing
- Anchoring and adjustment, mental accounting
- Framing, availability
### Slide 6: Emotional biases (1)
- Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as gains please
- Disposition effect: sell winners, hold losers
- Overconfidence: prediction and certainty forms
### Slide 7: Emotional biases (2)
- Self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion
- Regret aversion can lead to herding
### Slide 8: Case - Priya
- 40% in former employer's stock
- Overconfidence, illusion of control, anchoring, loss aversion, endowment
- Adapt with staged reduction; educate on anchoring
### Slide 9: From biases to markets
- Bubbles: overconfidence, confirmation, herding
- Momentum: under-reaction and herding
- Value effect: preference for glamour stocks
### Slide 10: Recap
- Classify, then decide moderate or adapt; know each bias by its behaviour

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Framing demo.** Gain frame: sure USD 900 versus 90% chance of USD 1,000 (expected value 900) - most choose the sure gain. Loss frame: sure loss of USD 900 versus 90% chance of losing USD 1,000 (expected value −900) - most gamble. Same expected values, opposite choices: risk aversion in gains, risk seeking in losses.
2. **Bias sorting drill.** Write six statements and have students classify: (a) "I'll wait until it gets back to what I paid" - anchoring/loss aversion; (b) "Every article I read confirms my view" - confirmation; (c) "I knew the crash was coming" - hindsight; (d) "This is my fun money, so I can gamble it" - mental accounting; (e) "I'll rebalance next year" repeated for five years - status quo; (f) "Plane crashes are common, I saw three on the news" - availability.
3. **Priya case.** Work the four steps from the notes: identify, classify, assess stakes, recommend. Final answer: adapt (staged sale over two years) plus education on anchoring.

## Questions to ask students
- Is overconfidence a cognitive or emotional bias? (Emotional.)
- Which bias explains holding a losing stock to "get back to even"? (Loss aversion, often with anchoring.)
- Why adapt rather than moderate an emotional bias? (Feelings rarely change through explanation; fighting them can make the client abandon the plan.)
- A client keeps a 30% cash "safety" account while carrying credit-card debt. Which bias? (Mental accounting.)
- Name a bias linked to market bubbles. (Overconfidence, confirmation or herding/regret aversion.)

## Analogies
- Cognitive errors are like a wrong map - hand over a better map and the traveller corrects course. Emotional biases are like fear of flying - showing statistics rarely helps; you plan a route by train instead.
- The endowment effect is the "my house is worth more than the neighbours'" feeling.
- Anchoring is a ship tied to the dock: it can drift a little, but it cannot sail away from the original number.

## Recording tips
- Run the framing demo live with a poll; the reversal is the most memorable moment of the lecture.
- Provide a one-page grid of every bias (family, behaviour, fix) as an on-screen recap.
- Slow down on overconfidence's classification - many candidates lose marks on that single point.
