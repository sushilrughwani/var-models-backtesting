## Slides
### Slide 1: Why plan first
- Theory tells us which portfolios are efficient
- Planning tells us which efficient portfolio fits this client
- The IPS is the written record of that fit
### Slide 2: Inside an IPS
- Client description and purpose
- Duties of client and manager
- Objectives and constraints
- Review schedule, benchmark, rebalancing rules
### Slide 3: Risk objectives
- Absolute (max loss, max σ) or relative (tracking error)
- Ability: horizon, wealth vs needs, income stability
- Willingness: psychological, from questionnaires and behaviour
### Slide 4: Resolving conflicts
- Follow the lower of ability and willingness
- Low willingness, high ability: educate, but respect the client
- High willingness, low ability: ability wins
### Slide 5: Required return
- $R = (1+s)(1+\pi)(1+c) - 1$
- s = spending / portfolio value
- Additive version is a slight underestimate
### Slide 6: Constraints - TTLLU
- Time horizon, Taxes, Liquidity, Legal/regulatory, Unique circumstances
- Return and risk are objectives, not constraints
### Slide 7: ESG approaches
- Negative screening, positive / best-in-class screening
- Thematic, impact investing, engagement
### Slide 8: Strategic asset allocation
- Long-term policy mix meeting the IPS
- Asset classes: homogeneous, distinct, investable
- Ranges permit tactical deviations
### Slide 9: Building the portfolio
- Security selection within classes
- Risk budgeting for active risk
- Core (passive) plus satellite (active)
### Slide 10: Recap
- IPS, lower-of rule, compounded required return, TTLLU, SAA tested against objectives

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Required return.** Portfolio USD 1,500,000; spending USD 60,000; inflation 2.5%; fees 0.5%. s = 4.0%. Required = 1.04 × 1.025 × 1.005 − 1 = 7.13% (additive 7.0%).
2. **Allocation test.** Equities 9%/18%, bonds 4%/6%, ρ = 0.2, σ cap 14%. 60/40: 7.0%, 11.52% (fails return). 70/30: 7.5%, 13.08% (passes both). 80/20: 8.0%, 14.69% (fails risk). Choose 70/30.
3. **Ability vs willingness.** A 35-year-old surgeon with USD 2 million, stable income and small spending needs scores very low on a risk questionnaire. Ability: high. Willingness: low. Recommendation: below-average risk tolerance overall, plus education about the cost of being too conservative.

## Questions to ask students
- Is "liquidity" an objective or a constraint? (Constraint.)
- A client wants aggressive growth but depends on the portfolio to pay next year's living costs. Which dimension wins? (Ability - which is low.)
- Why compound rather than add spending, inflation and fees? (Each applies to a base that has already grown; adding understates the requirement.)
- What is the difference between strategic and tactical allocation? (SAA is the long-term policy mix; TAA is a temporary deviation within allowed ranges.)
- Where would a client's wish to exclude tobacco stocks go in the IPS? (Unique circumstances / ESG preferences.)

## Analogies
- The IPS is a flight plan: destination (objectives), aircraft limits and weather (constraints), and check-in points (reviews).
- Ability versus willingness is like a car that can do 200 km/h driven by a nervous passenger - you drive at the speed the passenger tolerates, and you never exceed what the car can handle.
- Strategic allocation is the recipe; tactical allocation is adding a pinch more salt tonight.

## Recording tips
- Put the TTLLU acronym on screen and keep it visible during the case study.
- Emphasise the "lower of the two" rule twice; it is a frequent exam point.
- Show the allocation-test table being filled in live so students practise the screening logic, not just the arithmetic.
