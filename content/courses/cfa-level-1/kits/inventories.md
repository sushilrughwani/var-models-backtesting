## Slides
### Slide 1: Same Goods, Different Profit
- Goods available = beginning inventory + purchases
- Split between COGS (income statement) and ending inventory (balance sheet)
- The method decides the split
### Slide 2: The Methods
- FIFO: oldest costs expensed first
- LIFO: newest costs expensed first (US GAAP only)
- Weighted average: average cost per unit
### Slide 3: Penrose Supply Data
- 100 units at 10; buy 200 at 12; buy 100 at 15
- Sell 250 units at 25
- Goods available 4,900 for 400 units
### Slide 4: Penrose Results
- FIFO COGS 2,800; ending inventory 2,100
- LIFO COGS 3,300; ending inventory 1,600
- Weighted average COGS 3,062.50; ending inventory 1,837.50
### Slide 5: Rising Prices - LIFO vs FIFO
- LIFO: higher COGS, lower profit, lower tax, higher CFO
- LIFO: lower inventory, current ratio and equity
- FIFO: the reverse
### Slide 6: LIFO Liquidation
- Selling more than purchased dips into old cheap layers
- Temporary margin boost
- Strip it out of sustainable earnings
### Slide 7: LIFO Reserve
- FIFO inventory = LIFO inventory + LIFO reserve
- FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS - change in LIFO reserve
- Equity adjustment: reserve × (1 - t)
### Slide 8: Write-Downs
- IFRS: lower of cost and NRV; reversals allowed up to cost
- US GAAP: LCNRV (FIFO, average), LCM (LIFO); no reversals
- Write-down is an expense in the period
### Slide 9: Inventory Ratios
- Turnover = COGS / average inventory
- Days on hand = 365 / turnover
- LIFO inflates turnover in rising prices
### Slide 10: Key Takeaways
- Know each method's COGS and inventory direction
- Convert LIFO to FIFO before comparing firms
- Reversal rules differ by framework

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Penrose Supply.** Beginning 100 at 10; purchases 200 at 12 and 100 at 15; 250 units sold at 25.
- FIFO: COGS = 1,000 + 1,800 = **2,800**; inventory 2,100; gross profit 3,450.
- LIFO: COGS = 1,500 + 1,800 = **3,300**; inventory 1,600; gross profit 2,950.
- Weighted average: 12.25 per unit; COGS **3,062.50**; inventory 1,837.50.
- LIFO reserve = 2,100 - 1,600 = 500.

**Example 2 - LIFO to FIFO COGS.** LIFO COGS 8,000; LIFO reserve rose from 1,200 to 1,500. FIFO COGS = 8,000 - 300 = **7,700**. Show the trap of subtracting the ending reserve (6,500).

**Example 3 - IFRS reversal.** Cost 80,000; written down to NRV 70,000 in year 1 (expense 10,000). In year 2 NRV rises to 84,000. Reverse 10,000; carrying amount = **80,000**, capped at cost. Under US GAAP it stays at 70,000.

## Questions to ask students
- In rising prices, which method gives the highest ending inventory? (FIFO.)
- Does LIFO increase or decrease operating cash flow in rising prices? (Increases it, through lower taxes.)
- What is the LIFO reserve? (FIFO inventory minus LIFO inventory.)
- Can an IFRS company reverse an inventory write-down? (Yes, up to the original cost.)
- Why might a LIFO firm's turnover look unusually high? (Old, low costs in the inventory denominator.)

## Analogies
- FIFO is a supermarket milk shelf: the oldest cartons are pushed to the front and sold first.
- LIFO is a pile of gravel: the truck dumps new gravel on top and customers scoop from the top, so the bottom layer can sit there for decades.
- The LIFO reserve is the "hidden savings jar": the value the balance sheet is not showing because old costs are still parked in inventory.

## Recording tips
- Draw the units as stacked layers with prices; move them physically into COGS for each method.
- Students confuse the change in reserve with the reserve itself; repeat "change for COGS, level for inventory".
- Always state the price direction before asking which method gives higher profit.
