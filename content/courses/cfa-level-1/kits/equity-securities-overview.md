## Slides
### Slide 1: The Residual Claim
- Paid last, after creditors and preference shareholders
- Unlimited upside, limited liability
- Usually votes on directors and major actions
### Slide 2: Statutory vs Cumulative Voting
- Statutory: one vote per share per seat
- Cumulative: shares x seats, can be concentrated
- Cumulative voting protects minority holders
### Slide 3: Share Classes and Embedded Options
- Dual classes separate votes from economics
- Callable shares: issuer can buy back
- Putable shares: holder can sell back
### Slide 4: Preference Shares
- Priority for dividends and in liquidation
- Cumulative vs non-cumulative dividends
- Participating and convertible features
### Slide 5: Public vs Private Equity
- Public: liquid, transparent, heavily regulated
- Private: illiquid, long horizon, more control
- Venture capital, buyouts, PIPEs
### Slide 6: Investing Across Borders
- Direct purchase on foreign exchanges
- Depository receipts traded locally
- Funds and ETFs
### Slide 7: Book vs Market Value
- Book: assets minus liabilities (historical)
- Market: price x shares (forward-looking)
- Price-to-book shows value beyond the accounts
### Slide 8: Return on Equity
- ROE = net income / average book equity
- Measures profit on shareholders' accounting capital
- Can be inflated by leverage or buybacks
### Slide 9: Cost of Equity
- The investor's required return; the firm's cost
- Implied estimate: r = D1/P0 + g
- Value is created when ROE exceeds the cost of equity

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - ROE, BVPS, P/B and implied cost of equity.** Net income 48m; equity 380m start, 420m end; 20m shares at USD 60; D0 = 1.20; g = 5%.
- Average equity 400m; ROE = 12.00%.
- BVPS = 21.00; market cap = 1,200m; P/B = 2.86.
- D1 = 1.26; r = 1.26/60 + 0.05 = 7.10%.

**Example 2 - cumulative voting.** Shareholder owns 1,000 shares; five seats up for election.
- Statutory: 1,000 votes for each seat separately.
- Cumulative: 5,000 votes, all can go to one candidate.
- Extension: with 10,000 shares outstanding, guaranteeing one seat out of five needs 10,000/6 + 1 = 1,667 shares (rounded down then plus one).

## Questions to ask students
- Which ranks higher in liquidation, preference or common shares? (Preference shares.)
- A firm skips a dividend on non-cumulative preference shares. Does it owe it later? (No.)
- Why might a founder favour a dual-class structure? (To keep voting control with a smaller economic stake.)
- If D0 = 2.00, g = 4% and P0 = 52, what is the implied cost of equity? (2.08/52 + 0.04 = 8.00%.)
- Can ROE rise while the business gets worse? (Yes, through higher leverage or buybacks shrinking equity.)

## Analogies
- Common shareholders are the last guests at a buffet: they eat only after everyone else, but they get everything left over.
- Cumulative voting is like getting several darts and being allowed to throw them all at one target.
- Book value is the receipt for what was spent building the house; market value is what buyers will pay for it today.

## Recording tips
- Put the preference share variants in a simple two-by-two table; students mix them up otherwise.
- Highlight D1 versus D0 visually in the cost of equity formula - it is the single most common slip.
- Link the cost of equity back to the corporate issuers module on cost of capital so students see the connection.
