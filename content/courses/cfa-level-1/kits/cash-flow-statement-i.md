## Slides
### Slide 1: Profit Is an Opinion, Cash Is a Fact
- Accruals record revenue earned and expenses incurred
- Cash flow statement shows money actually received and paid
- Profitable firms can still run out of cash
### Slide 2: Three Sections
- Operating: customers, suppliers, employees, taxes
- Investing: capex, asset sales, securities bought and sold
- Financing: debt, shares, dividends
### Slide 3: IFRS vs US GAAP
- US GAAP: interest paid/received and dividends received in operating; dividends paid in financing
- IFRS: interest and dividends paid may be operating or financing
- IFRS: interest and dividends received may be operating or investing
### Slide 4: Non-Cash Transactions
- Asset bought with a note to the seller
- Debt converted to equity
- Disclosed separately, excluded from the three sections
### Slide 5: Indirect Method Structure
- Start with net income
- Add non-cash charges; subtract gains, add losses
- Adjust for changes in working capital
### Slide 6: Working-Capital Sign Rules
- Operating asset up: subtract
- Operating liability up: add
- Reverse both for decreases
### Slide 7: Direct Method Lines
- Cash from customers = revenue - change in receivables
- Cash to suppliers = COGS + change in inventory - change in payables
- Same CFO total as indirect method
### Slide 8: Corvin Industries CFO
- 200 + 50 + 10 - 30 + 15 + 20 - 5
- CFO = 260
- Loss on sale added back; proceeds go to investing
### Slide 9: Corvin CFI, CFF and Net Change
- CFI = -120 + 25 = -95
- CFF = +60 - 40 - 30 = -10
- Net change in cash = 155
### Slide 10: Key Takeaways
- Classify correctly, especially interest and dividends
- Master the indirect sign rules
- Direct method shows sources; indirect shows the profit-to-cash bridge

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - Corvin Industries (USD thousands).** NI 200; depreciation 50; loss on sale 10; receivables +30; inventory -15; payables +20; wages payable -5; capex 120; sale proceeds 25; borrowing 60; dividends 40; buyback 30.
- CFO = 200 + 50 + 10 - 30 + 15 + 20 - 5 = **260**.
- CFI = -120 + 25 = **-95**.
- CFF = 60 - 40 - 30 = **-10**.
- Net change = **155**.

**Example 2 - Gain instead of loss.** NI 150; depreciation 40; gain on sale 12; receivables +25; inventory +10; payables +18. CFO = 150 + 40 - 12 - 25 - 10 + 18 = **161**. Show that adding the gain gives the wrong 185.

**Example 3 - Direct method.** Revenue 800; receivables fell from 90 to 70. Cash from customers = 800 - (70 - 90) = **820**. Then: COGS 600, inventory fell 15, payables rose 20, so cash to suppliers = 600 - 15 - 20 = **565**.

## Questions to ask students
- Receivables rose by USD 30. Does that raise or lower CFO? (Lowers it by 30.)
- Why is a loss on sale of equipment added back? (It reduced net income but used no cash; the cash proceeds appear in investing.)
- Where are dividends paid classified under US GAAP? (Financing.)
- A company buys land by issuing bonds to the seller. Which section? (None; disclosed as a non-cash transaction.)
- Do the direct and indirect methods produce different CFO? (No; same total, different presentation.)

## Analogies
- The indirect method is like reconciling your salary to your bank balance: start with what you earned, then adjust for what friends still owe you and the bills you have not yet paid.
- Receivables are an interest-free loan to customers: the sale is booked but the cash is in someone else's pocket.
- The three sections are three wallets: the business wallet (operating), the shopping-for-equipment wallet (investing) and the family-and-bank wallet (financing).

## Recording tips
- Keep a two-column "asset up = subtract / liability up = add" card on screen throughout the indirect method section.
- Students misplace gains and losses; walk through the full journal of an equipment sale once.
- Finish by tying the net change in cash to the balance sheet so students see the statement close.
