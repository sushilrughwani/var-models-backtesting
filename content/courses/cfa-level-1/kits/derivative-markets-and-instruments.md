- 05:00 - Exchange-traded derivatives: standardisation, liquidity, transparency, the CCP and daily margin.
- 09:00 - OTC derivatives: customisation, dealers, counterparty risk.
- 12:00 - Central clearing of standard OTC trades and collateral for bespoke ones.

## Slides
### Slide 1: What Is a Derivative?
- A contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate or event
- Four ingredients: underlying, notional, settlement date(s), payoff rule
- Settlement: physical delivery or cash
### Slide 2: Types of Underlyings
- Assets: equities, bonds, commodities, currencies
- Rates and indices: interest rates, inflation, equity indices
- Events: credit default (credit derivatives)
### Slide 3: Exchange-Traded Derivatives
- Standardised size, expiry and settlement
- Concentrated liquidity and public prices
- Central counterparty is buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer
- Daily margining limits credit losses
### Slide 4: OTC Derivatives
- Privately negotiated, usually with a dealer
- Fully customisable amount, date and underlying
- Less transparent and less liquid per contract
- Counterparty credit risk is the key concern
### Slide 5: Exchange vs OTC at a Glance
- Standardised vs custom
- Cleared by CCP vs bilateral (or centrally cleared)
- Transparent vs private
- Easy to close vs harder to exit
### Slide 6: Central Clearing After the Crisis
- Standard OTC swaps now novated to a CCP
- Margin posted by both sides
- Keeps flexibility, reduces counterparty risk
- Bespoke trades use collateral agreements
### Slide 7: Benefits of Derivatives
- Transfer risk to those willing to bear it
- Price discovery in liquid futures markets
- Low transaction costs and capital needs
- Easy shorting and quick exposure changes
### Slide 8: Leverage in Numbers
- Notional USD 500,000, margin USD 25,000: 20 times leverage
- 2% index rise: USD 10,000 gain = 40% on margin
- 5% fall: margin wiped out
### Slide 9: Risks of Derivatives
- Leverage magnifies losses
- Complexity and model risk
- Basis risk in imperfect hedges
- Counterparty, liquidity and systemic risk
### Slide 10: Key Takeaways
- Derivatives redistribute risk, they do not create value from nothing
- Exchange: standard, transparent, cleared
- OTC: flexible, private, credit risk managed by clearing and collateral

## Whiteboard examples
1. **Leverage.** Index futures notional USD 500,000, initial margin USD 25,000. Index rises 2%. Gain = 500,000 × 0.02 = 10,000. Return on margin = 10,000 / 25,000 = 40%. Cash purchase return = 2%. Leverage = 20 times. If the index falls 5%, loss = 25,000, the entire margin.
2. **Venue choice.** A corporate treasurer must hedge EUR 7.3 million of receipts due on 17 March, a date that does not match any futures expiry. Solution: an OTC currency forward with a bank, because the amount and date can be tailored; accept counterparty risk or ask for a collateral agreement. An index manager hedging a standard index for the June expiry should use exchange-traded futures.

## Questions to ask students
- Who is the counterparty to your trade on a futures exchange? (The clearinghouse / CCP.)
- Name one advantage and one disadvantage of OTC contracts. (Customisation; counterparty risk or lower transparency/liquidity.)
- Does the CCP take market risk? (No, it holds offsetting positions; it manages credit risk with margin.)
- With 5% margin, what percentage move in the underlying wipes out the margin? (5%, since leverage is 20 times.)
- Why might futures markets lead the cash market in price discovery? (Lower costs and higher liquidity attract informed traders.)

## Analogies
- A CCP is like an escrow agent at a house sale: neither side has to trust the other, only the agent, who holds deposits from both.
- A derivative is a side bet on the weather: the bet does not change the rain, it only changes who pays when it rains.
- Exchange contracts are off-the-rack suits (cheap, available, standard sizes); OTC contracts are tailored suits (perfect fit, but you depend on the tailor).

## Recording tips
- Show the exchange-vs-OTC comparison as a two-column table on screen and keep it visible while discussing clearing.
- Students often think derivatives are only for speculation; spend a minute on the farmer/baker hedge to reframe them as insurance tools.
- Work the leverage numbers slowly; this sets up margin and mark-to-market in the futures lesson.
