## Slides
### Slide 1: What a Multiple Does
- Value per unit of earnings, book, sales or EBITDA
- Quick, intuitive and market-based
- Hides all the assumptions inside one number
### Slide 2: Two Ways to Use Multiples
- Comparables: relative to peers, industry or history
- Fundamentals: the multiple a model justifies
- Relative cheapness is not absolute cheapness
### Slide 3: Justified P/E
- Leading: (1 - b) / (r - g)
- Trailing: (1 - b)(1 + g) / (r - g)
- Higher payout, higher g, lower r: higher P/E
### Slide 4: Justified P/B
- P/B = (ROE - g) / (r - g)
- ROE above r justifies P/B above 1
- Best for banks and insurers
### Slide 5: P/E, P/B, P/S Compared
- P/E: intuitive, but earnings can be negative or distorted
- P/B: stable, but ignores intangibles
- P/S: hard to manipulate, but ignores profitability
### Slide 6: Enterprise Value
- EV = equity + debt + preferred + minority interest - cash
- The value of the whole firm to all capital providers
- Pair with EBITDA, EBIT or sales
### Slide 7: From EV to Price per Share
- Implied EV = peer multiple x target EBITDA
- Equity = EV - debt - preferred - minority + cash
- Divide by shares outstanding
### Slide 8: Asset-Based Valuation
- Fair value of assets - fair value of liabilities
- Suits holding, resource, real estate and financial firms
- Poor fit for intangible-heavy businesses
### Slide 9: Choosing the Approach
- Stable dividends: DDM; no dividends: FCFE or multiples
- Different leverage: EV multiples
- Liquidation or asset-heavy: asset-based
### Slide 10: Exam Traps
- Match numerator and denominator (equity vs firm)
- Add cash when moving from EV to equity
- Trailing P/E includes the (1 + g) factor

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1 - EV/EBITDA.** EBITDA 120m, peer EV/EBITDA 8.0x, debt 300m, cash 60m, 40m shares.
- EV = 960; equity = 960 - 300 + 60 = 720; per share = USD 18.00.
- P/E cross-check: 14 x 2.10 = 29.40. Discuss why the two differ.

**Example 2 - justified multiples.** Payout 40%, r = 9%, g = 5%.
- Leading P/E = 0.40/0.04 = 10.0x; trailing = 10.0 x 1.05 = 10.5x.
- P/B with ROE 14%, r 10%, g 6%: (0.14 - 0.06)/(0.04) = 2.0x.

**Example 3 - asset-based.** Book assets 500m; real estate +80m above book; inventory -15m; liabilities 260m; 40m shares.
- Adjusted equity = 305m, or USD 7.63 per share: a floor well below earnings-based values.

## Questions to ask students
- Why pair EV with EBITDA rather than net income? (Both are measured before payments to debt holders.)
- A firm has an ROE equal to its cost of equity. What is its justified P/B? (1.0.)
- Which multiple is most useful for a loss-making start-up? (P/S, since earnings and EBITDA may be negative.)
- If all peers are overvalued, what does a "cheap" P/E relative to peers tell you? (Only that it is cheap relatively, not that it is undervalued.)
- Why is asset-based valuation weak for a software company? (Most value is intangible and not on the balance sheet.)

## Analogies
- Comparables are like pricing a house from recent sales on the same street; if the whole street is in a bubble, your estimate is too.
- Enterprise value is the price of buying the whole house including its mortgage, minus any cash left in the drawer.
- Asset-based valuation is the auction value of the furniture: a useful floor, but it misses what the family earns.

## Recording tips
- Draw the EV bridge as a waterfall chart: equity up to EV and back down again; it ends the sign confusion.
- Derive the justified P/E from the Gordon formula on screen in two lines; students then never need to memorise it.
- Close by tying all three valuation approaches together: DDM, multiples and asset-based values should be triangulated.
