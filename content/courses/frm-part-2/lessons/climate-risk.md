## Learning goals
- Distinguish physical risk (acute and chronic) from transition risk and give examples of each.
- Explain how climate drivers are transmitted into credit, market, liquidity, operational and insurance risk.
- Describe the main climate scenario families and which risks dominate in each.
- Calculate the effect of a carbon price on a borrower's earnings, leverage and interest coverage.
- Explain why climate risk is hard to measure and how banks embed it in governance and risk appetite.

## Explanation

### Two families of climate risk
Climate change matters to a risk manager not as an environmental topic but because it changes cash flows, collateral values and default rates.

**Physical risk** is damage from the climate itself.
- *Acute*: event-driven shocks such as floods, hurricanes, wildfires and heatwaves.
- *Chronic*: slow shifts such as rising sea levels, higher average temperatures and changing rainfall, which erode productivity and property values over decades.

**Transition risk** arises from the move to a low-carbon economy.
- *Policy and legal*: carbon taxes, emissions trading, efficiency standards, litigation against high emitters.
- *Technology*: cheaper renewables or batteries strand fossil-fuel assets.
- *Market and reputation*: consumers, investors and employees shift away from carbon-intensive firms.

The two trade off. A fast transition lowers long-run physical damage but raises near-term transition losses; a slow transition does the opposite.

### Transmission channels
Climate drivers do not create a new risk category; they amplify existing ones.

- **Credit risk:** lower borrower earnings and damaged collateral raise default probabilities and loss given default. A mortgage book in a flood zone or a loan to a coal miner are standard examples.
- **Market risk:** sudden repricing of carbon-intensive equities and bonds when policy or expectations change.
- **Liquidity risk:** clients draw on credit lines after disasters; assets that become hard to value become hard to sell.
- **Operational risk:** extreme weather disrupts offices, data centres and suppliers.
- **Underwriting risk:** insurers face larger and more frequent claims.

These run through **micro channels** (individual firms and households) and **macro channels** (lower productivity, higher inflation, weaker growth).

### Why it is hard to measure
Climate risk differs from the risks that backward-looking models handle well:
- **Long horizons:** effects play out over decades, beyond typical planning and loan maturities.
- **No useful history:** past data do not contain the future climate or the policies to come.
- **Non-linearity and tipping points:** damage can accelerate sharply once thresholds are crossed.
- **Deep uncertainty:** outcomes depend on politics and technology, not just physics.
- **Data gaps:** emissions and asset-location data for borrowers are often missing or estimated.

### Scenario analysis
Because history is a poor guide, the main tool is **scenario analysis**: coherent narratives of how the climate, policy and economy might evolve, translated into macro variables, sector shocks and finally exposures. Supervisory scenario sets typically include:
- **Orderly:** early, gradual policy action. Moderate transition costs, limited physical damage.
- **Disorderly:** action is delayed or uneven, then abrupt. **Highest transition risk.**
- **Hot house world:** current policies only. **Highest physical risk**, especially late in the horizon.

Scenarios are not forecasts; they explore vulnerabilities. A common metric of transition exposure is the **weighted average carbon intensity**:

$$
\text{WACI} = \sum_i w_i \,\frac{E_i}{R_i}
$$

where $w_i$ is the portfolio weight of issuer $i$, $E_i$ its greenhouse gas emissions and $R_i$ its revenue.

### Governance and risk management
Supervisors expect boards to own climate risk, banks to include it in risk appetite statements, credit policies and capital planning, and firms to disclose governance, strategy, risk management and metrics in line with international disclosure standards.

## Worked example
A cement producer has EBITDA of USD 500 million, debt of USD 1,900 million, annual interest of USD 95 million and emissions of 2 million tonnes of CO₂. A transition scenario introduces a carbon price of USD 100 per tonne. The firm can pass 40% of the cost on to customers.

**Step 1 - gross carbon cost.** $2{,}000{,}000 \times 100 = $ USD 200 million.

**Step 2 - absorbed cost.** $200 \times (1 - 0.40) = $ USD 120 million, which is 24% of EBITDA.

**Step 3 - stressed EBITDA.** $500 - 120 = $ USD 380 million.

**Step 4 - leverage.** Before: $1{,}900/500 = 3.8\times$. After: $1{,}900/380 = 5.0\times$.

**Step 5 - interest coverage.** Before: $500/95 = 5.26\times$. After: $380/95 = 4.0\times$.

**Conclusion:** leverage jumps from 3.8x to **5.0x**, likely breaching a typical covenant and justifying a downgrade of the internal rating and a higher probability of default in the stress scenario.

## Common exam traps
- Classifying a carbon tax as physical risk or a flood as transition risk.
- Treating climate risk as a separate risk type instead of a driver of credit, market, liquidity and operational risk.
- Assuming the hot house scenario has the highest transition risk; it has the highest physical risk.
- Reading scenarios as forecasts with probabilities attached.
- Ignoring pass-through: only the cost a firm cannot pass on hits its margins.

## Key takeaways
- Physical risk comes from acute events and chronic shifts; transition risk comes from policy, technology and market changes.
- Climate risk flows through existing risk types via micro and macro channels.
- Long horizons, missing history, tipping points and data gaps limit standard models.
- Scenario analysis is the core tool: disorderly paths stress transition risk, hot house paths stress physical risk.
- Carbon-price stresses work through absorbed costs into earnings, leverage and default probability.
