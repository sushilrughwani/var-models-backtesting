title: "Operational Risk Governance and Framework"
---

## Learning goals
- Define operational risk and classify losses into the standard Basel event types.
- Explain how the board, senior management and the three lines of defence share responsibility for operational risk.
- Translate a qualitative risk appetite statement into measurable limits and escalation triggers.
- Distinguish the core building blocks of a sound operational risk management framework.

## Explanation

### What operational risk is (and is not)
Market and credit risk are taken on deliberately because they earn a return. Operational risk is different: it is the by-product of simply running a business. A bank faces it whenever a process breaks, a person errs or acts dishonestly, a system fails, or something external (a flood, a cyberattack, a fraudster) hits it. The regulatory definition includes **legal risk** but leaves out **strategic risk** and **reputational risk**, even though operational failures often damage reputation.

Losses are grouped into seven event types so that firms can compare data internally and across the industry:

1. Internal fraud
2. External fraud
3. Employment practices and workplace safety
4. Clients, products and business practices (mis-selling, fiduciary breaches)
5. Damage to physical assets
6. Business disruption and system failures
7. Execution, delivery and process management

Historically, "clients, products and business practices" has produced the largest share of loss amounts (large conduct fines and settlements), while "execution, delivery and process management" produces the largest number of events: many small errors.

### Governance: who owns the risk
A sound framework starts at the top. The **board** approves the framework and the risk appetite, and makes sure the firm has a strong risk culture. **Senior management** turns that into policies, processes and systems, and allocates resources and accountability.

Day-to-day responsibility is split across the **three lines of defence**:

- **First line - the business.** Business units and support functions own the risks they create. They identify and assess risks, run the controls and report incidents.
- **Second line - independent risk management.** The operational risk function designs the framework and tools (RCSA, KRIs, scenarios), challenges the first line's assessments, aggregates the risk profile and reports to the board risk committee.
- **Third line - internal audit.** Audit gives independent assurance to the board that the first two lines are working as designed.

The key word for the second line is **challenge**: a second line that only collects first-line self-assessments without questioning them is a post office, not a control.

### Risk appetite and tolerance
A risk appetite statement says how much operational risk the firm is willing to accept in pursuit of its goals. Because nobody wants operational losses, appetite is usually framed as **tolerance**: zero appetite for certain behaviours (internal fraud, sanctions breaches) and quantitative limits elsewhere, such as:

$$
\text{Utilisation} = \frac{\text{Losses to date}}{\text{Loss limit}}
$$

where the loss limit is often expressed as a share of gross income or of capital. Firms set an early warning **trigger** below the limit (for example at 80%) so that management can act before the limit is breached.

### Framework building blocks
A complete framework links together:

- **Identification and assessment** - RCSA, KRIs, loss data, scenario analysis (next lesson).
- **Change management** - new products, systems and outsourcing are risk-assessed before launch.
- **Monitoring and reporting** - a regular, forward-looking risk profile to senior management and the board.
- **Control and mitigation** - preventive and detective controls, insurance, and the decision to accept, reduce, transfer or avoid a risk.
- **Resilience and continuity** - the ability to keep critical operations running through disruption.
- **Disclosure** - public reporting that lets outsiders judge the approach.

A strong **risk culture** ties these together: staff speak up about near misses, incentives do not reward hidden risk taking, and lessons from incidents feed back into controls.

## Worked example
A bank has annual gross income of USD 2,000 million. The board sets an operational loss tolerance of 2% of gross income, with an amber trigger at 80% of the limit. By the end of September, booked operational losses total USD 28 million.

1. Loss limit: $0.02 \times 2{,}000 = 40$, so USD 40 million.
2. Amber trigger: $0.80 \times 40 = 32$, so USD 32 million.
3. Utilisation: $28 / 40 = 0.70$, or 70%.

Status: **green, 70% of the limit used, USD 4 million of headroom before the amber trigger**. If a single USD 6 million mis-selling settlement were then booked, losses would reach USD 34 million (85%), crossing the amber trigger and requiring escalation to the risk committee with a remediation plan.

Qualitatively, suppose the same bank discovers a trader concealing losses. Because appetite for internal fraud is zero, the event is escalated immediately regardless of its size: the first line suspends the trader and fixes the control gap, the second line assesses whether similar gaps exist elsewhere, and internal audit later tests the fix.

## Common exam traps
- Including reputational or strategic risk in the definition of operational risk; legal risk is in, the other two are out.
- Saying the second line "owns" operational risk. The first line owns it; the second line oversees and challenges.
- Confusing internal audit with the second line. Audit is independent assurance, not risk management.
- Mixing up the event types with the largest loss amounts (clients, products and business practices) and the largest event counts (execution, delivery and process management).
- Treating risk appetite as a target to be used up. It is a ceiling, and triggers below the limit exist to prompt action early.

## Key takeaways
- Operational risk arises from processes, people, systems and external events, and includes legal risk.
- Seven standard event types allow consistent loss classification and benchmarking.
- The board sets appetite and culture; senior management implements; the three lines split ownership, oversight and assurance.
- Appetite statements combine zero-tolerance areas with quantitative limits and early warning triggers.
- A framework is only as good as its links: identification feeds mitigation, monitoring and reporting, and incidents feed back into controls.
