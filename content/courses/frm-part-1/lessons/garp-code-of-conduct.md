## Learning goals
- Describe the structure of the GARP Code of Conduct: its principles and its professional standards.
- Explain what professional integrity requires of a risk manager, including independence and honest representation of work.
- Identify and handle conflicts of interest through fair dealing and full disclosure.
- Apply the confidentiality principle to realistic workplace scenarios.
- Explain who the Code applies to and the consequences of violating it.

## Explanation

### Why a code for risk managers?
Risk managers are often the people who must say "no" to profitable ideas. Their value depends on being trusted: by boards, regulators, clients and colleagues. The disasters studied earlier in this module show what happens when risk numbers are massaged, warnings are ignored or conflicts are hidden. The Code of Conduct sets out the ethical behaviour expected of GARP members and holders of, and candidates for, the FRM designation.

### Structure of the Code
The Code is organized into **principles** and **professional standards**, each supported by more specific rules of conduct.

**Principles**
1. **Professional integrity and ethical conduct**
2. **Conflicts of interest**
3. **Confidentiality**

**Professional standards**
1. **Fundamental responsibilities**
2. **Adherence to generally accepted practices**

### Professional integrity and ethical conduct
Members are expected to act honestly, professionally and with integrity. In practice that means:
- Using reasonable judgment while keeping **independence of thought** - a risk view should not bend to whoever pays the bonus.
- Taking reasonable care that their services are not used for improper, fraudulent or illegal purposes.
- Never knowingly misrepresenting their analysis, recommendations, qualifications or actions.
- Avoiding dishonest or deceptive conduct, and conduct that harms the reputation of the profession, GARP or the FRM designation (including misconduct around the exam).
- Being aware that norms differ across cultures, while still holding to the Code's standards.

### Conflicts of interest
Members should deal fairly with all parties and **disclose fully and fairly** any actual or potential conflict to those affected. A conflict is not automatically a violation; failing to disclose it, or letting it bias the work, is. Typical examples: a risk manager whose pay rises with desk revenue, a consultant asked to validate a model their own firm built, or personal holdings in a security being assessed.

### Confidentiality
Members must not use confidential information for improper purposes and should keep it confidential unless they have consent or disclosure is legally required. Above all, they must not use confidential information **for personal benefit**, such as trading ahead of a client's large order.

### Fundamental responsibilities
Members should comply with all applicable laws, rules and regulations, understand the legal framework governing their work, and recognize that their ethical responsibilities are **personal**: they cannot be delegated or outsourced to an employer, a compliance department or a vendor.

### Adherence to generally accepted practices
Members should perform work diligently and independently of interested parties, stay current with **generally accepted risk management practices**, and **clearly flag any departure** from them. Communications should be factual, avoid false information and **distinguish fact from opinion**.

### Applicability and enforcement
The Code applies to GARP members and to FRM holders and candidates. A breach can lead to sanctions such as temporary suspension or permanent removal from membership and loss of the right to use the FRM designation.

## Worked example
**Scenario.** Priya is a market risk analyst at Lindqvist Capital. Her manager asks her to switch the VaR model from a 2-year to a 5-year look-back window just before quarter-end, which lowers reported VaR and keeps the trading desk inside its limit. Priya's bonus depends partly on desk profitability. She also overhears that a client will sell a large bond block tomorrow.

**Step 1 - Integrity and generally accepted practices.** Changing a model window is not wrong in itself, but doing it *to hide a limit breach* misrepresents risk. Priya should document the change, show results under both windows, and clearly flag that the switch departs from the bank's established methodology.

**Step 2 - Conflict of interest.** Her bonus link to desk profits is a potential conflict. She should disclose it to her manager and the head of risk, and not let it influence her analysis.

**Step 3 - Confidentiality.** The client's planned sale is confidential. Trading her personal account ahead of it would use confidential information for personal benefit - a clear violation.

**Step 4 - Fundamental responsibilities.** "My manager told me to" is not a defence; ethical responsibility cannot be delegated. If pressure continues, she should escalate through the risk or compliance function.

**Conclusion:** Priya complies with the Code by **documenting and disclosing the model change, disclosing her conflict, keeping the client information confidential and escalating any pressure to mislead**.

## Common exam traps
- Thinking a conflict of interest is itself a violation; the obligation is fair dealing and full disclosure.
- Believing compliance staff or a supervisor can take over a member's ethical responsibility - they cannot.
- Assuming any departure from standard practice is forbidden; it must be clearly disclosed, not avoided at all costs.
- Forgetting that the Code applies to FRM candidates as well as certified holders and members.
- Presenting opinions or forecasts as facts in a risk report.

## Key takeaways
- The Code rests on three principles (integrity, conflicts, confidentiality) and two professional standards (fundamental responsibilities, generally accepted practices).
- Independence of thought and honest representation are the core of a risk manager's integrity.
- Conflicts must be disclosed fully and fairly; confidential information must never be used for personal gain.
- Ethical responsibility is personal and cannot be delegated.
- Violations can cost membership and the right to use the FRM designation.
