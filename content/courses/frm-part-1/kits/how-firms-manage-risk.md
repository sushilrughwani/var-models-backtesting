## Slides
### Slide 1: How Firms Manage Financial Risk
- Four responses to a risk
- When hedging creates value
- The risk appetite statement

### Slide 2: Four Responses
- Retain: core risks the firm is paid for
- Avoid: do not take the exposure
- Mitigate: controls, collateral, limits, diversification
- Transfer: insurance, derivatives, securitisation

### Slide 3: The Case Against Hedging
- In frictionless markets, shareholders can hedge themselves
- Hedging then just costs fees and premiums
- So value must come from frictions

### Slide 4: Frictions That Make Hedging Valuable
- Convex taxes
- Costs of financial distress and higher debt capacity
- Underinvestment when internal cash dries up
- Clearer signal of management performance

### Slide 5: Tax Convexity
- $E[T(X)] \ge T(E[X])$ when $T$ is convex
- Stable earnings pay less tax on average
- Saving = expected unhedged tax minus hedged tax

### Slide 6: Transfer With a Forward
- Locks in the rate, removes upside and downside
- A loss on the forward offsets a gain on the exposure
- Judge the combined position, not the derivative alone

### Slide 7: Costs and Pitfalls
- Spreads, option premiums, staff and systems
- Margin calls create liquidity strain
- Accounting mismatches without hedge accounting
- Hedging programmes drifting into speculation

### Slide 8: Risk Appetite Statement
- Board-approved, linked to strategy and capital
- Quantitative limits plus qualitative statements
- Cascades into business-line limits

### Slide 9: Capacity, Appetite, Limits
- Capacity: most risk the firm can bear
- Appetite: the amount it chooses to take
- Limits: day-to-day controls derived from appetite

### Slide 10: Recap
- Combine retain, avoid, mitigate, transfer
- Hedge because of frictions, not by default
- Anchor everything to the risk appetite statement

## Whiteboard examples
**Example 1: Tax convexity.** Earnings 20m or 80m, 50/50. Tax 20% up to 50m, 40% above.
- Tax at 20m = 4m. Tax at 80m = 10 + 12 = 22m. Expected = 13m.
- Hedged at 50m: tax = 10m. Saving = 3m. After-tax earnings: 37m unhedged vs 40m hedged.

**Example 2: Exporter forward.** Receives EUR 5m in six months; forward 1.10.
- Hedged: 5m x 1.10 = USD 5.5m in every state.
- Unhedged at spot 1.02: 5.1m (hedge gained 0.4m). At spot 1.18: 5.9m (hedge lost 0.4m, but the receivable gained 0.4m).

## Questions to ask students
- If taxes were a flat 30% with full loss offsets, would the tax argument for hedging still work? (No: a linear tax function gives no convexity saving.)
- The airline's fuel hedge lost money when oil fell. Was it a failure? (Not necessarily; fuel became cheaper, offsetting the hedge loss. Judge the net position.)
- Which is larger: risk capacity or risk appetite? (Capacity; appetite is set below it.)
- Why can a perfectly matched futures hedge still cause trouble? (Daily margin calls require cash before the offsetting gain on the exposure is realised.)
- Name a risk a bank should retain rather than hedge. (Core lending credit risk it is paid to bear, within limits.)

## Analogies
- Risk appetite is a household budget: capacity is the total income, appetite is what you decide to spend on holidays, limits are the per-trip caps.
- A forward hedge is a fixed-price menu: you never pay more, but you miss the happy-hour discount.
- Hedging under convex tax is like smoothing your driving speed: same average speed, less fuel burned.

## Recording tips
- Draw the convex tax curve and mark the two outcomes and the chord; the picture makes Jensen's inequality obvious.
- Emphasise that the frictionless argument is the starting point, then add frictions one at a time.
- Show the exporter example as a table with three columns: spot, unhedged USD, hedged USD.
