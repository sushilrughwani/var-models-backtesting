## Slides
### Slide 1: Why Pool Money?
- Diversification and professional management at small scale
- Investors own shares of a common portfolio
- Value per share is the NAV
### Slide 2: Net Asset Value
- NAV = (assets - liabilities) / shares outstanding
- Open-end funds issue and redeem at end-of-day NAV
- Share count changes every day
### Slide 3: Closed-End Funds
- Fixed share count after the initial offering
- Shares trade on an exchange at market prices
- Price can sit at a premium or, more often, a discount to NAV
### Slide 4: ETFs
- Exchange-traded throughout the day, usually tracking an index
- Authorised participants create and redeem in kind
- Arbitrage keeps price close to NAV; low cost and tax-efficient
### Slide 5: Costs
- Front-end and back-end loads
- Annual expense ratio
- Small annual differences compound over long horizons
### Slide 6: Undesirable Practices
- Late trading: orders after the cut-off at today's NAV
- Market timing: exploiting stale prices
- Front running and directed brokerage
### Slide 7: Hedge Fund Fees
- Management fee (e.g. 2% of assets)
- Incentive fee (e.g. 20% of profits after management fee)
- Hurdle, high-water mark and clawback protect investors
### Slide 8: High-Water Mark in Action
- -20% gross year: value falls to 78 after fees
- +40% gross year: value 107.64 before incentive fee
- Incentive only on 7.64 above the 100 peak: ending value 106.112
### Slide 9: Strategies
- Long/short equity, dedicated short, distressed
- Merger and convertible arbitrage, fixed-income arbitrage
- Emerging markets, global macro, managed futures
### Slide 10: Performance Biases
- Survivorship bias: dead funds disappear from databases
- Backfill bias: good histories added after the fact
- Asymmetric fees can encourage risk-taking
### Slide 11: Takeaways
- Know how each vehicle is priced and traded
- Compute fees in the right order
- Treat reported hedge fund returns with scepticism

## Whiteboard examples
**1. NAV.** Assets USD 520m, liabilities USD 20m, 25m shares. NAV = (520 - 20) / 25 = USD 20.00.

**2. 2 plus 20, gross return 30%.**
- After management fee: 30% - 2% = 28%.
- Incentive fee: 20% × 28% = 5.6%; net = 22.4%.
- With a 5% hurdle on the excess: incentive = 20% × 23% = 4.6%; net = 23.4%.

**3. High-water mark over two years.** Start 100. Year 1 gross -20%, year 2 gross +40%.
- Year 1: 100 - 20 - 2 = 78; no incentive fee.
- Year 2: gain 31.2, management fee 1.56, value 107.64.
- With HWM at 100: incentive = 0.2 × 7.64 = 1.528; end value 106.112.
- Without HWM: incentive = 0.2 × 29.64 = 5.928; end value 101.712.

## Questions to ask students
- Why does a closed-end fund's price differ from NAV but an ETF's price barely does? (Only ETFs have a creation/redemption arbitrage mechanism.)
- What is late trading and who is harmed? (Buying or selling at today's NAV after the cut-off; long-term shareholders are diluted.)
- A fund returns -10% gross with 2 plus 20 fees. What is the investor's net return? (-12%: management fee applies, no incentive fee.)
- Why are hedge fund index returns biased upwards? (Survivorship and backfill bias.)
- What does a fund of funds add to costs? (A second layer of management and incentive fees.)

## Analogies
- An open-end fund is a bakery that bakes to order at the day's price; a closed-end fund is a fixed batch of loaves resold in a market where prices drift away from the cost of ingredients.
- The high-water mark is a ratchet: the manager has to climb back to the old peak before getting paid again.
- Survivorship bias is judging a marathon by interviewing only the finishers.

## Recording tips
- Do the fee calculations in a table with columns for gross return, management fee, incentive base and net return so students see the order of operations.
- Stress the difference between hurdle (a minimum return) and high-water mark (a previous peak); students mix them up.
- Mention that fee conventions vary and exam questions state the order; read the stem carefully.
