Full-length mock exams are the closest thing you have to the real CFA® or FRM® exam before the day itself. Used well, they reveal weaknesses that topic-by-topic practice cannot: fading concentration in the third hour, poor pacing, a tendency to second-guess, or a topic that collapses when mixed in with everything else. Used badly, they become stressful scorecards that consume your best questions and tell you little you can act on.

This article covers when to take mocks, how to sit them, how to review them, and how to use them to manage both time and nerves.

## What a mock exam is for

A mock has three jobs:

1. **Diagnosis.** Which topics and question types break down under realistic conditions?
2. **Rehearsal.** Practising the physical and mental routine of a long exam: pacing, breaks, focus and recovery from difficult questions.
3. **Calibration.** Giving you a rough sense of where you stand, so you can adjust the remaining plan.

Notice that "predicting my result" is not on the list. Mock scores depend on the provider's question style and difficulty, on which topics happen to appear, and on how tired you were that day. Treat the score as one input among several, never as a forecast.

## When to take them

### Not too early

Taking a full mock before you have covered most of the material is usually a poor use of a scarce resource. You will see large gaps you already knew about, and you will burn questions you could have used later. Topic-level and mixed question sets do a better job at that stage.

### The typical schedule

For most candidates I suggest two to four full-length mocks, concentrated in the final five to six weeks:

| Timing | Purpose |
|---|---|
| About five to six weeks out | First mock: baseline diagnosis under full conditions |
| About three to four weeks out | Second mock: check whether repairs have worked; refine pacing |
| About two weeks out | Third mock (optional): consolidation and confidence |
| About one week out | Final mock (optional): routine rehearsal, then stop |

Spacing matters more than number. Each mock should be followed by enough time to review it fully and act on what you learned. Taking mocks on consecutive weekends without proper review is a common mistake: it feels productive but mostly repeats the same errors.

Stop taking full-length mocks in the last few days. You need rest and consolidation, not another long test.

## How to sit a mock

Conditions matter. The more realistic the rehearsal, the more useful the information.

- **Same time of day as the real exam.** If your exam starts in the morning, take your mock in the morning. Concentration and energy vary through the day.
- **Same duration and break structure.** Follow the current official session structure and timing. Do not pause for messages, coffee or a quick look at your notes.
- **Same tools.** Use your exam calculator, scratch paper and nothing else. Put your phone in another room.
- **Simulate the environment.** A quiet room, a desk, no music. Some candidates even practise wearing the clothes they will wear on the day so that nothing feels unfamiliar.
- **Commit to every answer.** Do not leave blanks. If you would guess on exam day, guess in the mock, and mark it as a guess on your scratch paper.

During the mock, note the time at regular checkpoints, for example after every quarter of the questions. This gives you pacing data to review afterwards.

## How to review a mock

The review is where the value is. Plan to spend at least as long reviewing as you spent sitting the exam, and spread the review over two or three sessions if needed. Ideally, start the review the same day or the next day while your reasoning is still fresh.

### Step 1: Sort every question

Put each question into one of four groups:

- **Correct and confident.** Skim the explanation briefly.
- **Correct but unsure or guessed.** Treat as wrong. You do not reliably know this.
- **Wrong, knew the content.** Usually a misread, an execution slip or a judgement error between two plausible options.
- **Wrong, did not know the content.** A knowledge gap.

### Step 2: Find the pattern

Tally the results by topic and by error type. You are looking for patterns, not individual mistakes. Common patterns include:

- One topic consistently weak across mocks.
- Errors concentrated in the last part of a session, suggesting fatigue or time pressure.
- Many misreads, suggesting you are reading stems too quickly.
- Many changed answers that went from right to wrong.

### Step 3: Update your error log

Write a specific entry for every wrong or guessed question: what you did, why it was wrong, and a rule for next time. The rule is what will change your behaviour on the next mock.

### Step 4: Adjust the plan

Translate what you found into the next week or two of study. If a topic is weak, schedule targeted review and fresh questions on it. If pacing broke down, add timed mixed sets. If fatigue showed up late in sessions, work on longer focused practice blocks and look honestly at your sleep.

## Managing time

Pacing problems are among the most fixable issues a mock reveals.

### Know your checkpoints

Divide the session into quarters and work out roughly where you should be at each point. Write these down before you start. Check against them a few times during the session rather than constantly watching the clock.

### Use a two-pass approach

On the first pass, answer every question you can solve efficiently. When a question is clearly going to take much longer than your average budget, make a provisional guess, flag it and move on. On the second pass, return to flagged questions with whatever time remains.

This approach makes sure that easy questions near the end of a session are not lost because you spent too long on a hard one in the middle.

### Review what took too long

In your mock review, look at questions where you significantly exceeded your time budget, even if you got them right. These are often calculation-heavy questions where calculator fluency would help, or long vignettes where you read every detail before looking at what was asked. Reading the question first and then scanning the information for what you need can save real time.

## Managing nerves

Some anxiety is normal and can even sharpen focus. The goal is not to eliminate nerves but to stop them from controlling your behaviour.

### Rehearsal reduces uncertainty

Much exam anxiety comes from the unknown. Every realistic mock reduces that unknown. By exam day, you should have already experienced a long session, a run of difficult questions, a moment of doubt and a recovery. None of it will be new.

### Have a reset routine

Practise a short routine for moments of panic during mocks: put the pen down, take two slow breaths, read the question again from the start. If you still cannot see a way in, guess, flag and move on. Practising the routine in mocks makes it available on the day.

### Expect a hard patch

In almost every long exam, there will be a run of questions that feel impossible. That says nothing about how you are doing overall; difficult questions are hard for everyone. Mocks are a good place to learn that a hard patch passes.

### Keep perspective on scores

A disappointing mock score in week five is information, not a verdict. It tells you where to focus. Candidates who react to a weak mock by panicking and changing their entire approach usually do worse than those who calmly review and adjust.

## Summary

Schedule a few full-length mocks in the final weeks, spaced far enough apart to review each one properly. Sit them under realistic conditions. Review every question, find the patterns, update your error log and adjust your plan. Use checkpoints and a two-pass approach to manage time. Practise a reset routine for nerves, and treat every score as information. The aim is that by the real exam, nothing about the day feels unfamiliar.
