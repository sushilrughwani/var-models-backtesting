Almost every candidate preparing for the CFA® or FRM® exams has access to a question bank. Far fewer use it well. The common pattern is to grind through questions, glance at the score, feel good or bad about the number, and move on. The questions get used up, but the learning they could have produced is mostly wasted.

A question bank is not primarily a measuring device. It is a diagnostic and training tool, and most of its value comes after you click "submit". This article sets out the system I teach: when to use questions, how to keep an error log that actually changes your behaviour, how to run review cycles, and how to build timing discipline.

## Three jobs a question bank can do

Before you start, recognise that questions serve different purposes at different stages:

1. **Learning.** Straight after a reading, untimed questions help you discover what you did not understand. Your accuracy here does not matter much.
2. **Consolidation.** Weeks later, mixed questions test whether the material has stayed with you and whether you can recognise which concept a question is testing.
3. **Performance.** In the final phase, timed sets rehearse the conditions of the real exam.

Mixing up these jobs causes problems. Doing timed sets on material you have only just met produces discouraging scores and little learning. Doing only untimed topic sets right up to the exam leaves you unprepared for the pace and the mix.

## Do not burn the bank too early

Question banks are finite. If you work through every question on a topic immediately after studying it, you have nothing fresh for consolidation or the final weeks. Questions you have seen before are much less useful, because you partly remember the answer rather than solving the problem.

A simple rule: for each topic, use roughly a third of the available questions during first learning, save a third for mixed review sets in the middle phase, and keep the final third untouched for the last weeks. The exact split matters less than the principle of keeping some unseen questions in reserve.

## The error log: the most important document in your preparation

An error log is a record of every question you got wrong, guessed correctly, or got right slowly. It is the single most useful tool for converting practice into improvement, and most candidates either do not keep one or keep one that is too vague to help.

A useful entry has five parts:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Topic and concept | Fixed income: modified duration |
| What I did | Used Macaulay duration directly to estimate price change |
| Why it was wrong | Forgot to divide by one plus the periodic yield |
| Error type | Formula/knowledge gap |
| Rule for next time | "Price change estimates use modified duration, not Macaulay" |

The last column is the one that matters. Writing "careless mistake" teaches you nothing. Writing a specific rule you can apply next time turns a mistake into a habit.

### Classify your errors

Over time, tag each entry with an error type. I use four:

- **Knowledge gap** - you did not know the concept or formula.
- **Misread** - you answered a different question from the one asked, missed a "NOT" or "least likely", or used the wrong number from the stem.
- **Execution** - you knew the method but made an arithmetic or calculator slip.
- **Judgement** - you narrowed it to two answers and chose the wrong one, often because you did not understand a subtle distinction.

The mix tells you what to fix. If most of your errors are knowledge gaps, you need more study of the content. If most are misreads, you need to slow down your reading of the question stem and underline the actual ask. If execution errors dominate, you need calculator practice. If judgement errors dominate, you need to review the reasoning behind correct answers, not just the answers themselves.

### Log lucky guesses too

A question you guessed correctly is a question you do not know. Mark any answer you were not confident in, and review it exactly as if it were wrong. Otherwise your score overstates your preparation and the weakness stays hidden until exam day.

## How to review a question set

After you finish a set, the review should take at least as long as the set itself. Here is the routine:

1. **Before checking answers**, mark your confidence on each question: sure, unsure or guessed.
2. **For every wrong or unsure answer**, read the explanation and then close it. Reconstruct the correct reasoning in your own words without looking.
3. **For correct, confident answers**, skim the explanation quickly. Occasionally you will find you were right for the wrong reason.
4. **Read the wrong options.** Ask why each distractor is wrong. Distractors are often built from common mistakes, and understanding them protects you against the same trap in a different question.
5. **Write error log entries** with a specific rule for each mistake.

## Review cycles: revisiting the error log

An error log you never reread is just a list of bad memories. Build review cycles into your week:

- **Weekly:** reread the entries from the past week. For each one, cover the "rule for next time" column and see whether you can state the rule yourself.
- **Every few weeks:** redo the questions you got wrong, without looking at your log first. If you get one right for the right reason, mark it as resolved. If not, it stays active.
- **Final phase:** your error log becomes your primary revision document. It is a personalised summary of exactly where you are vulnerable.

Over time, you should see the same types of mistake appearing less often. If a particular error type keeps recurring, that is a signal to change how you study that topic, not just to do more questions.

## Building timing discipline

Accuracy without speed will not pass a timed exam. But timing should be introduced gradually.

### Know your per-question budget

Work out the average time available per question from the current official exam format: total time divided by number of questions. Write it down. That figure is an average, not a rule for each question; some will take a fraction of it and some will take more.

### Practise in stages

- **Early:** untimed. Focus on understanding.
- **Middle:** set a loose time limit for mixed sets, roughly matching your per-question budget, and note which questions blew through it.
- **Final phase:** strict timing, exam-length blocks, no pausing.

### Learn to move on

Every timed set should include practice at abandoning a question. If you have spent well over your budget and are not converging on an answer, make your best guess, flag it and move on. Candidates who refuse to let go of one hard question often run out of time for three easy ones at the end.

Track this explicitly. In your error log, note any question where you went significantly over time, even if you got it right. Slow correct answers are a timing problem hiding behind a good score.

## Reading your scores sensibly

Question bank scores fluctuate with topic mix, question difficulty and your energy that day. Do not overreact to a single set. Look at trends across several sets, and look at accuracy by topic rather than overall. A steady overall score can hide one topic quietly getting worse.

Also remember that providers write questions with different styles and difficulty levels. Treat question bank results as information about your relative strengths and weaknesses, not as a prediction of your exam result.

## Summary

Use questions for learning, consolidation and performance, in that order. Keep unseen questions in reserve. Keep an error log with specific rules, classify your errors, and log your lucky guesses. Spend as long reviewing as answering. Revisit the log regularly, redo old mistakes, and build timing discipline in stages. Done this way, a question bank stops being a scoreboard and becomes the engine of your preparation.
