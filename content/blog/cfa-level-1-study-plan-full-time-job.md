Most study plans for the CFA® Level I exam are written as if the candidate has nothing else going on. They assume you can sit down for three hours every evening, never travel for work, never get sick and never have a quarter-end close. Then real life arrives in week three, the plan collapses, and the candidate concludes that they are "behind" and starts to panic.

A plan that survives a full-time job is built differently. It assumes disruption, it protects a small amount of non-negotiable time, and it has slack built in from day one. This article walks through how I help working candidates build exactly that kind of plan.

## Start from the exam date and work backwards

Write your exam date at the bottom of a page. Above it, block out the final stretch for review and mock exams. I treat the last five to six weeks as a separate phase: no new material, only revision, question practice and full-length mocks. Above that, block out one "buffer week" for every six to eight weeks of planned study. Only the time that remains is available for first-pass learning of the curriculum.

This exercise is sobering, and that is the point. Many candidates discover that the window for actually learning the material is much shorter than the calendar suggests. Better to discover it in month one than in the final month.

## Measure your real weekly capacity, not your ideal one

Before you assign topics to weeks, run an honest two-week audit. For fourteen days, record every block of time you actually spend studying, with start and end times. Do not change your behaviour to look good; you are collecting data.

At the end, you will usually find three kinds of time:

- **Anchor sessions** - reliable blocks of 60 to 120 minutes, typically early mornings or weekend mornings, when you can do focused work on new material.
- **Fragment time** - commutes, lunch breaks, waiting rooms. Ten to twenty-five minutes, interrupted, low energy.
- **Recovery evenings** - after-work slots where you are tired. Useful for light review, but not for learning a difficult new concept like duration or the mechanics of hypothesis testing.

Your plan should assign each kind of work to the right kind of time. New reading goes into anchor sessions. Flashcards and short question sets go into fragments. Recovery evenings get error-log review or nothing at all. Trying to learn derivatives pricing at 10 p.m. after a full workday is how candidates convince themselves they are "bad at" a topic they would handle easily when rested.

## Allocate by weight and by difficulty for you

Look up the current topic weight ranges published by CFA Institute and list the topics. Then add a second column: your honest starting familiarity, from 1 (never seen it) to 3 (I use this at work). A candidate from an accounting background might rate financial statement analysis highly and quantitative methods low; an engineer might be the reverse.

Allocate anchor sessions in proportion to weight multiplied by unfamiliarity. A heavily weighted topic you have never studied gets the most time. A lightly weighted topic you already know gets a quick pass and practice questions. This is not about skipping material - every topic gets covered - but about putting deep study where it changes your score most.

## Build the weekly template

Here is the template I suggest to most working candidates. Adjust the specifics, but keep the structure:

| Slot | Activity |
|---|---|
| Weekday mornings (3-4 of them) | One anchor session: new reading plus end-of-reading questions |
| Weekday fragments | Flashcards and 10-question mini-sets from earlier topics |
| One weekday evening | Error-log review, 30 minutes |
| Saturday morning | Long anchor: finish the week's reading, then a 30-45 question mixed set |
| Sunday | Short weekly review and planning, then genuinely off |

Two features matter here. First, there is a weekly review that looks back across everything studied so far, not just this week's topic. Second, there is protected time off. Candidates who study seven days a week for months tend to arrive at the final phase exhausted, and that is precisely when the most valuable work happens.

## Make the minimum session non-negotiable

Work will sometimes take over a whole week. The plan survives if you define a **minimum viable day**: something small enough that you can do it even on your worst day. For many people it is 15 minutes of flashcards plus five practice questions. On a bad day, you do the minimum and stop without guilt.

The minimum day keeps the habit alive and keeps old material from decaying. Missing a full week entirely is far more damaging than having a week of minimum days, because restarting is the hardest part.

## Use buffer weeks properly

When a work crisis wipes out your anchor sessions, you do not try to "double up" the following week. Doubling up rarely works for people with full-time jobs; it simply creates a second missed week. Instead, you slide the remaining schedule forward by one week and consume a buffer.

If you reach a buffer week and you are on schedule, use it for a mixed review of everything covered so far, or bring the next topic forward. Either way, you have not lost anything. Buffers are what make a plan realistic rather than aspirational.

## Talk to your manager early

This is uncomfortable for many candidates, but it helps. Tell your manager the exam date months in advance and ask whether you can avoid travel or take leave in the final weeks. Many employers are supportive of professional qualifications, and even when they cannot change workloads, knowing the date lets you plan around predictable busy periods. If your company has a quarter-end or audit season close to the exam, your buffer weeks should be placed right before it.

## Track progress with questions, not pages

Pages read is a misleading metric. You can read an entire reading and retain very little. Track two things instead:

1. **Readings completed with end-of-reading questions attempted.** A reading is not "done" until you have tested yourself on it.
2. **Rolling accuracy on mixed question sets.** Every Saturday set includes questions from all prior topics. If your accuracy on older topics drifts down, schedule a review block before moving further.

This gives you an early-warning system. A topic that slips in week eight can be fixed in week nine. A topic you discover you have forgotten in the final week is much harder to rescue.

## Protect sleep and physical health

Sleep is when memory consolidates. Cutting sleep to fit in more study hours is one of the most common mistakes I see among working candidates, and it is self-defeating: you study more hours and retain less from each. If the only way to hit your plan is to sleep five hours a night, the plan is wrong, not your willpower.

Short walks, regular meals and some exercise are not luxuries during exam preparation. They are part of the plan.

## A sample month for a working candidate

To make this concrete, imagine a candidate with roughly four anchor sessions and a long Saturday each week. Month one might look like this:

- **Week 1:** Quantitative methods, first half. Saturday set on those readings only.
- **Week 2:** Quantitative methods, second half. Saturday set covering all of quant.
- **Week 3:** Economics, first part. Saturday set mixes economics and quant.
- **Week 4:** Buffer week. If on schedule: full review of quant with a timed set, plus a head start on the next topic.

Notice that the Saturday sets become progressively mixed. By month three, every weekend you are touching material from the first week. That is deliberate: it is spaced review built into the schedule.

## The real goal

A good plan is not the most ambitious one; it is the one you are still following in the final month. Build for the life you actually have, measure honestly, keep the minimum day sacred, and use buffers instead of guilt. Done this way, preparing for Level I alongside a full-time job is demanding but entirely manageable.
