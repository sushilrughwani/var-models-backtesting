The last month before the FRM® Part I exam has a different character from everything that came before it. You are no longer learning the curriculum; you are consolidating it, filling gaps and rehearsing performance. Candidates who treat the final 30 days as "more of the same, but faster" tend to arrive tired and scattered. Candidates who treat it as a distinct phase with its own goals tend to arrive calm and sharp.

This article gives a practical framework for that final month. It assumes you have completed a first pass of all the material. If you have not, the advice still applies, but you will need to make hard choices about coverage first - more on that below.

## Before day 30: take stock

Start the final month with an honest inventory. Spend one session building a simple table with each of the major Part I areas - foundations of risk management, quantitative analysis, financial markets and products, and valuation and risk models - and, for each, note:

- Your recent accuracy on mixed practice questions.
- Your confidence level (high, medium, low).
- Any specific subtopics in your error log that keep recurring.

Check the current topic weightings published by GARP, and use them to rank your weak areas by how much they matter. A low-confidence area with a large weight is your top priority. A low-confidence area with a small weight still needs attention, but it should not consume your best hours.

### If you have not finished the material

Be realistic. In the final month, it is generally better to have a solid working knowledge of most topics than a deep knowledge of some and nothing on others. For any reading you have not covered, aim for the core ideas and the most commonly tested calculations, then practise questions on it. Do not try to master every detail at this stage.

## Days 30 to 21: targeted repair

The first ten days are for fixing your largest weaknesses while keeping everything else warm.

- **Mornings or anchor sessions:** work through your top-priority weak topics. Revisit the concept briefly, then move quickly to questions. Prefer questions you have not seen before.
- **Daily maintenance:** 20 to 30 minutes of mixed questions or flashcards covering all four areas, so stronger topics do not decay while you focus on weaker ones.
- **Formula fluency:** begin a daily formula drill. Write the key formulas from memory on a blank page - value-at-risk under normality, portfolio variance for two assets, the Sharpe and Treynor ratios, bond duration and convexity relationships, option bounds and put-call parity - then check them.

Take your first full-length mock exam around the end of this period. Treat it as a diagnostic, not a verdict. Its job is to show you what breaks under realistic time pressure.

## Days 20 to 11: integration and timing

In the middle stretch, shift the balance from topic-by-topic repair towards mixed practice and timing.

- **Mixed timed sets:** at least every other day, do a block of questions drawn from all areas, timed at the per-question pace of the real exam.
- **Mock review:** the review of your first mock should be thorough. Every wrong or guessed answer goes into the error log with a specific rule. This review often takes longer than the mock itself, and that is fine.
- **Second mock:** take another full-length mock towards the end of this period, at the same time of day as your real exam if possible.

Pay attention to quantitative analysis and valuation topics in particular. These areas tend to involve calculations where small execution errors cost marks, and they reward regular, short practice rather than occasional long sessions. Keep your calculator in your hand during this period; fluency with the time value of money, statistics and bond functions saves real minutes.

### Do not neglect the qualitative material

Candidates with quantitative backgrounds sometimes under-prepare for the more descriptive content: risk governance, case studies of risk management failures, and the professional conduct material. These questions can be straightforward if you know the content and frustrating if you do not. Short, regular flashcard reviews work well here.

## Days 10 to 4: consolidation

By now your main gaps should be smaller. The goal is to make what you know reliable.

- **Error log as primary text:** reread your entire error log. Redo questions you got wrong earlier. Each one you now answer correctly, for the right reason, is a closed gap.
- **One last full mock,** if your schedule allows, ideally about a week out. After that, stop doing full-length tests. You need the remaining days to absorb, not to exhaust yourself.
- **Formula sheet:** produce your own one-page summary from memory, then correct it. The act of producing it is the revision.
- **Logistics:** confirm your test centre, travel route and timing, identification requirements and the approved calculator list. Check your calculator batteries and settings. Bring a spare if allowed.

## Days 3 to 1: taper

Athletes taper before a race. Exam candidates should too. The last three days are not the time for new material or marathon sessions.

- **Day 3:** a moderate session of mixed questions and error-log review. Stop at a reasonable hour.
- **Day 2:** light review of your formula sheet and flashcards. Short sessions only.
- **Day 1:** a brief look at your one-page summary, if it calms you. Prepare everything you need for the morning. Go for a walk. Eat normally. Sleep.

The urge to cram on the final evening is strong. Resist it. Information you try to absorb in the last twelve hours is unlikely to stick, while a poor night's sleep will certainly hurt your concentration across a long exam.

## Exam day

A few practical points:

- **Arrive early.** Give yourself buffer for travel delays and check-in procedures.
- **Have a pacing plan.** Know roughly where you should be at the halfway point. Check against it once or twice; do not watch the clock constantly.
- **Flag and move on.** If a question is taking well beyond your per-question budget, make your best guess, flag it and come back if time permits. Every question carries the same weight, so a difficult one is not worth more than an easy one.
- **Read carefully.** Watch for "NOT", "least likely" and units. Many errors on exam day are misreads rather than knowledge gaps.
- **Do not change answers without a reason.** Revisit flagged questions, but only change an answer if you can identify a specific mistake in your original reasoning.
- **Reset after a hard question.** One difficult question says nothing about the next one. Take a breath and approach each question fresh.

## Managing nerves in the final month

Anxiety tends to rise as the exam approaches, and it often shows up as an urge to study everything at once. Structure is the best antidote. Having a written plan for each of the remaining days means you do not have to decide what to do every morning, and you can see that the important work is being covered.

It also helps to separate effort from outcome. You control how carefully you review, how well you sleep and how calmly you pace yourself. You do not control which specific questions appear. Focus on the inputs.

## A one-page summary of the month

| Period | Main focus | Mocks |
|---|---|---|
| Days 30-21 | Repair top weaknesses, daily maintenance, formula drills | First mock near day 21 |
| Days 20-11 | Mixed timed practice, deep mock review | Second mock near day 11 |
| Days 10-4 | Error log, formula sheet, logistics | Optional final mock about a week out |
| Days 3-1 | Taper, sleep, light review | None |

The final month is less about heroics and more about discipline. Repair what matters most, rehearse under realistic conditions, review honestly and taper deliberately. Walk in rested, with a plan, and let your preparation do its work.
