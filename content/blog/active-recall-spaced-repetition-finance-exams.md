Ask a group of candidates how they review and most will describe some version of rereading: going back through notes, highlighting, perhaps rewriting summaries. It feels responsible. It feels like work. And for professional finance exams such as the CFA® and FRM® exams, it is one of the least efficient ways to spend your limited study time.

This article explains why rereading misleads you, what active recall and spaced repetition actually are, and how to build both into a realistic weekly routine.

## The fluency trap

When you reread a page on, say, the relationship between bond prices and yields, the material looks familiar. Familiarity produces a feeling of understanding: "Yes, I know this." The problem is that recognising information when it is in front of you is a very different skill from producing it when it is not.

The exam does not show you your notes and ask whether they look familiar. It gives you a scenario and asks you to generate the right concept, select the right formula and apply it under time pressure. Rereading trains recognition. The exam tests retrieval. That mismatch is the core reason why candidates who "covered everything" are still surprised on exam day.

There is a second problem. Rereading gives you no feedback. You cannot tell whether you would have remembered something because the answer is already on the page. You finish a session feeling confident, with no evidence either way.

## What active recall means in practice

Active recall simply means forcing yourself to retrieve information from memory before you look at it. The effort of retrieval is not a side effect; it is the mechanism. Each successful retrieval makes the memory easier to access next time, and each failed retrieval tells you exactly where your gaps are.

Practical forms of active recall for finance exams include:

- **Blank-page recall.** After finishing a reading, close everything and write down the main ideas, formulas and relationships you remember. Then open your notes and mark what you missed in a different colour.
- **Question-first study.** Attempt practice questions on a reading before you feel ready. Wrong answers are cheap at this stage and show you what to focus on.
- **Flashcards with a real prompt.** A good card asks you to produce something: "What happens to the price of a callable bond relative to an otherwise identical option-free bond when rates fall sharply, and why?" A poor card just names a term.
- **Explain it aloud.** Pretend you are teaching a colleague the difference between systematic and idiosyncratic risk. Where you hesitate or hand-wave is where your understanding is thin.
- **Derive, do not memorise.** For formulas with structure, such as the present value of an annuity, practise rebuilding them from first principles. If you can derive it, you will not forget it in the exam room.

Retrieval should feel effortful. If every flashcard is easy, you are reviewing too soon or your cards are too shallow.

## What spaced repetition adds

The second ingredient is timing. Memories fade over time, and reviewing just as a memory is starting to fade strengthens it more than reviewing while it is still fresh. Spaced repetition means reviewing material at increasing intervals: soon after first learning it, then a few days later, then a week or two later, then longer.

The contrast is cramming, or massed practice: doing all your review of a topic in one long block. Cramming can work for a test tomorrow, but a professional exam covers months of material, and the topics you studied in month one are still on the exam.

You do not need complicated software to space your reviews, although flashcard apps that schedule cards automatically are convenient. A simple manual system works:

1. Review new material within a day or two of first studying it.
2. Review again at the end of the week, in your weekly mixed question set.
3. Review again roughly two to three weeks later.
4. From then on, keep the topic alive with occasional mixed questions until the final review phase.

## Interleaving: the overlooked partner

A related idea is interleaving: mixing different topics within a single practice session rather than doing all the questions on one topic in a block. Blocked practice feels smoother because you know which tool to use before you read the question. Mixed practice is harder, because you first have to recognise what kind of problem you are looking at.

That recognition step is exactly what the exam demands. On exam day, a question about forward rates might follow a question about ethics and precede one about inventory accounting. If you have only ever practised in topic blocks, you are training a skill the exam never asks for.

## A weekly routine that combines all three

Here is a simple structure I recommend:

| When | What | Type |
|---|---|---|
| During each new reading | Pause every few pages, close the text, summarise from memory | Active recall |
| End of each reading | Blank-page recall, then end-of-reading questions | Active recall |
| Daily, 15-20 minutes | Flashcards due today (old and new topics) | Spaced repetition |
| End of week | 30-45 mixed questions across all topics studied so far | Interleaving and spacing |
| Every few weeks | Timed mixed set under exam-like conditions | All three |

Notice that very little time is spent passively reading old material. When you do return to notes, it should be to look up something you got wrong, not to wander through pages hoping it sticks.

## Making good flashcards for quantitative content

Finance exams mix conceptual and numerical content, and flashcards work for both if you design them carefully.

- **Concepts:** Ask for direction and reason. "If the correlation between two assets falls, what happens to portfolio standard deviation, holding weights and individual volatilities constant? Why?"
- **Formulas:** Ask for the formula, but also for when to use it and what each term means. A formula you can write but not apply is not worth much.
- **Mini calculations:** Small numbers you can do in your head or with a quick calculator check. Keep them short; long calculations belong in question practice, not flashcards.
- **Traps:** Make cards from your own mistakes. "I keep confusing the effective annual rate with the stated annual rate. Which one compounds?"

Keep cards atomic: one idea per card. A card that asks for five things will always feel half-right, and you will grade yourself generously.

## Why this feels worse but works better

The honest warning is that active recall and spacing feel harder than rereading. You will get things wrong more often. Sessions will feel less smooth. It is easy to conclude that the method is not working because it does not produce that comfortable sense of mastery.

That discomfort is a feature. Learning researchers sometimes call it "desirable difficulty": effort during practice that makes the learning more durable. The comfortable feeling from rereading is the one to distrust, because it is not linked to how much you will remember in eight weeks.

## Common objections

**"I need to read the material at least once."** Of course. Active recall replaces rereading, not first reading. Read carefully the first time, then switch to retrieval.

**"I don't have time to make flashcards."** Make fewer, better ones, and make them from your mistakes. Twenty cards that target your real gaps beat two hundred cards copied from a summary.

**"Spacing means I'll forget things in between."** Some forgetting is the point. Retrieving a partly forgotten memory strengthens it more than reviewing one you can recall effortlessly.

## The takeaway

Rereading feels like studying because it is comfortable. Retrieval feels like struggling because it is effortful. For exams that test whether you can apply months of material under time pressure, the struggle is what you want. Close the book more often, test yourself before you feel ready, space your reviews, and mix your practice. Your notes will look less highlighted, and your scores will tell the real story.
